Padel warm up: a routine that actually prepares you

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

A good padel warm up takes about ten minutes before you step on court, then continues with a few minutes of easy hitting: raise your heart rate, move the joints you'll use, rehearse padel movements, and only then start hitting harder. Before an official match you get just a 3-minute courtesy rally (FIP Rule 2), so the real preparation has to happen before that. Here is a routine, the drills and the stretches worth doing.

Why a padel warm up matters

Padel looks gentle from the outside, but it's full of sudden starts, stops, lunges, twists and overheads. A cold body doesn't like any of those. Padel New Zealand notes that a poor warm-up is associated with muscle strains, ankle sprains and shoulder problems, and the physio advice from The Padel School is to build up gradually towards the changes of direction, overheads and faster movements the game demands.

There's also a performance reason. The first few games are where cold players drop serve and miss easy volleys. A proper warm-up means you start the match at the level you finish it.

What the rules allow: the 3-minute courtesy rally

Under FIP Rule 2, players get an obligatory 3-minute courtesy rally before the match; the match itself starts with the first serve. The word courtesy matters: it's a chance for both pairs to get a feel for the ball and court, not a practice session of smashes at the opponents. Three minutes is nowhere near enough to warm up properly, so do your preparation before you walk on. For all the match timings, see padel match times and warm-up rules.

The structure: a simple warm up routine

Sports scientists often use the RAMP model, developed by Ian Jeffreys in 2007: Raise (heart rate and body temperature), Activate and Mobilise (key muscles and the joints and ranges of motion you'll use), and Potentiate (short, sharp, sport-specific efforts). Padel New Zealand's suggested padel warm-up follows the same shape. Here's a version that takes around 10 minutes off court and a few on it.

PhaseTimeWhat to do
Raise3 minEasy jog or brisk walk, side shuffles, skipping, gentle changes of direction
Mobilise3–4 minArm circles, shoulder rotations, trunk twists, walking lunges with rotation, high knees, heel flicks
Activate2 minSquats, a few calf raises, band or bodyweight shoulder rotations, wrist circles
Potentiate2–3 minSplit steps, short accelerations and stops, lateral shuffles, shadow swings
On court5+ minProgressive hitting: mini rallies, volleys, lobs, overheads, serves

Padel New Zealand suggests 15 to 20 minutes in total. If you have the time, use it. If you only have ten, the table above still covers the essentials.

Padel warm up exercises, one by one

Raise

  • Jog and shuffle: one length of the court jogging, one length side-shuffling facing the net, one facing away.
  • Skips: easy skipping with relaxed arm swings.

Mobilise

  • Arm circles: ten forwards, ten backwards, starting small and getting bigger.
  • Walking lunge with rotation: lunge forward and turn your chest over the front knee. Five each side.
  • Trunk twists: feet planted, rotate gently left and right.
  • Leg swings: front to back and side to side, holding the fence or glass for balance.

Activate

  • Bodyweight squats: ten, controlled.
  • Calf raises: ten, to wake up the ankles and Achilles.
  • Shoulder rotations: elbow at your side, rotate the forearm out and in. With a light band if you have one.
  • Wrist and forearm circles, plus gently opening and closing the hand.

Potentiate

  • Split steps: a small hop as an imaginary opponent hits, then a quick first step left, right or back.
  • Accelerate and stop: three or four short sprints of a few metres with a controlled stop.
  • Shadow swings: forehands, backhands, volleys, a few bandejas and serves without a ball. Benenden Hospital's physiotherapy advice suggests at least a minute of shadow strokes before playing.

Padel warm up stretches: static or dynamic?

This is where many players get it backwards. Holding long static stretches before you play isn't the best preparation for a fast sport. A 2016 review by Behm and colleagues found that static stretching tested straight afterwards caused a small-to-moderate drop in performance on average, with the bigger losses from holds of 60 seconds or more per muscle group. Dynamic stretching gave a small improvement.

The same review also found that when stretching was followed by further dynamic activity, there was no clear effect on performance, and it recommends stretching within a warm-up that ends with dynamic work. So:

Before you play

Mostly dynamic movement. If you like to stretch a tight area, keep holds short, under 60 seconds, and follow them with the dynamic phase.

Save for later

Long static holds. Padel New Zealand puts them in the cool-down: 20 to 30 seconds per stretch, without bouncing or pain.

One more piece of evidence worth knowing: a 2014 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine (Lauersen and colleagues) found no injury-prevention effect from stretching programmes, while strength training and balance training did reduce injuries. Stretch because it feels good and helps your range of motion, but get your injury protection from the strength work in our padel exercises guide.

Padel warm up drills on court

Once you're on court, build the ball speed gradually. A useful order:

  1. Mini padel at the net. All four players at the service line or closer, soft control rallies. Two minutes.
  2. Groundstrokes from the back. Cross-court rallies, including a few balls off the back glass.
  3. Volley and groundstroke. One player at the net, one at the back, then swap.
  4. Lobs and overheads. The back player lobs, the net player plays bandejas. Easy pace first.
  5. Serves and returns. A few from each side.

Leave smashes until the end, and hit them at around three-quarters pace at first. The shoulder is the last joint you want to load cold.

Padel warm up games

If you're playing socially or coaching a group, a short game makes the warm-up go faster. Original ideas that work well:

  • First to ten, net only. Rallies inside the service boxes, no smashes, first pair to ten points.
  • Keep it alive. Both pairs cooperate to reach twenty shots in a row, all four players touching the ball.
  • Glass only. A rally where every ball has to come off the back glass before you play it. Great for timing.

More ideas are in our drills for four players.

The cool-down

After the match, take five minutes to walk around, then do your static stretches: calves, hamstrings, quads, hip flexors, glutes, lower back, chest, shoulders and forearms, 20 to 30 seconds each, as Padel New Zealand suggests.

Not medical advice

This routine is for healthy players. If you're returning from injury or have pain that persists, talk to a physiotherapist or doctor.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a padel warm up be?

Around 10 minutes off court plus a few minutes of progressive hitting. Padel New Zealand suggests 15 to 20 minutes in total if you have time.

How long is the warm-up in a padel match?

Three minutes, as an obligatory courtesy rally before the match, under FIP Rule 2. The match itself starts with the first serve, so do your proper warm-up beforehand.

Should I stretch before padel?

Mostly dynamic movements. Long static holds, especially 60 seconds or more per muscle, can slightly reduce performance straight afterwards. Short holds followed by dynamic work are fine.

What is the best warm up for padel?

One that raises your heart rate, mobilises the shoulders, hips and ankles, rehearses padel movements like split steps and lunges, and then builds up the hitting gradually.

Does warming up prevent padel injuries?

Physio and federation advice links poor warm-ups to strains, sprains and shoulder problems. No warm-up guarantees you won't get injured, but it's a sensible habit alongside strength training and gradual increases in playing time.
