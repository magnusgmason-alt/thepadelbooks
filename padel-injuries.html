Padel injuries: what the research says and how to avoid them

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 30 Oct 2026

Padel injuries are common but mostly the overuse kind: the research shows the elbow is the most commonly reported injured joint, alongside a mix of shoulder, lower back, knee, calf and ankle problems, with tendon and muscle injuries far ahead of anything else. The good news is that the main causes are things you can control: how fast you increase your playing, how strong you are and how you warm up. Here is what the evidence says, and what to do about it.

Not medical advice

This article summarises the research on padel injuries in plain language. If you're in pain, especially pain that lasts, is severe or followed a fall or a pop, see a doctor or physiotherapist.

How common are padel injuries?

The best overview is a 2023 systematic review in BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine by Dahmen and colleagues, which pooled eight studies with 2,022 players. It found:

  • About 3 injuries per 1,000 hours of training and 8 per 1,000 matches, figures the authors note rest on limited data.
  • Between 40% and 72% of players reported an injury in the previous 8 to 12 months, and 60% to 95% over their playing career.
  • Injuries were mainly to tendons and muscles. In three of the four studies that reported injury type, tendon injuries were the most common, at 40% to 48%.

The authors are clear about the limits: most studies were from Spain, relied on players' own memory, and used different definitions of injury. So treat the numbers as a rough picture, not precise rates. The review also found no studies testing padel-specific injury prevention programmes.

The most common padel injuries

These are the ranges the review found across studies, by body area:

AreaShare of injuries (range across studies)Typical problem
ElbowAbout 10% to 37%; the joint the review found most often reportedLateral epicondylitis ("tennis elbow")
ShoulderAbout 7% to 34%Rotator cuff tendinitis
Lower backAbout 9% to 28%Lumbar pain
KneeAbout 10% to 23%Patellar tendinitis
Calf and legAbout 8% to 22%Calf (gastrocnemius) tears, "tennis leg"

The wide ranges reflect how differently the studies were run. The consistent message: the elbow is the single joint reported most often, but the legs as a whole (knee, calf, ankle and foot) also account for a large share, and in two studies the lower limbs overall came out ahead of the upper limbs.

Padel elbow

Lateral epicondylitis, the same condition as tennis elbow, was the main elbow diagnosis in the review, making up 20.5% of all injuries in one study and an even larger share in a study that counted it per player rather than per injury. The authors suggest repeated loading from overheads may explain the link between padel and elbow tendon problems. Our full guide to padel elbow covers symptoms, causes and what helps.

Padel shoulder pain

The shoulder does a lot of work in padel: bandejas, víboras, smashes and serves, often many in a row when you're being lobbed. Rotator cuff tendinitis appeared among the diagnoses in the review. Shoulder strength, especially the rotator cuff and the muscles around the shoulder blade, is the main thing you can train. See padel exercises.

NHS guidance for mild shoulder pain is to keep it gently moving rather than resting it completely, avoid activities that make it worse, and use pain relief and heat or cold packs if needed. Mild shoulder pain can take four to six weeks to recover fully. See a GP if it hasn't improved after two weeks, if it's very hard to move your arm, or if the pain began after a fall or injury. Get urgent help if the pain is sudden or severe, you can't move your arm, or it has changed shape or lost feeling.

Padel knee pain

Lunges, sudden stops and quick changes of direction all load the knee. Patellar tendinitis was among the diagnoses in the review, and NHS 111 Wales lists the typical sport-related causes of knee pain: sprains and strains from twisting or overuse, tendonitis from repeated running or jumping, and torn ligaments or cartilage, which often come with a pop, instability or the knee giving way.

Knee pain can often be treated at home, and the NHS says you should start to feel better within a few days: take weight off it, use an ice pack wrapped in a towel for up to 20 minutes every two to three hours, and consider simple painkillers. See a GP if it hasn't improved within a few weeks. Get urgent advice if you can't bear weight, the knee is badly swollen or changed shape, or it locks or gives way.

Calf, Achilles and ankle injuries

The calf is a classic padel injury. In the review, partial gastrocnemius ruptures made up 6.8% of injuries in one study and "tennis leg", the same calf-muscle tear, 22.1% of injuries by body region in another. Ankle sprains made up around 8%. Both happen in the explosive, stop-start movements padel is full of. Calf raises and single-leg strength work help, and so does a proper warm-up before you sprint for a drop shot. See our padel warm-up.

Shoes may matter too. A 2025 study of 216 Spanish players (Rocamora-López and Mateo-Orcajada) found more injuries in players with more weekly match hours, more muscle, tendon and ligament injuries among players using mixed or herringbone soles, and more muscle and ligament injuries on sand courts. It was a cross-sectional survey, so it can't prove cause and effect, but it's a reason to choose shoes made for your court. See padel shoes.

Eye injuries

Eye injuries are rare compared with the elbow, but they can be serious. In 2021, ophthalmologists at the University of Gothenburg reported that several Swedish eye clinics were seeing a rise in padel-related eye injuries, and described three cases that needed surgery. Most eye injuries are mild, but some cause lasting damage. They pointed to the ball speed, up to 130 km/h on a smash, and the difficulty of judging rebounds, and recommended eye protection: according to the university, goggles or a visor, used correctly, can cut the risk of eye damage by up to 90%.

How to prevent padel injuries

No padel-specific prevention trials exist yet, so this advice comes from broader sports-medicine research applied to what padel players actually get injured by.

  1. Build up gradually. The International Olympic Committee's consensus statement on load in sport says emerging evidence shows poor load management is a major risk factor for injury. The "new padel addict" pattern, going from nothing to four or five sessions a week, is exactly that. Add sessions one at a time.
  2. Strength train regularly. A 2014 meta-analysis of 25 trials (Lauersen and colleagues) found strength training cut sports injuries to less than a third, while stretching on its own had no benefit. Focus on legs, calves, core, shoulders and forearms.
  3. Warm up properly. Ten minutes of movement before you hit, then a gradual build-up of pace and overheads. The 3-minute courtesy rally before an official match isn't enough on its own.
  4. Fix the technique that overloads you. Late contact, a tight grip and arm-only overheads all load the elbow and shoulder. A coach can spot these quickly.
  5. Use the right equipment. Shoes for your surface, a racket that isn't too heavy for you, and eye protection if you're worried about your eyes. See padel equipment.
  6. Don't play through pain. Tendon problems in particular get harder to fix the longer you ignore them.

When to see a professional

See a doctor or physiotherapist if pain lasts more than a couple of weeks, keeps coming back when you play, or affects everyday tasks. Seek urgent help for a pop or sudden pain in the calf or knee, a joint that gives way or can't take weight, severe swelling, a deformed joint, or any blow to the eye that affects your vision.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common padel injury?

The elbow, usually lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow). A 2023 systematic review found the elbow was the joint most often reported, although the legs as a whole also account for many injuries.

Is padel bad for your knees?

Not inherently, but the lunges and quick changes of direction load the knee. Knee injuries made up roughly 10% to 23% of padel injuries across studies. Leg strength, a good warm-up and suitable shoes help.

Why does my shoulder hurt after padel?

Often from repeated overheads, especially if you've increased your playing quickly or your technique relies on the arm alone. If it doesn't improve within two weeks of self-care, see a GP or physiotherapist.

How common are injuries in padel?

Research suggests about 3 injuries per 1,000 hours of training and 8 per 1,000 matches, with 40% to 72% of players reporting an injury in the previous 8 to 12 months. The data is limited and mostly from Spain.

Should I wear eye protection for padel?

It's worth considering. Eye injuries are uncommon, but they can be serious, and researchers at the University of Gothenburg recommend goggles, saying that eye protection used correctly can reduce the risk of eye damage by up to 90%.
