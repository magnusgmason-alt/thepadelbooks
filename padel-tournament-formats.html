Padel tournament formats: which one should you play?

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 30 Oct 2026

The main padel tournament formats are the americano, the mexicano, king of the court, the box league, the round robin and the knockout, and each one suits a different group and a different amount of court time. Social nights usually use a rotating format where everyone plays all evening, while competitive events use fixed pairs and a draw. This guide explains how each format works, how long it takes and when to choose it.

None of these formats is set by the International Padel Federation (FIP). The FIP rules govern how a match is played and scored. Clubs and booking apps decide how matches are strung together, so expect small local differences.

Americano

Everyone plays as an individual. Partners and opponents rotate on a fixed schedule, every rally is a point, and matches are played to a fixed total such as 16, 24 or 32 points. You keep your pair's score as your own, so the player with the highest total wins. With 8 players on 2 courts, a full americano is 7 rounds, and everyone partners everyone once.

It's the default social format because nobody sits out and nobody gets knocked out. The weak spot is levels: with a wide spread of ability, some matches are one-sided. Our full guide to the padel americano covers the rules, scoring, team and mixed versions.

Mexicano padel

The mexicano keeps americano scoring but changes how players are paired. Round 1 is drawn at random. After that, players are ranked by points, and each round is drawn from the standings: the top four play on court 1, the next four on court 2, and so on. Apps split each group of four slightly differently; Visu's generator, for example, puts 1st and 3rd against 2nd and 4th.

The result is that matches get closer as the evening goes on, because you end up playing people of a similar level. Partners and opponents can repeat, which some players don't like. Choose it when your group has a wide range of levels and you want every match to be competitive.

King of the court padel

Courts are ranked from top to bottom, with court 1 as the "king" court. After each short round, the winners move up one court and the losers move down one. The pair on court 1 that wins stays there, and the pair on the bottom court that loses stays at the bottom.

Clubs run it in a few ways:

  • Fixed pairs or individuals. Some keep pairs together, others split the pair and rotate partners as players move.
  • Short matches. Typical rounds are a single game to a set number of points, or a few no-advantage games, on a 10 to 15 minute timer.
  • Scoring. Some count rounds won on court 1; others give more points for wins on higher courts, for example 4 for a win on court 1 down to 1 on court 4.

It sorts players by level quickly and creates a lot of energy, because every round has something at stake. It works best with about 8 to 16 players. With fewer, there isn't enough movement between courts.

Box league

A box league runs over weeks rather than one evening. Players are placed in small groups, the "boxes", by level. In a typical padel box league, a box has four players who arrange their own matches during the period, often a month, partnering each of the other three once. At the end of the period, the winner of each box moves up, the last player moves down, and the middle players stay.

Box leagues are the easiest way to get regular, level-matched games without a fixed partner. The catch is organisation: four busy people have to agree on dates, so the club needs clear deadlines for results.

Round robin

In a round robin, fixed pairs play every other pair once. The standings are based on matches won, then usually games or points difference. The number of matches grows quickly: with 4 pairs there are 6 matches, with 6 pairs there are 15, and with 8 pairs there are 28.

To keep it manageable, play short matches: one set, a timed match, or a mini set to four games. The FIP rulebook allows shorter options such as a 4-game set and a 10-point super tie-break, which are useful when court time is tight. Large events often split pairs into groups of three or four for a round robin, then send the top pairs into a knockout.

Knockout

A knockout is the classic tournament draw: lose and you're out, win and you go through to the next round until one pair is left. It's the only format that produces a clear champion pair in the fewest matches, which is why competitive events use it.

  • Single elimination: fastest. A 16-pair draw needs 15 matches over four rounds.
  • Consolation or plate draw: first-round losers go into a second draw, so everyone gets at least two matches.
  • Double elimination: you're out after two defeats. Fairer, but it takes much longer.
  • Groups then knockout: a round robin in small groups, then the top pairs go into the knockout. Common for weekend events.

Seed the draw by level or rating so the strongest pairs don't meet in round one. For social events, the big drawback is that half the pairs are out after their first match.

Which format for social nights?

Choose a rotating format when

People arrive alone, you want everyone to play all evening, and the aim is fun and meeting players. Americano for similar levels, mexicano for mixed levels.

Avoid a straight knockout when

It's a social night. Half the players are out after one match and stand around watching.

  • 8 players, 90 minutes, similar level: americano to 21 or 24 points.
  • 12 to 16 players, mixed levels: mexicano, or king of the court if the group likes competition.
  • Fixed pairs, one evening: round robin with short matches, or a team americano.
  • Regular games over a season: box league.
  • A proper club championship: groups then knockout, played over a day or a weekend.

How to fit a format into your court time

Court time is usually the limit, so work backwards from it.

  1. Count the courts and the minutes. Two courts for two hours is 240 court-minutes. Leave ten minutes for a warm-up and a few minutes between rounds.
  2. Pick the match length. A 24-point americano match takes about 10 minutes, a 32-point one about 15. A best-of-three-set match can take well over an hour.
  3. Shorten the scoring if needed. Golden point at 40–40, a super tie-break instead of a third set, or 4-game mini sets all save time. See golden point padel and padel scoring.
  4. Publish the plan. Share the format, round times and scoring before the day, so nobody argues on court.
Use an app

Club booking systems and dedicated apps generate americano, mexicano and king of the court schedules and keep the leaderboard. Playtomic, for example, lets organisers set a points target, commonly 12, 24 or 32, and choose whether the leaderboard ranks by points or wins.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most popular padel tournament format?

For social play, the americano. Everyone plays every round, partners rotate, and individual points decide the winner. Competitive events usually use fixed pairs in groups and a knockout.

What is mexicano padel?

A rotating format like the americano, but after a random first round each round is drawn from the current standings, so players of similar level play together.

How does king of the court work in padel?

Courts are ranked. After each short round, winners move up a court and losers move down. The player or pair with the most success on the top court wins.

What is a padel box league?

A league over several weeks where players are grouped in small boxes, often of four, by level. Box winners move up and the last players move down at the end of each period.

Which format is best for beginners?

An americano or mexicano for players of a similar level. Nobody is knocked out, matches are short, and you play with lots of different partners. See first time playing padel before your first event.
