Padel health benefits: 7 myths, and what the evidence really says

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel health benefits are real, but they're often oversold. Studies show padel keeps your heart rate at around 70 to 80% of maximum during play, which makes it genuine moderate-intensity exercise, and it's social enough that people keep coming back. What the research doesn't yet have is a single controlled trial proving padel-specific effects on weight, heart disease or mental health. Here are seven common claims, sorted into what's true and what's myth.

Myth 1: "Padel is too gentle to count as real exercise"

Truth

Padel is moderate-intensity exercise, with heart rates around 70 to 80% of maximum during matches.

Myth

Because it's doubles on a small court, padel is too easy to make a difference.

A 2022 review of padel research (Sánchez-Alcaraz and Courel-Ibáñez, International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health) summarises the physiological data: during matches players worked at about 40 to 50% of their maximum oxygen uptake, with heart rates of around 140 to 160 beats per minute, roughly 70 to 80% of maximum. Rallies are short, typically 10 to 15 seconds, with slightly longer rests in between, and players covered roughly 2,500 to 3,500 metres per match in the studies reviewed.

That profile fits the NHS description of moderate-intensity activity, and the NHS recommends adults get at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity. Two or three padel sessions a week gets most people there. So, is padel good exercise? Yes: it's a stop-start, moderate workout, harder if you play long rallies against good opponents.

Myth 2: "Padel is proven to make you live longer"

Truth

Racket sports are linked with lower death rates in a large observational study, but padel itself wasn't studied.

Myth

Science has shown that playing padel adds years to your life.

The study usually behind this claim followed 80,306 British adults, average age 52, for about nine years (Oja and colleagues, British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2017). People who played racket sports had a 47% lower risk of death from any cause and a 56% lower risk of death from heart disease and stroke than those who didn't. That's impressive, but it's observational, so it can't prove cause and effect: people who play racket sports may differ in other ways. The authors also noted a small number of deaths and self-reported activity. And padel wasn't analysed separately: the data came from health surveys in England and Scotland between 1994 and 2008.

Myth 3: "Padel will melt the weight off"

Truth

Padel burns a useful amount of energy and helps you keep weight off, but diet does most of the work for weight loss.

Myth

Play enough padel and you'll lose weight whatever you eat.

An hour of padel burns a meaningful number of calories; our padel calories guide works through the estimates. But Mayo Clinic's summary of the evidence is that cutting calories promotes weight loss more effectively than increasing exercise, while combining the two can give you an edge, and that people who keep weight off long term are regularly physically active. So is padel good for weight loss? It's a great partner to sensible eating and a strong habit for keeping weight off, especially because people enjoy it enough to keep playing. A post-match pint and chips can cancel it out quickly.

Myth 4: "Padel is scientifically proven to boost mental health"

Truth

Many players report feeling better and more connected, but padel-specific mental health research is thin.

Myth

Studies show padel reduces stress and anxiety.

A 2025 systematic review of padel, pickleball and wellbeing (Lauxtermann and Stubbs, Frontiers in Psychology) found eight padel studies on mental health with 538 participants, but they mostly looked at competitive performance: self-confidence, pre-match anxiety and mental fatigue. None of the included padel studies measured social outcomes, the samples skewed young and male, and the authors called for wellbeing research beyond emotional functioning.

That doesn't mean the social side isn't real; it just hasn't been measured well. LTA Padel highlights how the small court and doubles format keep you close to your partner and opponents, and links that sociability with people sticking with exercise. In LTA Padel's own feature, one player describes how group sessions helped her meet new people and improved her confidence. Enjoying it is a perfectly good reason to play.

Myth 5: "Padel replaces the gym"

Truth

Padel covers a lot of your cardio needs, but you still need strength training.

Myth

If you play padel, you don't need to do anything else.

The NHS recommends strengthening activities that work all the major muscle groups on at least two days a week, on top of aerobic activity. Padel doesn't load your muscles enough to tick that box, and strength training is also the best-supported way to reduce sports injuries. The best combination is padel plus two short strength sessions. So in the padel vs gym debate, it's not either-or: our padel exercises guide shows how to fit both into a week.

Myth 6: "Padel is a young person's sport"

Truth

People play padel well into later life, but injury risk rises with age and needs managing.

Myth

Padel is for 25-year-olds, or it's so gentle that age doesn't matter.

Both halves of this myth are wrong. Padel is low enough in impact, and doubles play forgiving enough, that plenty of people start in their forties, fifties and sixties. But injuries are common at every level, mostly overuse problems of the elbow, legs, shoulder and back, and at least one large survey found risk climbs after 40. See our guides to padel over 40 and padel for seniors for how to manage it.

Myth 7: "The health benefits of padel are all proven by trials"

Truth

Padel's health research is mostly descriptive; controlled trials are still missing.

Myth

Padel has been shown in trials to improve heart health, fitness and body composition.

The 2022 review by Sánchez-Alcaraz and Courel-Ibáñez is clear on this: the padel studies to date are cross-sectional or observational, and it found no padel intervention trials. We know a lot about how hard padel is and which injuries it causes; we know far less about what regular padel does to health over months and years compared with other exercise. Expect the same broad benefits as any regular moderate exercise, and be sceptical of precise numbers in headlines.

Frequently asked questions

Is padel good exercise?

Yes. Studies show heart rates of around 70 to 80% of maximum during matches, which makes padel moderate-intensity exercise that counts towards the recommended 150 minutes a week.

Is padel good for weight loss?

It helps, especially for keeping weight off, but changing your diet usually has a bigger effect on weight loss than exercise alone. Padel plus sensible eating works best.

Is padel good for your heart?

It's regular moderate exercise, which supports heart health. Racket sports are linked with lower heart-disease deaths in observational research, but padel hasn't been studied specifically.

Is padel good for mental health?

Many players say it lifts their mood and helps them meet people, but padel-specific research on mental health and social benefits is still very limited.

Is padel better than the gym?

They do different jobs. Padel is great cardio and fun; the gym builds the strength the NHS recommends twice a week and that helps prevent padel injuries. Do both.
