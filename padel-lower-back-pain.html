Padel back pain: straight answers to the questions players ask

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel back pain is common, and it usually comes from three movements: rotating hard on groundstrokes, bending low for volleys and balls off the glass, and arching back on smashes. In a 2025 survey of 645 French players, the lower back was the place players most often reported pain. The good news is that most back pain settles, staying active helps rather than hurts, and a stronger trunk and more mobile hips take a lot of load off your spine. Here are direct answers to the questions players ask most.

General information only

This isn't medical advice and can't diagnose the cause of your pain. The red-flag symptoms near the end need urgent attention.

Why does my lower back hurt after padel?

Usually because of the combination of rotation, bending and extension that padel asks for, repeated hundreds of times a session. The Aspetar review of padel injuries points to jumping, rotational and extension movements, and smashes in particular, as the main sources of load on the spine. Across the studies in the 2023 systematic review by Dahmen and colleagues, the lower back accounted for roughly 9% to 28% of injuries, and it was the fourth most commonly injured area after the elbow, knee and shoulder.

Pain is even more common than injury. In the 2025 French survey (Lecler and colleagues), the calf and elbow were the most commonly injured areas, but the lower back was where players most often reported pain. Many players live with a back that grumbles after a long session without ever having a diagnosable injury.

Which padel shots strain the back most?

Three situations stand out:

  • Smashes, especially the kick smash. Arching back to reach a high ball and then snapping forward is a big extension and flexion load. The Aspetar authors single out the kick smash.
  • Low volleys and balls out of the corner. If you reach down by bending at the waist instead of the knees, your lower back holds the weight of your upper body at a bad angle, over and over.
  • Big rotations from a fixed base. Swinging hard with your feet planted and your hips locked asks the lower back to rotate, which it isn't built to do much of.

The same review notes trunk problems appear more often in padel than in tennis, which makes sense on a smaller court with more low balls and more overheads.

Is padel bad for your back?

Not in itself. We found no research showing that recreational padel damages a healthy spine. What the research does show is that back pain is common among players, which says more about load and preparation than about the sport. NHS inform, the national health information service in Scotland, describes keeping active as an essential part of treatment and recovery for back problems. For most people, sensible padel is part of the solution.

The useful question isn't whether to play, but how much and how. A sudden jump in sessions, no warm-up and a weak trunk is the combination that tends to end in a sore back.

How do I get low with my legs, not my back?

This is the single biggest habit to change. Think "lower the hips" rather than "lower the racket".

  • Widen your base. Feet a little wider than shoulder-width, as in a good volley ready position, let you sink without folding forward.
  • Bend knees and hips together. Your chest stays reasonably upright and your back keeps its natural shape.
  • Use a lunge for wide, low balls. Step towards the ball and drop the back knee, rather than reaching with the arm and bending sideways.
  • Get there early. Most back-unfriendly shots happen when you're late and have to reach. Better positioning gives you time to set your legs; The Padel Playbook: Beginner & Intermediate explains where to stand with court diagrams.

What core and hip exercises help padel back pain?

Strength work has the best evidence of anything for preventing sports injuries: a 2014 meta-analysis (Lauersen and colleagues) found strength training reduced injuries to less than a third, while stretching programmes showed no clear benefit. For a padel back, focus on trunk control in all directions and hips that move well, so the spine doesn't have to make up for them.

ExerciseSets × repsWhat it does for padel
Dead bug3 × 8 each sideKeeps the trunk still while arms and legs move
Side plank3 × 20–30 s each sideSide-to-side control for lunges and wide balls
Bird dog3 × 8 each sideBack and hip control without load on the spine
Pallof press (band)3 × 10 each sideResisting rotation, so the hips and trunk turn as one
Glute bridge3 × 12Hip extension, taking work away from the lower back
Half-kneeling hip flexor stretch2 × 30 s each sideHip extension range for overheads
90/90 hip switches2 × 8 each sideHip rotation, so you turn from the hips, not the spine

Two sessions a week is enough to make a difference. These are starting points; if an exercise reproduces your back pain, swap it or ask a physiotherapist. More options are in padel exercises.

Should I play padel with back pain?

If it's a familiar, mild ache that eases as you warm up, gentle play is often fine. NHS inform advises avoiding long periods of sitting or resting and not bracing or holding yourself still. Leave out the smashes, take the lobs with a bandeja, and play shorter sessions until it settles.

If the pain builds during play, changes how you move, or is worse the next day, take a few days off padel while staying active in other ways. According to NHS inform, most flare-ups begin to settle within six weeks, with most of the improvement in the first couple of weeks. If you haven't improved after six weeks, speak to a healthcare professional.

Does warming up help my back?

It's sensible. Trunk rotations, walking lunges with a twist and a few minutes of easy hitting prepare the spine for the speed and range a match demands, and the Aspetar review lists inadequate warm-up among padel's injury risk factors. Our padel warm-up has a complete routine. Pair it with drills that train you to move your feet to low balls: the footwork drills in The Padel Drill Book work well for that.

When is back pain serious?

Contact your GP if your back pain is persistent and unchanging despite self-care, if movement doesn't affect the pain, or if it's worse at night. NHS inform describes cauda equina syndrome as a rare but serious condition that needs urgent attention. Call 111 (or your local emergency number) if you have back pain with any of these:

  • numbness or pins and needles between the inner thighs or genitals, or around the back passage or buttocks
  • new difficulty starting or stopping urination, or leaking urine
  • loss of bladder or bowel control, or loss of sensation when you go to the toilet
  • changes in erection, ejaculation or genital sensation
  • significant trauma, such as a fall from height, in the last seven days

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal for my back to ache after padel?

A mild ache after a long or intense session is common, and the lower back is the area padel players most often report pain. It should settle within a day or two. Pain that lingers, builds or keeps returning is worth addressing.

Can padel cause a slipped disc?

We found no evidence that recreational padel causes disc problems in a healthy back. If back pain comes with numbness, pins and needles or other new symptoms, get it assessed.

What is the best exercise for padel back pain?

There isn't a single best one. A mix of trunk control (dead bug, side plank, bird dog, Pallof press) and hip strength and mobility, twice a week, is a good place to start.

How long does padel back pain last?

According to NHS inform, most flare-ups of back pain begin to settle within six weeks, with most of the improvement in the first couple of weeks. If it hasn't improved after six weeks, see a healthcare professional.

Do smashes cause back pain?

They can contribute, especially the kick smash, which combines arching back, jumping and rotating. Replacing some smashes with bandejas reduces the load.
