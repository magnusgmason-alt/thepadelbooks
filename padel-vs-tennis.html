Padel vs tennis: what's the difference?

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

Padel vs tennis comes down to one thing: the walls. Padel is played as doubles on a smaller, enclosed court where the ball stays in play off the glass, with an underarm serve and solid rackets; tennis is open, bigger and built around power. Tennis players pick padel up fast, but the habits that win tennis points often lose padel ones. Here's what's different and how to adapt.

Padel vs tennis at a glance

PadelTennis
Court20 × 10 m, enclosed by glass and mesh23.77 m long; 8.23 m wide for singles, 10.97 m for doubles; open
WallsIn play after the bounceNone: out is out
Net88 cm in the middle, 92 cm at the postsAbout 91 cm in the middle
RacketSolid, stringless, perforated; max 45.5 cm; wrist cord compulsoryStrung, longer
ServeUnderarm, after a bounce, at or below waist heightUsually overarm, a major weapon
FormatAlmost always doublesSingles and doubles
ScoringTennis scoring, plus golden point or Star Point options at 40–40Standard tennis scoring

The walls change everything

In tennis, a ball that gets past you is gone. In padel, once it bounces on your side it can come off the back or side glass and you can still play it, as long as you hit it before the second bounce. You can even hit the ball into your own glass to get it back over the net. The only limit is that your shot has to bounce on the opponents' floor before it touches their walls.

The result is longer rallies, fewer outright winners and a game decided far more by position and patience than by power. Hit a tennis-style drive deep and hard, and a decent padel player simply lets it come off the glass and plays it back.

Padel ball vs tennis ball

They look nearly identical, but they're not the same ball.

Diameter6.35–6.77 cm6.54–6.86 cm
Mass56.0–59.4 g56.0–59.4 g
Rebound from 2.54 m135–145 cm135–147 cm
Internal pressureSlightly lowerSlightly higher

The padel ball is a little smaller and has slightly less internal pressure, which makes it a touch slower and easier to control in a small space. Can you use tennis balls for padel? For a casual knock, yes. For proper play, use padel balls: tennis balls bounce and fly differently off the glass.

Is padel easier than tennis?

To start, yes. The underarm serve removes tennis's biggest barrier, the court is smaller, and the walls give you a second chance. Babolat's comparison notes that complete beginners can often exchange 10 to 20 shots in their first padel sessions, while in tennis it can take months to rally properly.

To master, no. Padel's difficulty moves from technique to decisions: when to let the ball come off the glass, when to lob, when to attack and where to stand with your partner. The Padel School puts it well: early progress can come quickly, but tactical understanding takes longer.

Padel for tennis players: what transfers

Coaches at The Padel School list the strengths tennis players typically bring:

  • Volleys and reactions at the net.
  • Clean ball striking and comfort with difficult balls.
  • Power, which becomes useful once it's controlled.
  • The split step, which coaches call crucial for preparation and movement.

And what typically goes wrong: avoiding the walls, rushing the net, struggling to control the smash, and going for drop shots and winners too early.

Padel tips for tennis players

  1. Use one grip. Padel coaching generally teaches the continental grip for forehands, backhands, volleys and overheads, so you never have to change grip in a fast exchange. It also makes slice and back-glass play easier. See our guide to the padel grip.
  2. Shorten the swing. A full tennis take-back is too slow in a 10 × 10 m half and sends the ball into the back glass. Compact, controlled swings win.
  3. Swap big drives for control and slice. The Padel School points out that a flat ball comes up higher after it hits the back glass, giving the opponents an easier ball, while slice keeps it low.
  4. Let the ball come to you. Practise letting deep balls come off the back glass instead of rushing to take them early.
  5. Learn to love the lob. In tennis the lob is an emergency shot. In padel it's how you win back the net. See our lob guide.
  6. Don't smash everything. A big smash often bounces off the back glass back to the opponents. Learn the bandeja to keep the net instead.
  7. Get padel coaching early. The Padel School's main advice for tennis players is to stop treating padel as a smaller version of tennis, and to fix positioning and wall play before tennis habits set in.

Padel rules for tennis players

Most rules will feel familiar. These are the ones that differ:

  • Serve: underarm, after a bounce behind the service line, struck at or below the waist with at least one foot on the ground. A serve that bounces in the box and then hits the mesh is a fault; the glass is fine.
  • No lines in the rally: the lines only matter on the serve. During the rally, the walls are the boundary.
  • Walls: legal after the ball bounces on your side, and your own glass is usable to get the ball back over.
  • Touching the net: lose the point, same as tennis, and the same applies to the posts.
  • Scoring at deuce: check whether you're playing advantage, golden point or Star Point. See padel scoring.
  • Wrist cord: compulsory, not optional.

Calories: padel vs tennis

There's no single honest number for either sport, because energy use depends on your weight, your level and how hard the game is. Here's what the research supports.

The 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities, the standard reference researchers use, has no entry for padel. For tennis it lists 4.5 to 6.0 METs for doubles and 8.0 METs for singles. A MET is a multiple of your resting energy use; roughly, calories per hour equal METs multiplied by your weight in kilograms. For a 70 kg player that's about 315–420 kcal an hour for doubles tennis and about 560 kcal for singles.

For padel, studies summarised in the Aspetar Sports Medicine Journal report heart rates of roughly 50–70% of maximum (approaching 70% in amateur matches) and oxygen uptake below 45% of maximum: mostly moderate intensity, though the same review notes it can be more intense than doubles tennis. A fast, competitive padel match will burn more; a relaxed social game, less. Our padel calories guide goes into the evidence in more detail.

Which should you play?

Choose padel if you want quick progress, long rallies, a social game and a sport where tactics beat power. Choose tennis if you love singles, open space and big serves. Plenty of people play both, and the skills help in each direction, as long as you treat them as separate sports.

Frequently asked questions

Is padel easier than tennis?

Easier to start, thanks to the underarm serve, smaller court and walls. Harder to master tactically, because the walls create many more options.

Can tennis players play padel straight away?

Yes, and they usually progress fast at first. The common hurdles are avoiding the glass, over-hitting and rushing the net.

Is a padel ball the same as a tennis ball?

No. It's the same weight but slightly smaller, with slightly lower pressure, so it plays a little slower.

Does padel burn more calories than tennis?

Not according to the available evidence. Recreational padel is mostly moderate intensity, in the range of doubles tennis or slightly above; singles tennis is usually more demanding. Your weight and the pace of the game matter more than the sport.

Is padel harder on the body than tennis?

It's different. Padel involves lots of short bursts, lunges, rotations and overheads. Build your playing time gradually, especially if you're coming from little or no racket sport, and see a qualified professional for persistent pain.
