Padel exercises: what to train, and why

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

The most useful padel exercises are simple strength and movement drills for the legs, core, shoulders and forearms, done two or three times a week: squats and lunges, calf raises, planks and rotation work, rotator cuff and shoulder-blade exercises, and short, sharp footwork. Research on sports injuries supports strength training far more than stretching. Here is what to do at home and in the gym, and why it matters for padel.

What padel asks of your body

Good padel exercises start from what the sport actually demands. A 2023 review of the research (Guijarro-Herencia and colleagues, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living) found that points in professional men's padel last about 9 to 13 seconds, with longer points in women's padel, and that players work at around 70 to 80% of their maximum heart rate on average. Padel is intermittent: short bursts of movement and hitting, then brief rests.

The injury picture points the same way. A 2023 systematic review in BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine (Dahmen and colleagues) found around 3 injuries per 1,000 hours of training and 8 per 1,000 matches. The elbow was the most commonly reported injury site, followed by the knee, shoulder and lower back, and tendon and muscle injuries were the most common types. The authors are careful to say the evidence is limited, but it tells you where to focus: legs for the lunges and changes of direction, core and back for the rotation, and the shoulder, forearm and elbow for the hitting.

What the evidence says about exercise and injuries

Two pieces of research are worth knowing, even though neither is padel-specific.

  • Strength beats stretching. A 2014 meta-analysis of randomised trials in the British Journal of Sports Medicine (Lauersen and colleagues) found that strength training reduced sports injuries to less than a third, and balance (proprioception) training also reduced them. Stretching programmes showed no beneficial effect.
  • Shoulder programmes can work for overhead sports. A randomised trial in 660 elite handball players (Andersson and colleagues, BJSM, 2017) found that a shoulder programme done three times a week as part of the warm-up, targeting rotator cuff strength, shoulder-blade strength, thoracic mobility and the kinetic chain, lowered the risk of shoulder problems by 28%. Handball isn't padel, but the overhead load is a reasonable comparison for players who smash and play bandejas a lot.

For general health, the NHS recommends strengthening activities that work all the major muscle groups on at least two days a week. Two short sessions a week of the exercises below, plus a pushing exercise such as press-ups for the chest and arms, meets that and supports your padel.

Padel exercises at home (no equipment)

All of these need nothing more than a bit of floor space, and a resistance band for the shoulder work if you have one. Padel New Zealand recommends this mix of leg, core and shoulder work for injury prevention.

The sets and reps are sensible starting points, not prescriptions. Stop if something hurts, and build up gradually.

Padel exercises for beginners

If you're new to padel and to training, start smaller. Twice a week, pick five exercises from the table, do two sets of each and focus on clean technique. The Padel School's physio advice is that a programme should match your current ability and injury history, and that a routine you can follow consistently beats a complicated one. After three or four weeks, add a third set or a little load.

Beginners also need to manage how fast they ramp up their playing time. Padel elbow is an overuse injury, so a sudden jump in playing time is a common risk; Padel New Zealand recommends increasing frequency and duration gradually and building in rest days.

Padel exercises in the gym

With weights available, you can load the same movement patterns:

  • Goblet squat or trap-bar deadlift: leg and hip strength.
  • Split squat or walking lunge with dumbbells: single-leg strength for lunges and changes of direction.
  • Romanian deadlift: hamstrings and lower back.
  • Single-arm row and lat pulldown: back and shoulder-blade strength to balance all the forward hitting.
  • Cable or band rotations (pallof press, wood chop): controlled trunk rotation.
  • Calf raises on a step: Achilles and calf capacity.

Choose loads you can lift with clean technique, and progress slowly. If you're unsure about technique or loading, a session with a qualified coach or physiotherapist is money well spent.

Padel shoulder exercises

Every serve, bandeja, víbora and smash loads the shoulder. Benenden Hospital's physiotherapists list exercises such as external shoulder rotation, single-arm pull-downs, prone holds, lateral raises, wrist rotations and core strengthening for players with padel shoulder. For healthy players, a short routine based on the same ideas:

  1. Band external rotation, elbow at your side: 2–3 × 12.
  2. Band pull-apart, arms straight in front: 2–3 × 15, squeezing the shoulder blades together.
  3. Prone Y and T raises, lying face down, light or no weight: 2 × 10 each.
  4. Single-arm band pull-down: 2–3 × 12 each arm.
  5. Wrist extension and flexion with a light dumbbell, forearm supported: 2 × 15. Lower slowly.

The handball trial above ran its programme three times a week as part of the warm-up. You can do the same: a few minutes of band work before you play doubles as shoulder preparation. See our padel warm up for the rest of the routine.

Not medical advice

If you have shoulder or elbow pain that lasts, see a physiotherapist or doctor before following any exercise programme. Persistent pain needs a proper assessment, not a generic routine.

Padel training exercises for speed and footwork

Because padel points are short bursts, your conditioning should be too. Long, slow runs have their place for general fitness, but on-court speed comes from short, sharp work with rest:

  • Split step and react: a partner points left, right, forward or back; split step, take two quick steps, recover. 6 × 20 seconds.
  • Lateral shuffle to touch: shuffle between two markers about three metres apart, touching each. 6 × 15 seconds with 30 seconds' rest.
  • Net to back and back: start at the net, turn and move back to the service line as if for a lob, then return. 6 to 8 reps.
  • Shadow points: play an imaginary point of five or six shots at full intensity, including footwork, then rest. Repeat 8 to 10 times.

Keep the work bouts close to real point length, roughly 10 to 20 seconds, and rest between them. For on-court drills with a ball, see our solo padel drills.

A sample weekly plan

  • Day 1: lower body and core (home or gym), 30 minutes.
  • Day 2: padel.
  • Day 3: upper body and shoulders, plus footwork, 30 minutes.
  • Day 4: padel.
  • Day 5 to 7: one more padel session and at least one rest or easy day.

Adjust to your schedule. The aim is two strength sessions, regular padel and enough recovery. The Padel School's physio advice is that if you're repeatedly sore or tired, reducing your load may help more than adding training.

Frequently asked questions

What exercises are good for padel?

Squats, lunges, calf raises, balance work, planks and side planks, rotation exercises, and rotator cuff and shoulder-blade exercises, plus short footwork drills. They target the areas padel loads most.

Can I do padel exercises at home?

Yes. Bodyweight squats, lunges, calf raises, glute bridges, planks and balance work need no equipment, and a resistance band covers the shoulder exercises.

How often should I train for padel?

Two strength sessions a week is a good target, in line with the NHS recommendation for strengthening activities. Add footwork work and leave time for recovery.

Does strength training prevent padel injuries?

There's no padel-specific trial yet, but a large meta-analysis across sports found strength training reduced injuries to less than a third. Stretching programmes showed no beneficial effect.

Which padel shoulder exercises should I do?

Band external rotations, band pull-aparts, prone Y and T raises and single-arm pull-downs are common choices. If your shoulder already hurts, see a physiotherapist first.
