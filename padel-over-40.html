Padel over 40: 10 rules for playing longer, smarter and pain-free

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel over 40 is a great idea: it's sociable, it's competitive at every age and the small court rewards anticipation over raw speed. But the research is blunt about one thing: in a 2025 study of 645 French players, those over 40 had about four times the odds of injury of younger players. These ten rules are about keeping the fun and losing the injuries, from how often to play to the racket in your hand.

General information, not medical advice

If you've been inactive for a long time, or have a heart, joint or other medical condition, check with your doctor before ramping up your padel.

Is padel good for over 40s?

Yes, for most people. It's a moderate-intensity, stop-start sport played in doubles, so you can enjoy it without being an athlete, and competitive age-group padel runs well into later life. The catch is that your body adapts more slowly than it did at 25, and padel punishes players who forget that. The rules below are built around the injuries that hit this age group most.

1. Know your real risk

In the French survey by Lecler and colleagues (Journal de Traumatologie du Sport, 2025), being over 40 was the strongest risk factor identified, with odds of injury about four times those of younger players. The Aspetar review of padel injuries adds that muscle strains are more common in players over 35, while younger players have more ligament injuries. Not every study agrees: a 2025 survey of 364 amateur players in Chile found injury rates similar across age groups. Either way, half or more of the players in both studies reported a recent injury, so prevention is worth your time at any age.

2. Build up your sessions slowly

The classic over-40 injury story starts with "I got hooked and started playing four times a week". Padel New Zealand describes load management as particularly relevant for older adults, new players and people returning after a break, and advises increasing frequency and duration gradually with rest days built in. Add one session at a time, and hold it for a few weeks before adding another.

3. Warm up every single time

The Aspetar review lists inadequate warm-up among padel's main injury risk factors, and the 3-minute rally before a match is nowhere near enough. Ten minutes of raising your heart rate, mobilising hips, shoulders and ankles, then split steps and short accelerations, is the minimum. Our padel warm-up lays it out step by step.

4. Lift something twice a week

The NHS recommends strengthening activities that work all the major muscle groups on at least two days a week. For padel after 40, that's not optional extra fitness: stronger legs, calves, trunk and shoulders absorb the lunges, stops and overheads that otherwise land on your tendons. Two 30-minute sessions of squats, lunges, calf raises, rows and band work make a real difference. Our padel exercises guide has a full plan.

5. Respect your calves and Achilles

If one injury defines padel over 40, it's the calf. The Aspetar review says "tennis leg", a tear in the calf muscle during a push-off, is most common in poorly conditioned men over 40, and the calf and Achilles complex was the most injured area of all in the French survey. Calf raises, a proper warm-up and not sprinting flat out from cold are your protection. Read our guide to calf strain in padel for the full picture.

6. Choose an arm-friendly racket

Elbow and shoulder problems creep up with age and volume. The usual advice from coaches and retailers is a racket towards the lighter end for your size, with an even or low balance and a softer face, which takes some sting out of off-centre hits. There's little research on racket specs and injury, so treat it as sensible rather than proven, but there's no reason to swing a heavy, stiff, head-heavy racket built for younger power players. Our guide to soft vs hard padel rackets explains the trade-offs.

7. Play smarter, not faster

Experienced players over 40 often beat fitter opponents by making them run. Three habits do most of the work:

  • Win the net with the lob. A good lob over the opponents moves them back and lets you take over the net, the attacking position in padel.
  • Use the walls. Letting a ball come off the back glass gives you time; chasing everything in the air costs energy.
  • Stay with your partner. Moving together as a pair closes the gaps, so neither of you has to sprint across the court.

The Padel Playbook: Intermediate & Advanced covers net control and the match tactics that win close games, which matter far more than pace at club level.

8. Plan your recovery between matches

Padel New Zealand advises planning rest or lighter sessions into your week and watching for early warning signs such as soreness or fatigue that doesn't go away. In practice, after 40:

  • avoid hard matches on back-to-back days when you're building up
  • eat a proper meal with carbohydrate and protein after you play; see what to eat before (and after) padel
  • sleep counts as training; a late match followed by a short night is when niggles start
  • replace one match a week with a drill session, which gives the same skill work with less all-out sprinting

Drills also let you control the volume: The Padel Drill Book has solo, partner and four-player drills you can scale to your level.

9. How often should you play padel over 40?

There's no research-based number. A 2022 review of padel research (Sánchez-Alcaraz and Courel-Ibáñez) describes recreational players typically playing two or three sessions a week of 60 to 120 minutes. For most people over 40, two or three sessions with at least one rest day between the hardest ones, plus two short strength sessions, is a sensible pattern. More is fine if you've built up to it gradually and your body isn't sending warning signs.

10. Don't play through warning signs

Tendon pain that warms up and then flares afterwards, a calf that feels tight on the first sprint, a shoulder that aches at night: these are early signals, and they're easier to fix in week one than in month three. Cut the load, keep moving and get a physiotherapist's view if it doesn't settle within a couple of weeks.

Stop immediately and get urgent help for chest pain, severe breathlessness or feeling faint during play; a sudden pop or kick in the calf or Achilles; or a knee that gives way or swells quickly.

Frequently asked questions

Is padel good for people over 40?

Yes. It's sociable, moderate-intensity exercise that rewards experience and tactics. The main thing to manage is injury risk, which rises after 40, through gradual build-up, warm-ups and strength training.

Is it too late to start padel at 40 or 50?

No. Many players start in their forties, fifties and beyond, and age-group competitions run into later life. Take lessons, build up slowly and add strength work.

How many times a week should I play padel over 40?

Two or three sessions, with a rest day between the hardest ones, suits most players. Increase gradually and add two short strength sessions a week.

What is the most common padel injury over 40?

Calf and Achilles problems are especially common, along with the elbow. One French study found the calf and Achilles complex was the most injured area, and tennis leg is most common in men over 40.

What racket is best for padel over 40?

Usually a lighter racket with an even or low balance and a softer face, which is kinder to the elbow and shoulder. Comfort and control matter more than raw power.
