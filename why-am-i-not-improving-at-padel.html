Why am I not improving at padel? Find the cause, then fix it

8 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

If you keep asking yourself "why am I not improving at padel?", the cause is almost always one of six things: you only play matches, you always play the same people, nobody gives you feedback, a technical habit has hit its ceiling, your body can't yet do what the next level asks, or you play well in practice and tighten up in matches. Each has a different fix. Take the self-test below to find yours, then follow the 4-week reset plan.

Why am I not improving at padel? The short version

Most players improve quickly for the first months, then stall. That is normal, not a sign that you have reached your limit. Early progress comes from simply turning up: every session teaches you something about the walls, the scoring and where to stand. Once those basics are in place, more of the same stops teaching you much, and progress needs a different input.

Research on skill learning points the same way. In their famous 1993 study, Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer separated "deliberate practice", effortful work designed to improve performance and built around feedback, from simply playing. Later studies have tempered the big claims: a 2016 meta-analysis by Macnamara, Moreau and Hambrick found that deliberate practice explained about 18% of the differences in sports performance. That is a meaningful share, but it leaves plenty of room for coaching, fitness, the people you play with and how you handle pressure. Which is why a padel plateau rarely has just one cause.

Cause 1: you only play matches

This is the most common reason. A match feels like practice, but it gives you very few repetitions of any single shot. The Padel School points out that after an hour of play you may have hit a particular shot only a handful of times. Its summary is hard to argue with: training builds skills, matches test them.

The fix: keep playing matches, but add structure. Pick one skill, repeat it with small adjustments and count the result. The Padel School suggests two or three focused sessions a week can make a real difference, and that consistency matters more than volume. If that is unrealistic, one session a week is still far better than none. The Padel Drill Book has 100 drills for solo, partner and four-player sessions, with a level for each, so you can build that hour without guessing.

Cause 2: same partners, same opponents

If you play the same three people every Tuesday, your game adapts to them. You learn that one opponent never lobs and the other can't handle a ball to the backhand. That wins the Tuesday match and teaches you very little about padel.

The fix: widen the pool. Play an americano, join a box league, accept open matches with strangers. Play some matches with stronger players, who punish your weak shots and show you better positions. If you use a rating app, note that on Playtomic only competitive matches change your level, and beating higher-rated opponents lifts it more. Our guides on how to find padel players and choosing a padel partner help here.

Cause 3: nobody tells you what's wrong

Feedback is the heart of deliberate practice, and most club players get almost none. Your memory of a match is unreliable: you remember the great volley and forget the six balls you left in the middle.

The fix: get one honest outside view a month. Three options cost little:

  • Video. Prop your phone behind the back glass and record a set. Our guide to filming your padel game shows what to look for.
  • Numbers. Ask a friend to tally your unforced errors and winners for one set. If the errors fall over the following weeks, you are improving, whatever the score.
  • A coach. Even one lesson every few weeks gives you a priority list.

Cause 4: a technique ceiling

Some habits work at one level and break at the next. The Padel School sees this most in players who skip coaching, often people from other racket sports who rely on matches and natural talent. They reach the top of their club, then stall because the fundamentals were never built, and old habits get harder to change the longer they last. The three areas it singles out are the glass, the overheads and the groundstrokes.

The grip

Most padel coaching starts from a continental grip for volleys, overheads and most groundstrokes. If you change grip for every shot, you will be late at the net. See our padel grip guide.

The walls

Being comfortable off the back glass is the skill that moves most beginners up a level. If you hit the ball before it reaches the glass because you don't trust the rebound, you are taking the hardest version of every shot. Our back glass guide breaks it down.

The overhead

If every lob sends you running back, you can never hold the net. A controlled bandeja is usually the missing piece between intermediate and advanced. The Padel Playbook: Intermediate & Advanced covers the bandeja, the víbora and net control with court diagrams.

Cause 5: fitness and footwork

Padel is a game of short, sharp movements. Research summarised in the Aspetar Sports Medicine Journal found professional players cover about 11 metres per point on average, and often less than 8 metres. Those few metres need a quick first step, a split step as the opponent hits, and legs strong enough to stay low for volleys late in a match.

The fix: two 20-minute sessions a week of lunges, squats, lateral shuffles and short accelerations will show up on court within weeks. If you are carrying a niggle that changes how you move, get it looked at before you train harder.

Cause 6: it falls apart under pressure

If you play freely in practice and tight in matches, the problem is not your forehand. Train with a score and a consequence, and give yourself a routine for the 20 seconds between points. Our padel mental game guide has nine habits that fit inside a club match.

Three rules make the plan work. One skill at a time. Numbers on paper, not feelings. And no new racket during the four weeks, or you will never know what made the difference.

When it isn't really a plateau

Sometimes the rating stalls while the game improves. On Playtomic, new players have low "reliability", so their level swings a lot while the system learns their game, and losing to lower-rated opponents costs more than losing to higher-rated ones. If you have just started playing stronger people, a flat or falling number can hide real progress. Judge yourself on errors and drill scores over a month, not on one week's rating. Frequently asked questions

Why am I not improving at padel even though I play a lot?

Because matches give you few repetitions of any single shot and little feedback. Keep playing, but add one focused practice session a week on one skill, and measure something, such as unforced errors per set.

How do I break a padel plateau?

Find the main cause first: match-only play, the same opponents, no feedback, a technique habit, fitness or pressure. Then work on that one thing for four weeks, with a measurement at the start and the end.

Should I take lessons if I'm already an intermediate player?

Usually yes. Coaches see technique ceilings, such as grip and glass habits, that are hard to spot yourself, and habits get harder to change the longer you leave them.

Can a new racket fix a plateau?

Rarely. A racket that suits you helps comfort and control, but it won't fix positioning, decisions or technique. Change one thing at a time so you know what worked.

How long does a padel plateau last?

There is no fixed length. It lasts as long as you keep doing the same things. Players who add structured practice and feedback usually see a change in their numbers within a few weeks.
