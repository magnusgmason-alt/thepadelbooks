The padel drop shot: how and when to play the dejada

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 30 Oct 2026

A padel drop shot, or dejada, is a soft shot that lands just over the net and dies before a player at the back of the court can reach it. Most are played as a gentle volley from the net when the opponents are deep. It's a lovely shot and an easy one to overuse, so this guide covers when the drop shot is on, how to hit it, what the rules say when it bounces back or out through the door, and how to defend against one.

What is a drop shot in padel?

A drop shot is a deliberately soft ball that just clears the net and lands close to it. HEAD sums it up as "delicate but devastating". In Spanish it's the dejada, and a very soft one that barely bounces is sometimes called a dormilona, the "sleepy" ball.

It works for a simple reason. Padel players defend from the back of the court, close to the glass, so they can play the ball after it rebounds. That leaves a big empty space between them and the net. A ball that drops into that space and stays low is very hard to reach.

The Padel Weekly describes it as a shot for advanced players, and that matches what coaches see: beginners and tennis converts tend to go for drop shots too early, before they can control their volleys. Get your basic volley reliable first.

Drop shot, chiquita or dormilona?

ShotWhere you areWhere the opponents areGoal
Dejada (drop shot)Usually at the net, volleyingAt the back of the courtWin the point outright, or force a desperate run forward
DormilonaAt the netAt the backA drop so soft the ball hardly bounces
ChiquitaAt the backAt the netMake the net players volley upwards so you can move forward

The terms overlap in everyday use, and some glossaries use "dormilona" for any drop shot. The useful distinction is the situation: a drop shot attacks an empty front court; a chiquita attacks the feet of players who are already there.

When to play a padel drop shot

Coach Ramiro Choya, speaking to Padel Addict, calls the dejada "the art of deception". It works when the opponents don't expect it and can't get there. Look for these situations:

  • The opponents are deep. Usually after a deep volley or a good bandeja has pushed them right back to the glass.
  • They're expecting pace. After a run of firm, deep volleys, a soft one is a surprise.
  • The court is slow or damp. Choya points out that the ball bounces lower in these conditions, so the drop dies faster.
  • One opponent is slower. Aim the drop on the side of the player less likely to reach it.

When not to play it

  • When an opponent is already near the service line or moving forward. They'll get there and punish it.
  • When the ball is low and fast and you're stretching. Soft touch needs balance.
  • When you've just played one. A predictable drop shot is an easy ball.
  • As your only weapon. The Padel School's advice on finishing at the net applies here: make sure the opponents are out of position first, and give yourself a margin for error.

How to hit a drop volley, step by step

Most padel drop shots are volleys. This is the technique Choya describes, which follows the same principles as the standard volley.

  1. Continental grip. The same grip as all your volleys. It opens the racket face naturally and makes slice easier.
  2. Prepare exactly like a normal volley. Same shoulder turn, same racket position. If you prepare differently, the opponents read it and start running early. Decide at the last moment.
  3. Relax the front knee at contact. It softens the whole body into the shot.
  4. Let the ball come a little further back. Contact slightly later than usual takes pace off the ball.
  5. Cut under the ball. Less power, more slice. An open face and a short, gentle cut make the ball check and stay low.
  6. Barely follow through. A long swing adds pace. Stop the racket early.
The height check

Choya's test for a good dejada: after its first bounce, the ball should stay below the height of the net tape. If it bounces up higher than that, it was too firm, and a quick opponent will reach it.

Where to aim

  • Cross-court. Choya recommends it: the ball has more court to land in, so there's less risk of missing.
  • Into the gap. The empty front court between the net and the deep player.
  • Away from the faster player. Make the slower opponent run.

Don't aim for the line or try to skim the tape. Clear the net by a safe margin and let the slice do the work.

The drop shot from the back of the court

Occasionally you'll see a drop shot from the back, played after the ball has come off the glass. Padelcentrum describes it as a counter-attack: after a ball rebounds off the back wall, the opponents expect a firm reply, so a soft one can surprise them. It's much riskier than a drop volley, because the ball has further to travel and the net players are usually close. If the opponents are at the net, a chiquita or a lob is nearly always the better option.

What the rules say about drop shots

A drop shot is an ordinary shot under the FIP rules, but two situations come up more often with drops than with anything else.

Ball spins back over the net

If your drop bounces correctly on their side and then comes back over to yours, the opponents may play it, even reaching over, as long as they don't touch the net, the posts or your court (Rule 14g). See can you reach over the net.

Opponent touches the net

If an opponent rushing in for your drop touches the net or posts with their body, racket or clothing while the ball is in play, they lose the point (Rule 13a).

A drop with angle can also bounce and leave through a side door. Where out-of-court play isn't authorised, that wins you the point (Rule 13d). Where it is, the opponents can run out and play it, provided it hasn't bounced a second time or touched anything outside the court (Rule 13e). See out-of-court play.

How to defend against a drop shot

Good players don't just react to the drop; they make it harder to play.

  • Don't crowd the glass. Stand far enough back to play the rebound, but not so close that you're pinned against the wall with no way forward. It's the same advice as in our back glass guide.
  • Watch the volleyer's racket. A face opening up and a shortened swing are the clues. You won't always read it, but a fast first step forward gives you a chance.
  • Get low and lift. If you get there, you'll be playing from below the net. Bend your knees and lift the ball, either deep as a lob or softly cross-court, rather than trying to hit hard upwards.
  • Talk to your partner. If one of you is pulled in, the other covers the lob and the middle.
  • Stop feeding it. The drop shot comes from short, floating balls that give the net player time. Deep lobs and low balls to their feet give them nothing to drop.

How to practise the drop shot

With a partner feeding from the back of the court, alternate: three firm, deep volleys, then one disguised drop. The feeder calls out as soon as they read the drop. When they stop calling early, your disguise is working. Then place a towel just over the net, cross-court, and count how many drops land on it and stay below tape height after the bounce.

Frequently asked questions

What is a dejada in padel?

Dejada is the Spanish name for the drop shot: a soft ball, usually a volley, that lands just over the net while the opponents are at the back of the court.

When should you play a drop shot in padel?

When the opponents are deep, near the back glass, and expecting a firm ball. Avoid it when an opponent is already near the service line or moving forward.

What is the difference between a drop shot and a chiquita?

A drop shot is usually played from the net into the empty front court while the opponents are deep. A chiquita is played from the back towards the feet of opponents who are at the net.

Can you reach over the net to play a drop shot?

Only if the ball has bounced on your side and come back over the net. You may then play it on the other side, as long as you don't touch the net, the posts or the opponents' court (Rule 14g).

Is the drop shot good for beginners?

Not as a first priority. Learn controlled, deep volleys first. The drop shot works best once you can disguise it within a normal volley.
