First time playing padel? Here's what to expect

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 30 Oct 2026

Your first time playing padel will usually be an hour on court with three other people, a hired racket and plenty of short, scrappy rallies, and most beginners are enjoying it within the first few minutes. You don't need to be fit, know the rules perfectly or own any kit. This guide walks you through that first session: how to book it, what to bring, what actually happens on court, and the etiquette that makes you a welcome fourth player.

Is padel easy to learn?

Easier than most racket sports, yes. The LTA says padel is generally considered easier to learn than tennis because of the smaller court, the underarm serve and the slower ball. The solid racket and the walls also help: balls that would be lost in tennis come back into play, so rallies start sooner.

That doesn't mean it's easy to play well. The glass walls take time to read, and the tactics run deep. But for a first session, the bar is low. If you can make contact with a ball, you'll have rallies. If you've played tennis or squash, expect the back glass to be the strangest part: the LTA calls getting used to it the biggest early challenge for tennis players.

How to book your first game

There are four common ways in. Pick whichever suits how confident you feel.

OptionHow it worksGood for
Book a court with friendsReserve a court for an hour, usually on an app or the club's website, and split the cost four waysGroups who want a relaxed first go
Intro session or clinicA one-off beginner class with a coach; rackets and balls are often providedAnyone who wants guidance from the start
Beginner courseA short series of weekly lessons, such as the LTA's six-week Padel Xpress course in BritainPeople who already know they want to keep playing
Open matchJoin a game other players have posted, matched by levelSolo players, once they've had a session or two

Booking apps such as Playtomic let you search for open matches by place and time and join on your own; you pay your share online when you sign up. Open matches carry a level range, and if you're outside it you have to ask the other players to accept you. Be honest about your level: a game that's too strong for you is no fun for anyone. Our guide to padel levels explains the scales.

For a very first session, an intro class or a game with friends is usually more comfortable than an open match with strangers. Padel Browser's survey of US clubs found every intro clinic it checked ran for an hour, prices ranged from free to about $40 for non-members, rackets were usually included and balls always came from the club. For court hire prices, see our guide to how much padel costs.

What to bring to your first padel session

  • Shoes: court shoes with good grip and side support, and non-marking soles if you're playing indoors. Some clubs spell it out: one UK chain asks for court shoes with "no studs or spikes", so leave football boots and running spikes at home. Running shoes are built for moving forwards and can feel unstable on sideways movement; see padel shoes.
  • Comfortable sportswear: whatever you'd wear to the gym. See our guide to what to wear for padel for more.
  • Water, and a towel if you're playing indoors in summer.
  • A racket, or money to hire one. Many clubs rent rackets for a few pounds. One UK club chain charges £5, with a £10 deposit.
  • Balls: check whether the club supplies them. If not, buy a tube at reception and take turns with your group next time.

Leave the watch and jewellery in your bag. And when you pick up the racket, put the strap around your wrist. The FIP rules make the wrist cord compulsory, and the LTA's first-session advice includes it for a simple reason: it stops a slipping racket from hitting your partner.

First time playing padel: what happens in the first hour

  1. Arrive a few minutes early. Change your shoes, collect your racket and find the court. Court slots run to time, because the next group is waiting.
  2. Warm up gently. Rally softly with the pair opposite, then try a few volleys at the net. Feed balls the other person can reach. Nobody smashes in the warm-up.
  3. Get used to the racket. The Padel School suggests spending your first session simply getting used to the racket's weight and the way the ball comes off it. It's solid and shorter than a tennis racket, so contact happens closer to your hand.
  4. Meet the glass. The first ball that bounces and comes off the back wall will surprise you. Let it come off, wait for it to drop, then play it. It's the single skill that makes padel feel like padel.
  5. Try some points. Serve underarm: bounce the ball behind the service line and hit it at or below waist height, diagonally into the opposite box. You get two attempts. Keep score loosely or not at all.

That's genuinely enough rules for day one. The LTA's own advice is not to worry about the rules on your first game and to concentrate on enjoying the rallies with your partner. When you want the full picture, read how to play padel.

Padel for beginners: what to focus on

Your first session isn't about winning. The Padel School frames it as a chance to learn the game and find out whether you enjoy it. Give yourself three small goals:

  • Keep the ball in play. The LTA points out that most beginner points are won through the other side's mistakes. One more ball over the net is your best shot.
  • Swing slower than you think. Hard hits fly into the glass and come back as easy balls for the opponents. Less power, more control.
  • Talk to your partner. Call "mine" or "yours" early. It's a doubles game, and the conversation is half the fun.

Beginner etiquette: how to be a good fourth

Padel is a small, social game, and a few habits go a long way. The FIP rules themselves ban hitting the ball at opponents when it isn't in play and treat unsportsmanlike behaviour severely. The rest is courtesy:

Do

Wait until the receiver is ready before you serve. Roll or gently tap loose balls back to the server. Own up straight away if the ball touched you or bounced twice. Give the other side the benefit of the doubt on close calls. Tap rackets at the net at the end.

Don't

Smash in the warm-up, launch balls across the court between points, celebrate the other pair's mistakes, or run over into the next session's time.

One rule surprises beginners: if the opponents' shot touches your body or clothing rather than your racket, you lose the point. The rules make no exception for a ball that looked to be going out, and good players call it on themselves.

After your first session

Most people walk off wanting to book the next one. To keep improving quickly, play regularly with people at a similar level, consider a few padel lessons early on before habits set in, and practise wall play whenever you can. Expect your legs to feel it the next day: padel is lots of short sprints and side steps, even at a gentle pace.

Frequently asked questions

Is padel easy for beginners?

It's one of the easier racket sports to start. The LTA says the smaller court, underarm serve and slower ball make it generally easier to learn than tennis, and most beginners have rallies in their first session.

Do I need my own racket for my first padel game?

No. Many clubs hire rackets for a small fee, and intro sessions often include them. Play a few times before you buy.

Can I play padel on my own?

Yes. You can join an open match or an intro class alone, and the app or coach will match you with other players. Padel itself is almost always played as doubles.

What should I wear for my first padel session?

Comfortable sportswear and court shoes with good grip and side support, with non-marking soles for indoor courts. Avoid running shoes if you can, and remove jewellery.

How long is a first padel session?

Usually an hour. Court bookings and intro clinics are commonly sold in 60-minute slots, but check your booking.
