Can you volley the serve in padel?

8 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

No: in padel you cannot volley the serve. The receiver must let the ball bounce in the receiving box and then return it before it bounces a second time (FIP Rule 8.1). If the serve touches either receiving player or their racket before it bounces, the point goes to the server (Rule 8.5). Here is how the return of serve works, including who receives and what happens when you are not ready.

The short answer

The serve must bounce before you hit it. Volley it, or let it touch you, your partner or either of your rackets before the bounce, and the server wins the point. Let it bounce twice, and the server also wins the point. You may play the serve off the glass after it has bounced.

What the rules say

FIP Rule 8.1 is the core rule. The receiver must let the serve bounce in the receiving box and must hit it before it bounces a second time. That rules out volleying the serve completely, and it also means a serve that bounces twice before you get to it is a point to the server, effectively an ace (Rules 8.1 and 13c).

FIP Rule 8.5 covers contact before the bounce. If the serve touches either receiving player or their racket before bouncing, the point goes to the server. That includes the receiver's partner, not just the receiver. The rule as written does not make an exception for a serve that looked as if it was heading long, so the safe habit is to get out of the way and let a bad serve land.

FIP Rules 8.2 and 8.3 set the receiving order. In the first game of each set, the receiving pair decides which of them receives first, and that player receives the first serve of every game in that set. The partners then receive alternately, and the order stays fixed for the set or tie-break. Rule 8.4 adds that if the order is changed by mistake, you carry on until the end of that game or tie-break and then go back to the correct order.

FIP Rules 9.2a and 6.10 deal with readiness. The server must wait until the receiver is ready, though the receiver has to adapt to the server's reasonable rhythm (Rule 6.10). If the serve is struck while the receiver is not ready, it is a let (Rule 9.2a), and no fault can be called on that serve. Once the receiver attempts a return, though, they can no longer claim they were not ready (Rule 6.11). A let on a first serve means the point is replayed with two serves (Rule 9.2b); a let on a second serve means only that second serve is repeated (Rule 9.2c).

Allowed

Letting the serve bounce in your box and hitting it straight away (Rule 8.1). Letting it bounce, come off the back or side glass, and then returning it before its second bounce (Rules 8.1 and 12).

Not allowed

Volleying the serve, or the serve touching either receiving player or their racket before it bounces: point to the server (Rules 8.1 and 8.5). Letting the serve bounce twice: point to the server (Rules 8.1 and 13c).

Real match situations

The fast serve at your body

The server hits a quick, low serve straight at you. You instinctively stick out your racket and block it before it bounces. Verdict: point to the server (Rule 8.5). Step aside or back and let the ball land first.

The serve that is clearly going long

The serve is flying towards the back of your box and looks long. You reach up and catch it, or it clips your shoulder. Verdict: under Rule 8.5 as written, a serve that touches either receiving player before bouncing is a point to the server. Do not touch a serve before it bounces, even if you think it is out. Let it land and call the fault.

Playing the serve off the back glass

The serve bounces deep in your box and you let it run onto the back glass, then hit it as it comes out. Verdict: perfectly legal. After the bounce, Rule 12 allows the ball to touch any part of the court; you just need to hit it before the second bounce. For many receivers this is the safest return against a deep serve. Our guide to the serve and return covers the technique.

The serve that dies in the corner

The serve skids off the side glass and bounces a second time before you can get your racket to it. Verdict: point to the server (Rules 8.1 and 13c).

The wrong partner receives

In the first game of the set, your partner received on the right. In game three, you both drift to the wrong sides and you receive from the right by mistake. Verdict: Rule 8.4 says you finish that game as you are and then go back to the correct order. Points already played stand.

You are not ready

You are still adjusting your grip and have not looked up when the server strikes the ball. If you do not try to play it, the serve is a let (Rule 9.2a). If you try to return it, you can no longer claim you were not ready (Rule 6.11). Signal clearly instead, for example by raising your hand, and the server must wait (Rule 6.10).

Common misconceptions

  • "You can volley the serve if it is a good serve." Never. Rule 8.1 requires the bounce every time.
  • "If it was going out, it is a fault even if it hit me." Rule 8.5 gives the point to the server when the serve touches either receiving player before bouncing; it does not make an exception for serves that looked long.
  • "The receiver's partner can take the serve if it comes near them." No. If the serve touches either receiving player or their racket before it bounces, it is the server's point (Rule 8.5). After the bounce, Rule 8.1 makes the receiver the player who returns it, and the receiving order in Rules 8.2 and 8.3 fixes who that is. The rulebook does not set out an explicit penalty for the partner playing it instead; at clubs it is treated as a point to the server, but that is convention, not an FIP rule.
  • "You must return the serve before it reaches the glass." No. Once it has bounced, glass contact is fine (Rule 12).
  • "The receiver has to stand inside the receiving box." No. Rule 3 lets the receiver, and both partners, stand anywhere on their own side of the court. "Diagonally opposite" only decides which opponent receives the serve; it is not a position rule.

Where to stand to receive

Rule 3 lets the receiver stand anywhere on their side of the court; it does not fix a spot. In practice most receivers stand a metre or two behind the service line, towards the side glass of their box, so they can step in to a short serve or let a deep one come off the glass. Standing too close makes the Rule 8.5 mistake more likely, because a fast serve reaches you before it has bounced. For more on where both players stand, see positioning in doubles.

Social games vs official matches

In official matches the umpire judges whether the serve bounced before touching the receiver. In social games, the receiver should own the call: if a serve hit you on the full, it is the server's point. Readiness is where social games get messy. Agree a simple habit before you start: the server waits until the receiver is in position and looking, and the receiver does not delay beyond a reasonable rhythm. Remember that play should be continuous, with no more than 20 seconds between points (Rule 2).

Returning well, legally

  • Back up for the fast serve. More distance gives the ball time to bounce and you time to read it.
  • Use the glass. A deep serve off the back glass is easier to control than one you rush to hit early.
  • Aim low or lob. A low return at the server's feet or a lob over the net player are the two standard replies.

Frequently asked questions

Does the serve have to bounce in padel?

Yes, twice over: the server bounces the ball before striking it (Rule 6.2), and the receiver must let the serve bounce in the box before hitting it (Rule 8.1).

What happens if the serve hits the receiver?

If it touches either receiving player or their racket before bouncing, the point goes to the server (Rule 8.5).

Can you return the serve off the glass?

Yes. After the serve has bounced in your box, it may touch the glass and you return it before the second bounce (Rules 8.1 and 12). If it touches the metal fence, the serve is a fault (Rule 7e), so you would not need to play it.

Who receives first in padel?

The receiving pair decides in the first game of each set, and that player receives the first serve of every game in the set (Rule 8.2). Partners then alternate (Rule 8.3).

Is a serve that bounces twice an ace?

Effectively, yes. The receiver must hit it before the second bounce, so the server wins the point (Rules 8.1 and 13c).
