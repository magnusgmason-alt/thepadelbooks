The padel forehand: technique, grip and slice

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

The padel forehand is a compact, side-on stroke: turn your shoulders early, take the racket back only to about hip height, and swing through the ball in front of your body with a continental grip and a firm wrist. Power comes from the turn and the weight transfer, not the arm. Here is how to hit it, how to add slice and what changes on the forehand volley.

What makes a good padel forehand

If you've played tennis, your forehand is probably your biggest weapon. In padel it needs to calm down. A big tennis forehand from the back usually lands deep, hits the back glass and comes straight back to the opponents at a comfortable height. The padel forehand is about control: low over the net, deep or at the feet, and with enough variety that the opponents can't guess between a drive, a lob or a chiquita.

LTA Padel sums up the difference: a short backswing and a push through the back of the ball, rather than a full tennis swing, with contact out in front of your body.

Padel forehand grip

Most coaching guides teach the forehand with the continental grip, the same hammer grip you use for volleys and overheads. It keeps the face slightly open, which suits slice, volleys and wall play, and you never need to change grip when the ball comes back to the net. Some coaches allow a slightly more eastern grip for forehands from the back when you have time, but beginners do best learning on continental. See the padel grip guide for how to find it.

Padel forehand technique, step by step

Instructions for a right-hander. Left-handers mirror them.

  1. Ready position. Racket up in front of you, free hand supporting the throat, knees soft, weight on the balls of your feet. Keep the feet moving.
  2. Turn side-on early. Turn your shoulders so your left shoulder points at the ball and your feet face the side wall. NOX calls the shoulder turn the most important thing in padel. The Padel School adds that you should take the racket back "way before" you play the shot.
  3. Short backswing. Take the racket back to about hip or waist height and no further, keeping it in your peripheral vision.
  4. Adjust with small steps so the ball will be at a comfortable distance. Bend your knees for low balls.
  5. Weight back to front. Step towards the ball and transfer your weight onto the front foot as you swing.
  6. Contact in front. Meet the ball in front of your body or roughly level with your front hip, at a comfortable distance from your body. Babolat suggests taking it after it has peaked off the bounce, on its way down.
  7. Swing through, not round. Finish towards your target rather than wrapping the racket round your shoulder. This keeps the ball low and deep.
  8. Recover to the ready position and move with your partner.

Sources describe the contact point slightly differently, from "level with the front foot" to "at the side of your body at hip height". The common ground is that it should never be late or cramped, and that you should meet the ball with your weight moving forwards.

Flat, slice or topspin forehand?

NOX describes three versions of the forehand, and the difference is mostly the racket angle and swing path:

ForehandRacket and swingFinishWhen
FlatFace upright, swing through the middle of the ballAround shoulder heightBalls around waist height, steady rallying
Slice (cut)Face tilted slightly back, swing slightly downwardBetween hip and shoulderBalls above the waist; keeps the ball low so you can move to the net
TopspinFace tilted slightly forward, swing upwardLower, below the hip in NOX's descriptionLow balls, dipping passes at net players' feet

Padel Browser recommends that most of your forehands be flat with a touch of slice: controlled, low and deep. Topspin is a tool, not a default: overuse it and your margin for error shrinks.

Padel forehand slice: don't chop

The Padel School's advice on sliced forehands is to hit through the ball rather than chop down on it. A steep chop is hard to time and produces a weak ball. On higher balls you can add a little more slice, but keep the swing travelling forwards.

Padel forehand wrist: keep it quiet

The wrist is where many forehands go wrong. Coaching guides agree that you shouldn't flick the wrist to generate pace. Power comes from the shoulder turn and the weight transfer, and the wrist stays fairly firm through contact. A flicky wrist makes the face angle unpredictable, sends balls long and puts extra load on the forearm, which is one reason it's linked to padel elbow.

A firm wrist doesn't mean a tight grip. Keep the hand relaxed between shots and firm up only as you hit.

The padel forehand volley

At the net the forehand becomes a short punch. The essentials, which our volley guide covers in detail:

  • Continental grip, unchanged from the groundstroke.
  • Racket up and in front, around chest height, ready.
  • Shoulder turn as the backswing; the racket shouldn't go back further than your shoulder.
  • Contact in front with the face slightly open and a short, high-to-low follow-through.
  • No wrist flick. Power comes from the incoming ball and your body.

The forehand off the back glass

Many padel forehands are played after the ball has come off the back glass. The Padel School's advice is to move with the ball and turn early. Padel Browser adds: don't swing early, give yourself space rather than crowding the wall, and step into the shot as the ball comes out. Our guide to the back glass has the full method.

Use the service line as a decision line

LTA Padel suggests a simple rule: if the ball bounces in front of the service line, play it before the glass. If it bounces beyond it, let it come off the glass first.

Common padel forehand mistakes

  1. Over-swinging, especially for tennis players. Swing harder than needed and you lose control.
  2. Late preparation, which forces a rushed swing.
  3. Open stance and arm only. The Padel School lists hitting from an open stance with only the arm as a key error. Turn side-on and use your body.
  4. Racket too far back, out of sight, which makes the timing harder.
  5. Hitting with the wrist.
  6. Moving the feet during the swing. Plant, hit, then reposition.
  7. Looking up before contact.

A simple forehand practice routine

Have a partner feed from the net. Hit ten flat forehands cross-court past the service line, then ten sliced forehands low over the net, then ten lobs from the same set-up so the opponent can't read your shot. Finish with ten balls fed off the back glass. Keep score of how many land in the target zone, not how hard they go.

Frequently asked questions

What grip should I use for a padel forehand?

Most coaching guides teach the continental grip for the forehand, the same grip you use for volleys. Some allow a slightly more eastern grip on forehands from the back once your technique is settled.

Should I use my wrist on a padel forehand?

No flicking. Keep the wrist fairly firm through contact and get your power from the shoulder turn and weight transfer.

Should padel forehands have topspin?

Mostly no. Coaching guides recommend flat forehands with a touch of slice for most balls, and topspin occasionally, for example to dip the ball at a net player's feet.

How big should the backswing be?

Short. Take the racket back to about hip or waist height, no further, and keep it in your peripheral vision.

Why do my forehands keep hitting the back glass?

Usually too much swing and too little control, or contact too late. Shorten the backswing, swing through towards the target and add a touch of slice to bring the ball down.
