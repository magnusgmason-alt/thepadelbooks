How to choose a padel partner who makes you better

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

The right padel partner is someone whose game fits around yours: a similar level, a style that complements yours, a side that suits you both, and the same idea of how often and how seriously you want to play. This guide covers what to look for, where to find a good padel partner and how to test a partnership with a few trial matches before you commit to a league or tournament.

What makes a good padel partner

Padel is almost always doubles, so your partner shapes every point you play. A good partnership is rarely two copies of the same player. In our experience, and in the coaching sources we checked, five things matter most:

  1. Side fit. One of you is comfortable on the left, the other on the right.
  2. Complementary styles. Someone who builds the point and someone who can finish it.
  3. A similar level. Close enough that the opponents can't simply target one of you all match.
  4. Shared goals. The same answer to "how often, and how competitive?"
  5. Temperament. Calm after errors, open to talking tactics, no blame.

Padelspeed's partner guide puts it neatly: level matters, but style compatibility matters just as much.

Start with an honest look at your own game

You can't pick a complementary partner until you know what you are. Answer these honestly, or ask someone who has played with you a lot.

QuestionIf yes, you lean towards
Do you enjoy long rallies, lobbing and defending off the glass?The builder, often on the right
Is your overhead your best shot, and do you like finishing points?The finisher, often on the left
Do you make few errors but rarely hit winners?The builder; you need a partner who can finish
Do you hit winners but also plenty of errors?The finisher; you need a steady partner
Are you left-handed?Usually the right side, where your forehand covers the middle

Left side or right side: getting the fit right

For two right-handers, the traditional split is a steadier right-side player who builds the point and a more aggressive left-side player whose forehand and overhead cover the middle. The Padel School adds two important caveats. First, don't choose your side too early: get plenty of experience on both, because playing one side teaches you what your partner on the other needs. Second, it matters more that the pair learns to move together than who stands where.

Left-handers are a special case. The Padel School notes they usually play the right side, where their forehand faces the middle. A right-hander and a left-hander then have both forehands in the centre, which is strong but needs a clear agreement on who takes the middle and the lobs. Padelspeed describes the same pattern: common strong pairings are an aggressive net player with a patient, consistent partner, or a reliable defender with a finisher. Two finishers tend to go for too much; two builders struggle to end points against good defence.

How big a level gap is too big?

A small gap is healthy. The stronger player stretches the weaker one, and the weaker player learns quickly. A big gap creates a predictable match: the opponents play every ball to the weaker player, who gets few chances to improve, while the stronger one gets frustrated.

If you use a rating app, the gap also affects your rating. On Playtomic, your partner's level is one of the factors in how much your own level moves after a competitive match. For league and tournament play, look for a partner within roughly one stage of you on your club's scale. Our guide to padel levels explains the main scales, and if you do play with a stronger or weaker partner, read how to play with a stronger or weaker partner.

Personality and commitment

More partnerships end over expectations than over forehands. Before you commit, agree on:

  • How often. Once a week? Three times? Training as well as matches?
  • How serious. Is this for fun, for the club league or for ranking points?
  • How you handle losing. Do you talk it through or need a day?
  • Who decides tactics. One of you, both, or by side?

Padelspeed lists three red flags worth taking seriously: a partner who blames others, resists tactical discussion, or competes with you instead of working with you. Emotional stability matters, because a partner who stays calm after errors keeps the pair organised. The Padel School makes a related point: harmony means making decisions together, not always getting along.

Where to find a padel partner

  • Americanos and social nights. You play with several partners in one evening, which is the fastest way to meet people at your level. See our padel americano guide.
  • Box leagues. You rotate partners within a small group of similar players.
  • Group lessons and clinics. Players at your level, with the same goal of improving.
  • Booking apps. Open matches filtered by level let you try partners without commitment.

Our guide on how to find padel players covers each route in detail.

Run a three-match trial

A friendly hit tells you whether you get on. A match tells you whether you play well together. Padelspeed's advice is to test pairings in real competitive matches, because they reveal much more. A simple trial:

  1. Match one: a relaxed game. Play your agreed sides. Notice whether you move as a pair and whether you call the middle.
  2. Match two: a competitive game against similar opponents. Use golden point. Watch how each of you handles a lost lead.
  3. Match three: against a slightly stronger pair. Pressure shows how you communicate when things go wrong.
  4. Debrief honestly. Ask three questions: Did we cover the middle? Did we trust each other's calls? Did we enjoy it?

If two of the three answers are yes, the partnership is worth building. When a partnership isn't working

Talk about it early and specifically: "We keep losing the middle" is a problem you can fix together; "you're not good enough" isn't. Try switching sides for a few matches before you give up. And remember that you don't need just one partner. Many club players have a regular league partner and play socially with others, which keeps their game varied and helps them avoid the plateau of always playing the same people.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find a good padel partner?

Play americanos, social nights, box leagues and open matches on booking apps, where you meet several players at your level. Once you find someone you click with, test the partnership in a few competitive matches.

Should my padel partner be better than me?

Slightly better is fine and helps you learn. A large gap usually means the opponents target the weaker player all match, which isn't much fun for either of you.

Which side should I play with a new partner?

Usually the steadier player takes the right and the more aggressive one the left, if you are both right-handed. A left-hander usually plays the right. Try both sides before you settle.

What makes a padel partnership work?

Complementary styles, a similar level, the same goals and good communication. Coaches stress that moving together as a pair matters more than who plays which side.

Can I have more than one padel partner?

Yes. Many players have a regular partner for leagues and tournaments and play with others socially, which keeps their game varied.
