Padel eye protection: what the research says and what to wear

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel eye protection is worth taking seriously: eye specialists in Sweden have called padel a potential high-risk sport for eye injuries and recommend protective goggles, and a 2026 survey of Italian players found about one in ten reported having had an eye injury. Most padel eye injuries are mild, but some cause lasting damage. Here's what the research says, how injuries happen, what to wear and what to do if a ball hits your eye.

This article is general information, not medical advice. If you've been hit in the eye and your vision has changed, go to A&E or call emergency services now.

What the research on padel eye injuries shows

The Swedish warning (2021)

In June 2021, ophthalmologists Gunnar Jakobsson and Catharina Thörnland, from the University of Gothenburg and Sahlgrenska University Hospital, described padel as a potential high-risk sport for eye injuries in Läkartidningen, the journal of the Swedish Medical Association. They reported that several Swedish eye clinics were seeing more padel-related eye injuries, and described three cases with moderate injuries that all needed surgery.

They noted that most padel eye injuries are mild, such as minor bleeding inside the eye that doesn't need surgery, but that some serious cases have caused lasting visual impairment. Their recommendation was to wear protective goggles, a safety visor or a face shield, which they said can reduce the risk of eye damage by up to 90% when used correctly.

The Italian player survey (2026)

A survey published in the journal Eye in 2026 by Aiello and colleagues analysed responses from 583 active padel players in Italy. Of those, 60 (10.3%) reported at least one eye injury. The most common self-reported injuries were corneal abrasions and bleeding on the surface of the eye, but some players reported retinal detachment, and six of the injured players had needed eye surgery. The ball caused 92% of injuries and the player's own racket about 8%.

Playing more matches per week was the only factor that independently predicted injury. Attitudes to protection were lukewarm: fewer than one in ten respondents strongly agreed that protective goggles are important. The authors call their findings hypothesis-generating, because the diagnoses were self-reported and the sample wasn't random, so don't read 10% as a precise injury rate. It does show eye injuries in padel aren't rare curiosities.

How padel eye injuries happen

The Gothenburg team gave three reasons padel is risky for the eyes:

  • Size. The ball is about the size of the opening of the eye socket, so it can hit the eye itself rather than the bone around it.
  • Speed. A smash can reach about 130 km/h.
  • Rebounds. Balls coming off the glass are hard to judge, which likely raises the risk.

On court, that translates into a few familiar situations:

  • At the net facing a smash or a hard drive. You have the least time to react. Keep your racket up in front of your chest, about two metres from the net, as in our volley guide, so it's between you and the ball.
  • Balls off the glass and the corners. Give yourself room and watch the rebound.
  • Your own and your partner's racket. In the Italian survey, about one injury in twelve came from the player's own racket. Calling "mine" or "yours" early prevents racket clashes in the middle.

Should you wear protective glasses for padel?

The Swedish ophthalmologists recommend it. They point to floorball, which caused the most eye injuries in Sweden during its rapid growth in the 1990s, and where mandatory goggles for children and juniors, plus strong recommendations for adults, cut eye injuries.

In practice, protective glasses make most sense if you:

  • play several times a week (the survey's strongest risk factor);
  • spend a lot of time at the net;
  • play with or against hard hitters;
  • have had an eye injury or eye surgery, or have vision in only one good eye;
  • are coaching or playing with children.

What to look for in padel eye protection

There's no eye protection standard written specifically for padel that we could find. The closest standards are for other racket sports:

  • ASTM F803 is the US standard specification for eye protectors for selected sports, and its scope includes racket sports in general. It doesn't name padel.
  • ISO 18527-2:2021 covers eye protectors for squash and racquetball, and also applies to sports with similar hazards and no greater risks.
  • EN 166 is often printed on safety glasses, but it's a general standard for eye protectors used in industry, laboratories, education and DIY. It isn't a sports impact standard, so don't assume EN 166 glasses are designed for a padel ball.

Prevent Blindness, a US eye-health charity, recommends polycarbonate lenses in protectors that meet the relevant sport standard, describing polycarbonate as the most impact-resistant lens material. It also says ordinary prescription glasses, sunglasses and even occupational safety glasses don't give adequate protection for sport.

Quick checklist for padel glasses

Polycarbonate lenses; a sports standard such as ASTM F803 for racket sports, or ISO 18527-2, on the label or spec sheet; a wraparound fit that stays put when you sprint and turn; anti-fog treatment; prescription inserts or prescription lenses if you need them.

If you wear glasses, ask an optician about prescription sports eyewear rather than playing in your everyday frames.

Kids' eye safety in padel

Children have less experience reading rebounds and quicker shots, and many play in mixed-age groups. The floorball example is the strongest argument for protective eyewear for juniors: it was mandatory goggles for children and juniors that helped cut eye injuries in that sport. For young players, also:

  • keep beginners at the back of the court during feeding drills;
  • avoid adults smashing at children at the net;
  • teach the ready position with the racket up from the first lesson.

What to do if a padel ball hits your eye

Stop playing. Then follow the NHS advice.

Go to A&E or call 999 if:

  • your sight has changed: blurred or double vision, loss of vision, flashing lights, halos or shadows;
  • you can see blood in the eye, or the pupil looks an unusual shape;
  • you have severe eye pain, sensitivity to light, or you can't move the eye or keep it open;
  • you feel sick or are being sick;
  • something hit your eye at high speed. A smashed padel ball counts.

Don't drive yourself; ask someone to take you or call an ambulance.

For a black eye with no vision problems, the NHS advises holding an ice pack or bag of frozen peas wrapped in a cloth against the area around the eye for 10 to 20 minutes at a time, regularly over the first one to two days. Don't press or rub the eye, and don't put ice directly on the skin. Get an urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111 if you develop a headache that won't go away or blurry vision, or if the injury isn't improving after 24 hours.

If you're unsure whether an eye injury is serious, get it checked rather than playing on.

Frequently asked questions

Is padel dangerous for your eyes?

It carries a real risk. Swedish ophthalmologists called padel a potential high-risk sport for eye injuries in 2021, and in a 2026 survey of Italian players about one in ten reported at least one eye injury. Most injuries are mild, but some cause lasting damage.

Should I wear goggles for padel?

Eye specialists recommend it, and they say correctly used protective eyewear can cut the risk of eye damage by up to 90%. It's especially worth it if you play often, spend a lot of time at the net, or are a junior.

Are sunglasses enough protection for padel?

No. Prevent Blindness says regular prescription glasses, sunglasses and even occupational safety glasses don't give adequate sports protection. Look for polycarbonate sports eyewear tested to a racket-sport standard.

What standard should padel glasses meet?

There's no padel-specific standard. Look for eyewear tested to ASTM F803 for racket sports or ISO 18527-2, which covers squash and racquetball and sports with similar hazards. EN 166 is a general safety-glasses standard, not a sports one.

What should I do if a padel ball hits my eye?

Stop playing. Go to A&E or call emergency services if your vision changes, you see blood in the eye, the pain is severe or the ball hit at high speed. For a simple black eye, use a wrapped cold pack for 10 to 20 minutes at a time and get checked if anything gets worse.
