Padel insoles: which type you need and how to fit them

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel insoles are worth trying if your feet ache after a session, your shoes feel flat, or you want a better fit inside your padel shoes, but they're a comfort and fit tool, not a cure. Below is what each type does, what the research says about heel and knee pain, how to fit an insole without ruining a good shoe, and when to stop experimenting and see a podiatrist.

This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have pain that is getting worse, numbness or a foot condition such as diabetes, speak to a GP or podiatrist before buying anything.

What padel insoles can and can't do

Padel is played in short bursts: split steps, side shuffles, lunges and quick stops, usually on sand-filled artificial grass. Your shoe does most of the work of holding your foot in place and gripping the court. An insole sits inside the shoe and changes three things: how much cushioning is under your foot, how the arch and heel are supported, and how much room is left inside the shoe.

That makes insoles useful for comfort, for filling a shoe that's slightly too roomy, and for some people with heel pain. What they don't reliably do is prevent injuries on their own. A 2016 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine, which pooled 11 trials of foot orthoses and 7 of shock-absorbing insoles, found that orthoses were linked to a lower overall injury risk, mainly fewer stress fractures, but shock-absorbing insoles didn't reduce the risk of any injury type. Much of the evidence came from military recruits, so it doesn't map neatly onto a weekly padel game. Treat any marketing that promises "injury prevention" with care.

The main types of insole

TypeWhat it isWho it tends to suit
Cushioning insoleA soft, full-length layer of foam or gelPlayers whose shoes feel hard or flat, or who play on firm, low-sand courts
Arch-support insoleA firmer insole with a shaped arch and a heel cupPlayers who want more structure under the midfoot
Heel cup or heel padA small insert under the heel onlyHeel soreness; leaves the front of the shoe unchanged
Prefabricated orthoticAn off-the-shelf, firm, contoured deviceA step up from a retail insole, often suggested by a clinician
Custom orthoticMade from a scan or cast of your foot after an assessmentSpecific problems a podiatrist has assessed

Many padel shoes come with a removable sockliner. Swapping it for an aftermarket insole is usually straightforward, as long as the new one doesn't make the shoe too tight.

Custom orthotics vs off-the-shelf insoles

Custom orthotics cost far more than retail insoles, so it's fair to ask whether they work better. For plantar heel pain, the best-known trial says: not by much. In a 2006 randomised trial by Landorf and colleagues, 135 people with plantar fasciitis were given a prefabricated firm orthosis, a custom-made rigid orthosis, or a soft sham insert. Pain improved in all three groups, with no difference between them. Both real orthoses gave a small improvement in function at three months, which had disappeared by twelve months.

That doesn't mean custom devices are never worth it. A podiatrist may recommend one for a specific foot shape or a problem that hasn't settled. But for most club players with tired or sore feet, a decent off-the-shelf insole is a sensible first step, and a custom device is something to discuss after an assessment, not something to buy first.

Insoles for heel pain and plantar fasciitis

Heel pain that's worst with your first steps in the morning is a common reason people look for insoles. NHS 111 Wales's self-care advice for plantar fasciitis includes using soft insoles or heel pads in your shoes, wearing cushioned, supportive footwear, resting and raising the foot, and gentle stretches for the sole of the foot and the heel. If that hasn't helped after two weeks, or you have tingling or loss of feeling in the foot, it advises seeing a GP, who may refer you to a physiotherapist or podiatrist.

For padel, that usually means a heel pad or cushioned insole in your court shoes, a bit less court time while the pain settles, and a proper warm-up before you start sprinting. Our calf strain guide covers calf mobility, which many players with heel pain also need to work on.

Insoles for knee pain

Knee pain is a harder case. Evidence for insoles here is weak. The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners' osteoarthritis guideline suggests not offering lateral wedge insoles for medial knee osteoarthritis, and says it can't recommend for or against shock-absorbing insoles or arch supports because the evidence is very low quality. It does say clinicians may consider advising shock-absorbing footwear.

So if your knees hurt after padel, don't expect an insole to fix it. Look at load (how often and how long you play), strength, and how you move on court first. Our padel knee pain guide goes through the common causes and when to get it checked.

Choosing padel insoles: a quick guide by need

  • Feet ache after long sessions: try a full-length cushioning insole first.
  • Heel sore in the morning: a heel pad or cushioned insole, plus the NHS self-care steps above.
  • Shoe feels slightly loose: a slightly thicker insole can take up volume, but check the heel still doesn't slip on side steps.
  • You already wear prescribed orthotics: take them to the shop when you buy padel shoes and try the shoes on with them in.
  • Sharp, worsening or one-sided pain: skip the shopping and get it assessed.

How to fit insoles in padel shoes

An NHS podiatry service's fitting guide uses a simple tracing method, which works well for most trim-to-fit insoles:

  1. Take out the original sockliner, if your shoe has a removable one.
  2. Use it as a template. Put the new insole underneath the old sockliner, lined up at the heel and the toes.
  3. Trace and cut. Draw round the old liner, cut along the line, then try the insole in the shoe. Trim a little more if it buckles at the toe.
  4. Seat the heel. Press the insole down firmly at the heel with your thumbs so it sits flat in the heel cup.
  5. Test on the move. Lace up with your playing socks and do a few side steps, lunges and stops. Your heel shouldn't lift and your toes shouldn't hit the front.

Two practical points. Don't stack a new insole on top of the original liner unless the shoe has room to spare, because a cramped forefoot is uncomfortable and can rub. And bring your insoles when you buy new shoes, so you size the shoe for the foot plus the insole.

What shoes and insoles for padel?

Start with the shoe, not the insole. A padel or clay-court tennis shoe with good side support and a sole that suits your court matters more than anything you put inside it. The Padel School recommends shoes built for racket sports over running shoes, which are designed mainly for forward movement. Our padel shoes guide covers fit and support, and our guide to padel shoe soles explains herringbone, omni and hybrid patterns. Once the shoe is right, add an insole only if you need more cushioning, a better fit, or something a clinician has suggested.

Good footwork reduces load too. Players who arrive balanced and on time take fewer lunging, braking steps. The Padel Drill Book has footwork drills you can do with a partner or alone, and The Padel Playbook: Beginner & Intermediate explains where to stand so you're not always scrambling.

When to see a podiatrist or GP

Stop experimenting with insoles and get advice if:

  • pain hasn't improved after two weeks of self-care;
  • you have tingling, numbness or loss of feeling in your foot;
  • you have diabetes or poor circulation, where foot problems can be more serious;
  • you can't put weight on the foot, or there's sudden swelling after a twist or a pop;
  • pain wakes you at night or is getting steadily worse.

A podiatrist can assess your feet, look at your shoes and decide whether you need an insole, an orthotic, or something else entirely.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best insoles for padel?

There's no single best insole. For general comfort, a full-length cushioning insole that fits your padel shoe without cramping it is a good start. For heel soreness, a heel pad or cushioned insole is a common first step. If you have a diagnosed foot problem, ask a podiatrist what suits you.

Are custom orthotics better than off-the-shelf insoles?

Not necessarily. In a randomised trial of people with plantar fasciitis, custom and prefabricated orthoses gave similar results to each other, and pain improved just as much with a soft sham insert. Custom devices can make sense after a podiatrist's assessment, but they shouldn't be your first purchase.

Can insoles stop knee pain from padel?

Don't count on it. Guidelines for knee osteoarthritis rate the evidence for shock-absorbing insoles and arch supports as very low quality and advise against lateral wedge insoles for medial knee arthritis. Load, strength and movement matter more. See a professional if knee pain persists.

Should I remove the original insole from my padel shoe?

Usually, yes. Take out the original sockliner and use it as a template to trim the new insole. Stacking both can make the shoe too tight, especially at the toes.

Do shock-absorbing insoles prevent injuries?

A meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found that shock-absorbing insoles didn't reduce the risk of any injury type. Foot orthoses were linked to fewer injuries, mainly stress fractures, though most of that evidence came from military trainees.
