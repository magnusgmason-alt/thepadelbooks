Padel balls: specs, types and how long they last

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 30 Oct 2026

Padel balls look like tennis balls, but under the FIP rules they're a fraction smaller, with their own limits on weight, bounce and internal pressure. The difference is small on paper and obvious on court, especially off the glass. This guide gives you the official specs, compares them with tennis balls, explains pressurised, pressureless and altitude balls, and shows you how long a can really lasts.

Official padel ball specifications

The FIP rules define exactly what counts as a padel ball. It must have a uniform outer surface in white, yellow or any other colour that contrasts with the court, and it must meet these limits:

SpecificationPadel ball (FIP)Tennis ball (ITF Type 2, medium)
Diameter6.35 to 6.77 cm6.54 to 6.86 cm
Weight56.0 to 59.4 g56.0 to 59.4 g
Bounce from 2.54 m onto a hard surface135 to 145 cm135 to 147 cm
Internal pressure4.6 to 5.2 kg per 2.54 cm²Not specified as a pressure; tested by deformation

The FIP's certification document also sets deformation limits (how much the ball squashes under load) that match the ITF's medium tennis ball. So the two balls are close cousins. The padel ball's upper size limit is smaller, and its maximum bounce is slightly lower.

Padel ball vs tennis ball: the real differences

  • Size: a padel ball can be up to 6.77 cm across; a tennis ball can be up to 6.86 cm. The ranges overlap, but padel balls run slightly smaller.
  • Pressure: padel balls are generally described as slightly less pressurised than tennis balls, which is why they feel a little slower and bounce a little lower. The Padel School and Padel Market both make this point. For reference, Wikipedia gives a pressurised tennis ball's internal pressure as about 12 psi above the surrounding air.
  • Bounce: the FIP caps the bounce at 145 cm; the ITF allows up to 147 cm.
  • Weight: identical limits, 56.0 to 59.4 g.

On court, those small numbers add up. A slightly slower, lower ball makes rallies longer and gives you time to let it come off the glass. That's central to how padel is played. See our guide to playing off the back glass.

Can you use tennis balls for padel?

For a knock-about, you can, but you shouldn't make a habit of it. Tennis balls are livelier, so they come off the walls faster and higher, which changes the timing of almost every shot. Padel Market makes the same point from the other side: padel balls have lower internal pressure, so they bounce lower and rallies last longer. Tennis balls also aren't legal for competition: the FIP says only FIP-approved balls may be used in its tournaments and other recognised competitions.

Fine

A casual game where nobody has padel balls and you just want to hit.

Not fine

Tournaments, leagues or practice where you want your timing off the glass to transfer to real matches.

Pressurised vs pressureless padel balls

Almost every padel ball you'll buy in a tube is pressurised. Padel Market describes them as giving a lively, dynamic bounce. They're what clubs and competitions use, but they lose pressure with play and once the tube is opened.

Pressureless balls are more durable and consistent, which makes them popular for coaching baskets, lessons and ball machines. In the FIP's technical specifications, a pressureless ball must have an internal pressure of no more than 7 kPa and is linked to high-altitude play above 1,219 m, after being acclimatised at that altitude for at least 60 days. Only FIP-certified balls may be used in FIP tournaments and recognised competitions, and Padel Browser warns that ordinary coaching-basket balls aren't legal for sanctioned play, so keep them for practice.

High-altitude padel balls

Thinner air makes balls fly faster and bounce higher. The current FIP rulebook (December 2025 edition) allows a different ball when playing more than 1,000 m above sea level; the FIP's older 2024 ball specification put the threshold at 800 m, and some retailers still quote that figure. The high-altitude ball must have a bounce of more than 121.92 cm and less than 135 cm. The FIP's certification document also specifies a pressurised high-altitude ball for courts at 1,219 m. If you play in a high mountain town, look for balls labelled for altitude.

How long do padel balls last?

Less time than most people think. Estimates vary because players judge a dead ball differently:

  • Padel Market: a high-end pressurised ball usually performs well for two to four matches before the bounce drops and the felt lifts.
  • Padel Browser: for social play, a can stays playable for roughly four to six hours of court time, across two or three sessions. After 90 minutes of hard competitive doubles, a new can is noticeably past its best.
  • Competitive players usually open a new tube for every match, Padel Market says.

For context, the FIP's durability test simulates nine matches and allows a ball to lose at most 4 cm of bounce and 0.4 g of mass. That's a lab standard for approval, not a promise of how long a ball feels fresh.

Signs your padel balls are dead

  1. The drop test. Drop an old ball and a new ball side by side from the same height. If the old one bounces visibly lower, it's done.
  2. The squeeze. A dead ball feels soft through the middle.
  3. The sound. A live ball cracks off the racket; a dead one thuds.
  4. The glass. Wall shots start dying short, because dead balls lose rebound off the glass first.
  5. The felt. Bald, matted felt skids low and grips the racket face less.

How to make padel balls last longer

  • Open a new tube on the day you play, since pressure starts dropping as soon as the seal breaks.
  • Keep tubes out of hot cars. Padel Browser calls a hot car the fastest way to kill balls that have never been hit, because heat speeds up pressure loss.
  • Store them sealed, somewhere dry at a moderate temperature, as Padel Market advises.
  • Consider a pressurising tube if you play often. Padel Browser notes that a true pressuriser has a pump to restore pressure between sessions, while ordinary pick-up tubes just carry balls. Padel Market adds that a pressuriser can't fix worn felt.

How to choose the best padel balls

The best padel ball is a fresh, FIP-approved one that suits your conditions. A few pointers:

  • Check FIP approval if you play leagues or tournaments. Approval has to be renewed every two years.
  • Match speed to your level. Many brands make a faster ball and a more controlled version. Beginners and improvers usually find the slower, more controllable balls easier.
  • Think about altitude if your club sits high above sea level.
  • Buy multipacks if you play every week; balls are the item you'll replace most often.

Padel Market's 2026 guide names the Head Padel Pro+ and Pro S+, the Wilson Padel Premier, the Bullpadel Premium Pro and the Nox Nerbo Plus Speed among its picks. HEAD, Wilson and Bullpadel are also the brands The Padel School mentions for beginner-friendly balls. Frequently asked questions

Are padel balls the same as tennis balls?

No, though they're similar. Under FIP rules, padel balls are 6.35 to 6.77 cm across, slightly smaller than tennis balls, with a maximum bounce of 145 cm against 147 cm for a medium tennis ball. They're generally slightly less pressurised, so they play a little slower.

Can I use tennis balls to play padel?

For a casual hit, yes, but they bounce higher and come off the walls faster, which spoils your timing. Only FIP-approved padel balls are allowed in FIP tournaments and recognised competitions.

How long do padel balls last?

Roughly two to four matches for a high-end ball, or four to six hours of social play, depending on how hard you hit and how strict you are. Competitive players usually open a new tube every match.

What is a pressureless padel ball?

A ball with no added internal pressure (no more than 7 kPa under FIP specs). It lasts longer and is used mainly for coaching and ball machines, and in the FIP specs for high-altitude play.

What colour are padel balls?

Usually yellow, but the FIP allows white, yellow or any other colour that contrasts with the playing surface.
