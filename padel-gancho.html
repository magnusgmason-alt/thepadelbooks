The padel gancho: what it is and how to hit it

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

The padel gancho is an overhead you play when a lob drifts over your non-dominant shoulder (the left shoulder for a right-hander) and there is no time to get round it for a bandeja: arm straight, contact high above your head, a hooking swing down through the ball into the far glass. Gancho is Spanish for 'hook'. Coaches don't all use the word the same way, so we start by pinning down what it means here, then take you through it step by step.

What is a gancho in padel?

The most specific coaching definitions agree. The Padel School describes the gancho as an overhead used on the right side of the court for lobs over the left shoulder, hit higher than a bandeja, over the top of the head, so you can stay further up the court. Padel Browser calls it the overhead you play when a lob drifts over your non-dominant shoulder while you are still near the net, and turning round to set up a bandeja or víbora would cost your position. Left-handers mirror everything.

Other glossaries describe it more loosely. Padel.FYI calls it a defensive overhead used to regain the net, similar to the bandeja but with a higher contact point. The Padel Weekly calls it a softer overhead that buys time and keeps you at the net, and Bounce describes a softer, angled overhead. The common ground is useful: whatever the exact shape, the gancho is a positional overhead. It keeps you at the net; it rarely wins the point.

Why the definitions differ

Padel's shot names grew up on club courts in Spain and Argentina, not in a rulebook. Our padel terms glossary flags the gancho as one of the words whose meaning varies. If your coach uses it differently, go with your coach.

Gancho vs bandeja vs víbora

Padel Browser puts the difference neatly: the bandeja and víbora are choices you make on your hitting side; the gancho is usually a rescue. Playing a bandeja over the wrong shoulder costs extra steps back and opens the court, and The Padel School adds that it leaves a lot of space for your opponent. The gancho's higher contact point lets you play the ball sooner. For the other two shots in detail, see how to hit a bandeja and our guide to the víbora.

When to use the padel gancho

  • The lob is over your non-dominant shoulder and you are still close to the net.
  • You have time to turn your shoulders but not to reposition your feet.
  • Backing up for a bandeja would leave your partner alone at the net.
  • You're the right-side player in a right-handed pair and the lob comes cross-court, down the middle or down the line over your left shoulder, which is The Padel School's classic situation.

If the lob is over your hitting shoulder, it's a bandeja or víbora ball. If it is deep enough to push you several steps back, Padel Browser's advice is to stop trying to hold the net and play a lob or let the ball come off the glass.

How to hit a gancho, step by step

  1. Recognise it early. The moment the lob leaves the opponent's racket, decide which shoulder it is heading for. The decision is the hardest part.
  2. Turn your shoulders sideways. Point your non-dominant hand at the ball. It tracks the height and keeps your shoulders turned.
  3. Move as little as you can. One or two crossover steps back at most. More than that and the gancho is the wrong shot.
  4. Keep your normal continental grip. No grip change; there is no time.
  5. Straighten the arm. Meet the ball above and in front of your head with the arm extended. Padel Browser calls the straight arm non-negotiable: a bent elbow drops the contact point and turns the shot into a weak push.
  6. Hook down through the ball. Wrist relaxed, swing slightly downwards and drive through it rather than slapping at it. Don't try to kill it: the ball is already travelling away from you, so a full-power gancho sails long.
  7. Aim deep into the glass. The Padel School's target is the glass or fence at the back, so the ball bounces, rebounds and dies near the back of the court.
  8. Recover forwards. Close the net with your partner straight away. The shot only works if you take the position it bought.

Where to aim your gancho

Deep, every time. A gancho that lands short bounces high in the middle of the court and gives the opponents an easy attack. Aim to land it in the last couple of metres in front of their back glass, ideally towards the corner so it meets the glass and comes out low. Through the middle is a fine second option when the opponents are slow to decide who takes it. Hitting harder is not a substitute for depth.

Gancho faults and fixes

Do

Turn sideways early, point at the ball, straighten the arm, hook through with a relaxed wrist, aim deep and close the net again.

Don't

Bend the elbow, back up three or four steps, go for a winner, land it short in mid-court, or face the net square on so the arm can't get over the ball.

The square-shoulders problem is the one most players don't notice. If your chest faces the net, your arm can't get over the ball, so the swing comes across your body and the ball drops short. Film one game and check.

Gancho drills

Left-shoulder lobs

A stands at the net on the right side. A feeder on the far side lobs every ball over A's left shoulder at a height A can reach with a straight arm. A plays the gancho deep and returns to the net. Twenty balls, then swap. Pass mark: 12 of 20 landing in the last three metres.

Gancho or bandeja?

The feeder mixes lobs over each shoulder. A shouts "gancho" or "bandeja" before the ball reaches its highest point, then plays the matching shot. Pass mark: 15 correct calls out of 20.

Hold the net

Two against two, one pair at the net, one at the back. The back pair must lob down the middle. The net pair score a point each time they return a middle lob and are both back at the net before the next shot. Play to 10.

Frequently asked questions

What does gancho mean in padel?

Gancho is Spanish for "hook". In padel it usually means an overhead played over your non-dominant shoulder with a straight arm and a high contact point, used to keep the net when there is no time for a bandeja.

Is the gancho the same as the bandeja?

No. Both are positional overheads, but the bandeja is played on your hitting side around head height with slice, while the gancho is played over the other shoulder, higher and with a straight arm.

Do left-handers play the gancho?

Yes, mirrored. For a left-hander the gancho is the overhead over the right shoulder, most often when playing on the left side of the court.

Should beginners learn the gancho?

Learn the bandeja first. Once you can keep the net with a bandeja, add the gancho for the lobs that go over the wrong shoulder. Until then, letting the ball come off the glass is a perfectly good answer.

Is the gancho a smash?

Not really. It is an overhead, but its job is depth and position, not pace. Hitting it like a smash usually sends it long.
