The padel split step: timing, technique and drills

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

The padel split step is a small hop you make as your opponent hits the ball, so you land balanced on the balls of your feet at the moment of contact and can push off in any direction. It is the first move of almost every good shot, at the net and at the back. Here is how to do it, step by step, how to time it in different situations, and three drills that make it automatic.

What is a split step?

The Padel School defines it as a small hop you make just as your opponent hits the ball, which readies you to react in any direction. Julien Bondia, a padel teacher writing for Padel Magazine, describes the same movement: a small jump on the balls of both feet, landing together, to improve your reaction time.

Why bother? Because a player standing flat-footed has to unweight before they can move, and that costs time you don't have at the net. The Padel School lists the missing split step as a classic footwork mistake: players without it react too late because they're flat-footed. With it, you read the ball earlier, move more explosively and arrive balanced.

Tennis players usually bring the habit with them. The Padel School notes that players from other sports should learn it from the moment they start padel.

How to do a padel split step, step by step

  1. Get into your ready position. At the net, racket high and in front of you; at the back, racket lower. Those are The Padel School's two ready positions.
  2. Watch the opponent's racket, not the ball. Their preparation tells you when contact is coming.
  3. Start a small hop as their swing goes forward. A few centimetres is plenty. The Padel School describes the padel version as lighter and quicker than the split step many tennis players make.
  4. Land as they hit. Both feet, balls of the feet, knees soft, weight slightly forward. Bondia's phrase for the timing is "neither before nor after".
  5. Keep the heels off the floor. The Padel School warns that planting your heels makes you slower.
  6. Read, then move. As soon as you know the direction, push off with your first step towards the ball. Beginners can focus on the hop and only move once the ball's path is clear.
  7. Prepare the racket early. The split step buys time; use it to get the racket back before the ball arrives rather than as it arrives.

Split step timing in every situation

SituationLand your split stepThen
At the net, opponents at the backAs the opponent strikesShort step and volley, or turn for the lob
At the back, opponents at the netAs the net player strikesTurn for the glass or move in for a low ball
Moving forward to the netStop as the opponent is about to hit, split, then continueKeep closing the net if the ball allows
Returning serveAs the server strikesMove to the ball; most serves are slow enough to step in
Your partner is hittingNo split needed, but resetRacket up and ready; dropping it is a common error

The moving-forward row is the one most club players miss. The Padel School says the right moment to stop is when your opponent is about to contact the ball; Bondia's advice is the same: if they hit before you reach the net, stop short, do the split step as they touch the ball, then carry on forward. Running through the contact is how you end up playing a half volley at your feet.

Split step at the net vs at the back

At the net the ball arrives fastest, so the hop is smallest and quickest. Stand about two metres from the net with the racket up at chest height, as in our volley guide, and keep the movement compact: the split takes you into a short step and a block or punch, or into a turn if the opponent lobs.

At the back you have a little more time, but the split step matters just as much, because your first move is often a turn towards the glass. Land, read whether the ball will reach the back or side glass, and start moving away from the wall early. See the back glass guide for what happens next.

Both partners split at the same time. When one of you is late, the pair moves out of shape, which is why padel positioning starts with both of you landing as the ball is struck.

Split step drills

Our padel footwork drills page already has a fed split-step timing drill. These three add variety.

1. Clap split

No ball. Your partner faces you across the net and shadows a swing, clapping at "contact". You must land exactly on the clap, then take one step left or right as they point. Six sets of ten. Pass mark: 8 of 10 landings on the clap, judged by your partner.

2. Stop, split, go

A starts at the back and walks forward. F, at the net on the other side, feeds a ball as A reaches mid-court. A must stop, split as F hits, volley back to F and then carry on to the net. Ten balls. Pass mark: 8 of 10 splits landed on the feed and a controlled volley.

3. Freeze

Four players rally, with a coach or fifth player watching. At random moments the watcher shouts "freeze" just as someone hits. Anyone still in the air, or standing flat on their heels, loses a point. It's a fun way to make the habit visible.

Frequently asked questions

When should I split step in padel?

Every time an opponent hits the ball. Time the hop so you land as their racket meets the ball, not before and not after.

Do you split step at the back of the court?

Yes. It is just as important at the back, because your first move is often a turn towards the glass. The racket is lower in your ready position, but the timing is the same.

How high should a split step be?

Small. A few centimetres off the ground is enough. A big jump keeps you in the air too long and slows your first step.

Is the padel split step different from tennis?

The idea is the same. The Padel School describes the padel version as lighter and quicker, because the court is smaller and the ball arrives sooner, especially at the net.

Do beginners need to split step?

It helps from day one, but there's no rush. Bondia suggests adult beginners focus first on getting the ball over the net, while children can build the habit early. Add it as soon as rallies start to last.
