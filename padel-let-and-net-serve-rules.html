Net serve and let in padel: how many times can you replay?

8 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

There is no limit: a serve that clips the net or posts and then lands correctly in the receiver's box is simply replayed, as often as it happens, as long as it doesn't touch the metal fence before its second bounce. Lets on serve are replayed too, for reasons the rulebook lists. What matters is knowing exactly when a net serve is a replay, when it turns into a fault, and when a whole point is replayed instead of a single serve.

The short answer

A serve that touches the net or posts and then lands in the correct box is a “net” and is repeated, with no limit on how many times (FIP Rule 9.1a). If that same serve then hits the metal fence before its second bounce, it is a fault, not a net. A net on the first serve repeats the first serve; a net on the second serve repeats only the second serve (FIP Rule 9.2c).

What the rules say

The FIP rulebook separates two ideas that club players often mix up: the net on a serve (Rule 9) and the let, where a serve or a whole point is replayed because something interrupted play (Rules 9.2 and 10).

The net serve (Rule 9.1)

  • FIP Rule 9.1a: if the serve touches the net or the posts and then lands in the correct service box, the serve is repeated. The condition is that the ball must not touch the metal fence (the mesh) before its second bounce.
  • FIP Rule 9.1b: it is also a net if, after touching the net or posts, the ball hits a player or their equipment. The serve is repeated.
  • The rulebook mentions no limit on the number of net serves. You can clip the net five times in a row and you still have the serve you were on.

The let on serve (Rule 9.2)

  • FIP Rule 9.2a: a let is called if the receiver was not ready. This ties in with Rule 6.10, which says the server must wait until the receiver is ready, while the receiver has to adapt to the server's reasonable rhythm.
  • FIP Rule 9.2b: a let on the first serve means the whole point is replayed, so the server gets two serves again.
  • FIP Rule 9.2c: a net or a let on the second serve means only the second serve is repeated. A fault already made on the first serve still counts.

Let points during a rally (Rule 10)

A let point means the point is replayed from the start. FIP Rule 10 lists when that happens:

  • 10a: the ball splits.
  • 10b: a foreign object enters the court, such as a ball from the next court.
  • 10c: play is interrupted by unexpected circumstances that have nothing to do with the players.
  • 10d: the ball hits an object on the opponents' side (for example another ball) and that object moves in a way that could interfere with play or put someone in danger.
  • 10e: the player must tell the umpire immediately. If play carries on, the right to a let is lost.
  • 10f: if the let request is not valid, the player who asked for it loses the point.

Rule 11 adds one more source of lets: an involuntary hindrance of an opponent is a let, while a deliberate one costs the point. A second involuntary hindrance by the same pair costs them the point. Our guide to hindrance and interference covers that in detail.

Replay the serve

The serve clips the net tape or a post, lands in the correct box and then bounces again, or hits the back or side glass, or strikes a player. That is a net under Rule 9.1, and the serve is taken again with no limit.

Fault

The serve clips the net and lands outside the box (Rule 7c), or clips the net, lands in the box and touches the metal fence before its second bounce (Rules 9.1a and 7e). That serve is a fault, not a net.

Real match situations

1. Net cord, then the side glass

Your first serve trickles over the net tape, lands just inside the receiver's box and runs into the side glass. Glass is fine on a serve, so this is a net. Replay the first serve (FIP Rule 9.1a).

2. Net cord, then the mesh

Same serve, but you are serving from a position where the ball bounces in the box and then kicks into the metal fence near the corner before bouncing a second time. That is a fault. The net exception only applies if the ball avoids the fence before its second bounce (FIP Rules 9.1a and 7e). If it was your second serve, it is a double fault and you lose the point (FIP Rule 13q).

3. Net cord, then the receiver's body

The serve clips the net and hits the receiver on the full before it bounces. Without the net touch, Rule 8.5 would give the point to the server. Because the ball touched the net first, Rule 9.1b treats it as a net and the serve is repeated.

4. Three nets in a row on a second serve

You fault your first serve, then clip the net three times on your second serve, each time landing in the box. Each one is a net. You keep repeating the second serve only; your first-serve fault still stands (FIP Rule 9.2c). The fourth serve decides it.

5. The receiver wasn't ready

You serve while the receiver is still turning round after collecting a ball. The receiver doesn't play at it and signals they weren't ready. That is a let (FIP Rule 9.2a). If it happened on your first serve, you replay the point with two serves (9.2b). If it was your second serve, you replay just the second serve (9.2c). If the receiver attempts a return, they can no longer claim they were not ready (Rule 6.11); and Rule 6.10 expects the receiver to adapt to the server's reasonable rhythm. Rule 6.11 also means a fault can't be called on a serve struck while the receiver wasn't ready.

6. A ball rolls in from the next court mid-rally

Halfway through a rally, a ball from the neighbouring court rolls across your service box. Stop and call it straight away: that is a let point under FIP Rule 10b and the whole point is replayed, including two serves. If you keep playing and only complain after you lose the rally, you have lost the right to the let (FIP Rule 10e).

Common misconceptions

  • “You only get one or two net serves.” No such limit exists in the FIP rulebook. Every net serve that lands in the correct box without touching the fence is repeated.
  • “A net serve that hits the fence is still a let.” It isn't. Touching the metal fence before the second bounce makes it a fault (Rules 9.1a and 7e). Hitting the glass is fine.
  • “A net on the second serve gives you two new serves.” Only the second serve is repeated (Rule 9.2c). Two new serves come only with a let on the first serve (9.2b).
  • “You can claim a let after the point.” You must call it immediately. If play continues, the right is lost (Rule 10e), and an invalid let request costs you the point (Rule 10f).
  • “A net serve that lands in the wrong box is replayed.” It must land in the correct box to be a net. Outside the box it is a fault (Rule 7c), and remember the lines are good.

Social games vs official matches

In an official match the umpire calls nets and lets. In most club games there is no umpire, so the players call them. A few conventions keep things friendly:

  • The receiving pair usually calls the net, because they are watching the ball come over. The server can't always see a light touch on the tape.
  • Call it at once. The FIP rulebook doesn't spell out what happens if the receiver plays a net serve and the point continues. The sensible club convention is to stop and replay the serve as soon as anyone notices, rather than finishing the rally and arguing afterwards.
  • Agree beforehand how you handle balls from other courts. At busy clubs, stray balls are common. Under Rule 10b they are a let, and calling it immediately (10e) is both the rule and the safest habit.
  • Be generous with doubt. If nobody is sure whether the serve touched the net, most club players simply replay it.

Practical tips

A net serve that keeps landing in the box is not bad luck forever; it usually means your trajectory is too flat. The serve in padel is hit at or below waist height (Rule 6.4), so a slightly higher arc over the net gives you more margin without losing depth. For more on that, see our guide to the serve and return.

Watch the corner on net cords. A ball that dies off the tape often lands short and wide, close to where the side glass gives way to the mesh. If you aim your serve into the centre of the box rather than tight to the side, a net cord is far less likely to drift into the fence and become a fault. Frequently asked questions

Is there a limit on net serves in padel?

No. The FIP rulebook mentions no limit. Each net serve that lands in the correct box without touching the metal fence before its second bounce is replayed (FIP Rule 9.1a).

What is the difference between a net and a let in padel?

A net is a serve that touches the net or posts and then lands correctly (Rule 9.1). A let is a replay for another reason, such as the receiver not being ready (9.2a) or a ball entering from another court (10b).

Does a let on the second serve give me two serves?

No. A net or let on the second serve repeats only the second serve (FIP Rule 9.2c). A let on the first serve replays the whole point (9.2b).

Is a net serve that hits the glass a fault?

No. After landing in the box the serve may hit the glass. Only the metal fence before the second bounce turns it into a fault (Rules 9.1a and 7e).

What if a net serve hits my partner?

Rule 9.1b says that if the ball touches the net or posts and then hits a player or their equipment, it is a net and the serve is repeated.
