Pinky-side wrist pain in padel: the TFCC explained

4 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 17 Nov 2026

If your wrist hurts on the little-finger side, especially when you twist your forearm, grip hard or push up from a chair, the likely suspect is the TFCC. It's a small but busy structure, and padel's topspin forehands, víboras and twisting mishits all load it. Here's what it is, how to recognise it and what usually helps.

Not medical advice

This guide explains common patterns in plain language. It can't diagnose you. If the pain is severe, came from a fall or twist, or isn't settling, get it assessed.

What the TFCC is

The triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) is a group of cartilage, ligaments and soft tissue on the little-finger (ulnar) side of the wrist. Princeton Medicine describes its job as stabilising the wrist during gripping, twisting and weight-bearing. It can be irritated by repeated loading, as in racket sports, or torn by a fall onto an outstretched hand or a forceful twist.

Typical symptoms

  • Pain on the little-finger side of the wrist
  • Pain when twisting the forearm (turning a door handle, a key, a racket)
  • Clicking or catching
  • A weaker grip, or a wrist that feels unstable
  • Pain when you put weight through the hand, for example pushing up from a chair

These symptoms aren't unique to the TFCC, so they're a reason to get it checked if they persist, not a diagnosis.

Why padel loads it

The little-finger side of the wrist takes load when you roll the wrist over on a topspin forehand, snap down on a víbora or flat smash, and when the racket twists in your hand on an off-centre hit. A heavier or head-heavy racket has more leverage to twist. Rehaboteket's physio guidance on wrist pain in racket sports lists a heavier racket, a grip size that doesn't suit your hand and a sudden rise in playing time among the common triggers. Falls are the other route: putting a hand out when you trip or collide with the glass.

What usually helps

Princeton Medicine notes that most TFCC problems start with non-surgical care. In practice that means:

  1. Calm it down. Avoid the painful twisting and gripping for a while. A splint or brace is sometimes used in the early phase. The NHS suggests ice wrapped in a towel for up to 20 minutes every two to three hours and simple painkillers for mild wrist pain.
  2. Rebuild gradually. Grip strength, forearm rotation and wrist control, progressed slowly: see our 6-week wrist plan.
  3. Change the padel load. Less wrist roll on forehands, spin from the swing path rather than a flick on the víbora, a lighter racket and a grip that fits so you don't need to squeeze.

When to see someone

See a GP or physio if the pain hasn't improved after two weeks of looking after it at home, keeps coming back, or comes with tingling or loss of feeling. After a fall, the NHS says not to treat a possible broken wrist yourself. Get urgent advice if you have:

  • severe wrist pain, or you feel faint, dizzy or sick from the pain
  • you heard a snap, grinding or popping noise when it happened
  • you can't move your wrist or hold things
  • the wrist has changed shape or colour
  • a very painful, hot or red lump on the wrist
  • you've lost feeling in part or all of your hand

Frequently asked questions

What is a TFCC injury?

Irritation or a tear of the triangular fibrocartilage complex, a group of cartilage and ligaments on the little-finger side of the wrist that stabilises it during gripping and twisting.

Can padel cause a TFCC injury?

Repeated twisting and gripping in racket sports can irritate it, and a fall onto an outstretched hand can tear it.

Does a TFCC injury heal on its own?

Many settle with non-surgical care: reducing painful activities, sometimes a splint early on, then gradual strengthening. Persistent symptoms need assessment.

Should I play padel with pinky-side wrist pain?

Reduce the shots and movements that hurt and get it checked if it doesn't settle within a couple of weeks or if it clicks, catches or feels weak.

Can a racket cause pinky-side wrist pain?

A heavy or head-heavy racket twists more on off-centre hits and a grip that doesn't suit your hand makes you squeeze; both add load to this side of the wrist.
