Indoor vs outdoor padel: what changes and which to choose

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Indoor vs outdoor padel comes down to control versus conditions: indoors you get a steady, predictable ball but a ceiling that limits your lobs, while outdoors you get unlimited height and usually cheaper courts, but wind, sun and temperature change every rally. The rules are the same. Below is a side-by-side comparison, real price data, and what to do differently on each type of court.

Ball speed and bounce

The ball itself doesn't change, but the air around it does. The Padel School's outdoor tactics guide says cold air reduces the bounce and hot air increases it, and that wet ground makes balls heavy so they bounce lower and play slower. Padel Browser adds that morning dew slows the ball while afternoon heat speeds it up.

Indoors, Padel Browser says courts generally give a more consistent bounce because the turf stays dry, the temperature is stable and the sand infill stays evenly spread. That consistency is the main reason many players feel they play "better" indoors. Our guide to padel balls explains why heat and pressure change how a ball plays.

Lobs: the ceiling versus the wind

Indoors, your enemy is the roof. The FIP court specifications require at least 6 metres of clear height across the whole court, with nothing such as lights projecting into it, and recommend 8 metres for new facilities. Many indoor clubs sit close to the minimum. A lob that touches the ceiling, a beam or a light before bouncing in the opponents' court loses you the point, while a ball that bounces correctly and is then carried into the ceiling is a correct return (FIP Rules 13g and 14d). Our page on what happens when the ball hits the ceiling has the details.

Outdoors, there's no ceiling, but there's wind. Padel Browser calls wind the single biggest variable between indoor and outdoor play, and says outdoor players flatten their trajectories and keep the ball lower. The Padel School's rule for the outdoor lob: with the wind behind you, hit with less power; into the wind, hit harder.

Sun, heat and rain

Outdoors, the sun becomes a tactic. The Padel School suggests lobbing often when your opponents have to look into the sun, because the overhead becomes much harder to judge. Padel Browser notes that afternoon sun makes smashes harder to read and can force you to adjust your serve.

Heat matters for comfort as well as the ball: Padel Browser compares summer outdoor sessions in hot US cities to a sauna, while climate-controlled indoor clubs stay comfortable all year. Rain stops outdoor play, and a wet court is slippery. The Padel School also warns that on wet glass the ball can slide down the wall instead of bouncing out, so get behind and under it. For a full outdoor playbook, see playing padel in wind and sun.

Lights, noise and other indoor details

Indoor courts swap weather for a different set of distractions. Lights hanging low over the court can blind you on a high lob much as the sun does outside, so in the warm-up look up from both ends and note where the bright spots are. Neighbouring courts, netting between them and echoing halls can also be distracting at first. Give yourself a few minutes to settle, just as you would outdoors.

Court surface and shoes

Both types normally use artificial turf with sand infill. Indoors it stays dry and even; outdoors it can be damp in the morning, dusty in the heat and slippery after rain. Good grip matters more outside, so check your soles; our guide to padel shoes explains the patterns.

Which is better for beginners?

Indoors has a slight edge for learning, because a predictable ball lets you groove your technique and learn the walls without guessing the wind. Padel Browser makes the same point, then adds a sensible caveat: how often you play matters more than perfect conditions, so if outdoor courts are cheaper or closer and you'll play more, play outdoors.

Choose indoor if…

You're learning technique, you want to play all year whatever the weather, you live somewhere very hot, cold or wet, or you mainly play in the evening.

Choose outdoor if…

You want lower prices, you love lobbing without a ceiling, you enjoy reading the conditions, or your nearest courts are outdoors and you'll play more often there.

What changes tactically

Outdoors:

  • Lob into the sun and with the wind; adjust the lob's power to the wind.
  • Be patient. The Padel School advises playing a few easy balls before going for a winner like the víbora, which is harder to execute outdoors.
  • Give yourself the warm-up to adapt: The Padel School suggests 5 to 10 minutes.

Indoors:

  • Check the ceiling and lights in the warm-up and play a few practice lobs.
  • Lob lower and deeper rather than higher if the roof is close.
  • Expect a truer bounce off the glass and commit to your back-glass shots earlier.

Frequently asked questions

Is indoor or outdoor padel better?

Neither is better overall. Indoor gives you consistent conditions and year-round play; outdoor is usually cheaper and has no ceiling, but wind, sun and temperature change the game.

Is the ball faster indoors or outdoors?

It depends on the temperature more than the roof. Warm air makes the ball livelier and cold or damp conditions slow it down. Indoor courts are simply more consistent from day to day.

How high does an indoor padel ceiling need to be?

The FIP court specifications require at least 6 metres of clear height over the whole court and recommend 8 metres for new facilities.

What happens if my lob hits the ceiling?

If it touches the ceiling or lights before bouncing in the opponents' court, you lose the point. If it bounces correctly first and then hits the ceiling, it's a correct return (FIP Rules 13g and 14d).

Is outdoor padel cheaper?

Usually. Playtomic data reported in October 2025 put average outdoor court prices below indoor ones in every country listed, for example €22 versus €25 per hour in Spain.
