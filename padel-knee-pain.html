Padel knee pain: what's causing it and what fixes it

9 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel knee pain is usually an overload problem rather than one bad moment: lunges, split steps and getting low for volleys pile load on the front of the knee, while twisting on a planted foot can strain the meniscus or ligaments. Where it hurts, when it hurts and whether the knee swells, locks or gives way tell you a lot about what's going on. Below is a symptom table, a quick self-check, the exercises with the best evidence, and the signs that mean you should stop and get it looked at.

General information, not medical advice

This page helps you understand common patterns of knee pain in padel. It can't diagnose you. If your knee is very painful, swollen, locking or giving way, get it assessed.

Why padel is hard on your knees

The knee is one of the most frequently injured areas in padel. A 2023 systematic review of padel injury studies (Dahmen and colleagues) ranked it second only to the elbow, with knee injuries making up roughly 10% to 23% of all injuries across the studies it pooled. A narrative review in the Aspetar Sports Medicine Journal describes the usual suspects: patellar tendinopathy, problems with the medial (inner) meniscus and sprains of the medial collateral ligament, which can follow the knee being forced inwards.

The reasons are easy to see on court. You lunge for wide balls, brake hard after a sprint to a drop shot, crouch to dig balls out of the back glass and push off explosively from a split step. Each of those loads the tendon below your kneecap and the joint surfaces behind it. Twist on a foot that is stuck in sand-filled grass and the meniscus gets involved too.

Padel knee pain symptom table

Use this to narrow down what kind of pain you have. It's a guide to the most likely explanation and a sensible first step, not a diagnosis. The NHS is clear on this point: don't self-diagnose a knee problem that isn't settling.

A two-minute self-check after you play

These questions won't tell you what's wrong, but they give you, and anyone you see later, useful information.

  1. Point to it with one finger. Just below the kneecap, around it, on the inner joint line or somewhere vague? Write it down.
  2. Note the timing. Did it hurt at the start of play and ease once warm, build during the match, or only show up the next morning? Mayo Clinic notes patellar tendon pain often appears at the start of activity or after a hard workout.
  3. Compare both knees. Look for swelling or puffiness on the sore side, and check whether you can fully straighten and bend both legs.
  4. Try everyday tests. Walk up and down a flight of stairs and stand up from a low chair. Pain on these is typical of front-of-knee problems.
  5. Check for mechanical symptoms. Catching, locking, or a feeling that the knee might give way points away from simple overload and towards something that needs assessing.

Front-of-knee pain after padel: tendon or kneecap?

Most padel knee pain sits at the front, and it usually comes in one of two forms.

Patellar tendinopathy hurts between the bottom of the kneecap and the point where the tendon joins the shinbone. Mayo Clinic lists running and jumping as the activities most closely linked with it, along with a sharp rise in how often or how hard you train, tight thigh muscles and strength imbalances. The Aspetar review notes it is about two to four times more likely in men than in women. In padel, a burst of extra matches, lots of jumping smashes and an Americano weekend on top of your usual games is the classic story.

Anterior knee pain, often called patellofemoral pain, is a duller ache around or behind the kneecap. An NHS physiotherapy leaflet from University Hospitals of Leicester describes it as a problem with how the kneecap glides when you bend and straighten the leg, made worse by tight or weak hip and knee muscles, and typically aggravated by running, jumping, stairs and getting up from a chair.

Both are overload problems, so the fixes overlap: reduce the aggravating load for a while, then rebuild strength so your knee can handle padel again.

Meniscus and twisting: the planted-foot moment

Mayo Clinic explains that a torn meniscus can happen during any activity that makes the knee twist or rotate forcefully, and lists sports with pivoting, tennis among them, as a risk factor. The risk also rises with age, because the cartilage wears. Typical signs are a pop at the time, swelling or stiffness, and a knee that catches, locks or won't take your weight. Symptoms can be delayed by a day or more, especially with smaller tears.

In padel, the dangerous moments are turning to chase a lob over your head with your weight stuck on one foot, and pivoting at the net to cover a ball down the middle. Two habits help: turn with small adjusting steps rather than spinning on one leg, and wear shoes with the right sole for your court so you can control a slide instead of sticking. Strengthening exercises for padel knees

For front-of-knee pain, the 2016 international consensus statement on patellofemoral pain (Crossley and colleagues, British Journal of Sports Medicine) recommends combining hip and knee exercises, and says that combination should be used in preference to knee exercises alone. Strength work is also the best-supported way to prevent sports injuries generally. Twice a week, try:

ExerciseSets × repsWhat it targets
Wall sit3 × 20–40 sQuadriceps without much knee movement
Slow step-down from a low step3 × 8 each legQuadriceps control, knee tracking over the toes
Split squat3 × 8 each legSingle-leg strength for lunges
Glute bridge3 × 12Hip extensors that support the knee
Side-lying leg raise or band side-steps3 × 12 each sideHip abductors that stop the knee caving in
Calf raise3 × 12Shock absorption from the ankle up

These are sensible starting points, not a prescription. NHS physiotherapy advice for anterior knee pain is to keep activity at a level that doesn't flare your pain, then build back gradually. If an exercise makes the pain clearly worse, drop the range or the load. For the wider programme, see our padel exercises guide.

Shoes, court surface and your knees

Footwear may matter more than people think. A 2025 cross-sectional study of 216 Spanish padel players (Rocamora-López and Mateo-Orcajada) linked herringbone soles with more muscle, tendon and ligament injuries, and found more muscle and ligament injuries on sand courts. It can't prove cause and effect, and it is one study, so don't throw out shoes that work for you. The practical message is to wear padel-specific shoes suited to your usual surface and replace them when the tread is worn smooth.

Insoles are another option for front-of-knee pain: the same consensus statement recommends foot orthoses to reduce patellofemoral pain in the short term. See our guide to padel insoles before you buy.

Knee support or brace: worth it?

Many players wear a sleeve or strap. The patellofemoral consensus made no recommendation for patellar taping and found its role unclear, and it gives no separate recommendation for braces. A sleeve that makes your knee feel more comfortable is unlikely to hurt, but it won't fix the load or strength problem underneath. If your knee genuinely feels unstable, that's a reason to see a physiotherapist, not to buy a stronger brace.

When to rest and how to build back

For a knee that aches after play, NHS self-care advice is to take some weight off it, use an ice pack wrapped in a towel for up to 20 minutes every two to three hours, and consider simple pain relief from a pharmacist. Mayo Clinic adds reducing the activities that bring on symptoms. Rest from padel doesn't mean rest from everything: keep walking and keep doing the strength work at a level that doesn't flare it.

When you return, start with a shorter session and no jumping smashes, leave at least a day between matches, and only add time once the knee is no worse the next morning. Red flags: get your knee checked

See a GP or physiotherapist if knee pain hasn't improved within a few weeks, or keeps coming back every time you play. The NHS says to get urgent advice the same day if:

  • your knee is very painful, or you can't move it or put any weight on it
  • it is badly swollen or has changed shape
  • it locks, gives way or clicks painfully (painless clicking is normal)
  • you have a high temperature or feel hot and shivery, with redness or heat around the knee

Frequently asked questions

Is padel bad for your knees?

Not inherently, but it loads them. Knee injuries made up roughly 10% to 23% of padel injuries across studies in a 2023 systematic review. Leg strength, a gradual build-up of playing time and the right shoes reduce the load.

Why does the front of my knee hurt after padel?

Usually overload of the patellar tendon, just below the kneecap, or of the joint behind the kneecap. Both tend to follow a jump in playing time, lots of jumping and lunging, or weak hips and thighs.

Should I keep playing padel with knee pain?

A mild ache that settles quickly may allow lighter play, but pain that builds during a match or lingers the next day means you should reduce your load. Swelling, locking or giving way means stop and get it assessed.

Does a knee brace help for padel?

The evidence is unclear. A sleeve may feel more comfortable, but it doesn't replace strength work or load management, and a knee that feels unstable should be assessed by a professional.

What exercises help knee pain from padel?

For front-of-knee pain, research supports combining hip and knee strengthening: wall sits, slow step-downs, split squats, glute bridges and hip abductor work, at a level that doesn't flare the pain.

Can a twisted knee in padel be a meniscus tear?

It can. Forceful twisting on a planted foot is the typical way the meniscus tears. Swelling, catching, locking or a knee that won't take weight needs assessing.
