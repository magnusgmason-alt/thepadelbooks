The padel cuchilla: the knife shot off the back glass

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

The padel cuchilla ('knife') is an attacking shot played off your own back glass: a lob bounces, comes out high, and instead of a plain bajada you cut down and around the ball with víbora-style spin so it lands deep and stays low. Glossaries describe it as a mix of the bajada and the víbora, and it's associated with the Spanish professional Paquito Navarro. It's an advanced shot; this guide explains what it is, how it compares with the other overheads, and how to build up to it.

What is the cuchilla in padel?

Cuchilla is Spanish for knife, and the sources agree on its outline. Tennis Warehouse's padel terminology calls it a mix between the bajada and the víbora, also called the knife shot. Total Padel's Spanish vocabulary says the same. Padel.FYI is more specific: an aggressive form of the bajada from the back wall that applies more side spin. The Padel Weekly credits it to Paquito Navarro and says it combines elements of the bajada and víbora to create side spin that is harder to retrieve off the back wall.

The Spanish fitness site Vitónica gives the clearest picture. It describes the cuchilla as the name given to Navarro's distinctive wall exit: the ball bounces, hits the back glass and comes out very high, and he attacks it from around head height, from top to bottom with cut, so that after it bounces and meets the opponents' back wall it doesn't sit up.

Overhead or wall shot?

It looks like an overhead, and some players use "cuchilla" loosely for any knife-like sliced attacking overhead. In the glossaries, though, it is played after the ball has come off your own back glass. That is the definition we use, and it matches our padel terms glossary.

CuchillaAfter the bounce and the back glass, ball very highAt the back of the courtSlice plus side spin, cut downwardsA low, deep ball the opponents can't attack

In short: the bandeja and víbora answer a lob in the air; the bajada and cuchilla answer one that has beaten you and come high off your glass. The cuchilla is the bajada with the víbora's bite.

When the padel cuchilla is on

  • The ball comes out of your back glass very high. Vitónica stresses that for this to be an attacking shot the ball must come off the wall or glass very high, so you can hit from around head height.
  • You've got behind the ball. You need to be between the ball and the wall, side-on and balanced, not chasing it.
  • The opponents have come to the net. A low, skidding ball deep in their court is exactly what a net pair hates.

When it isn't on: the ball comes out below shoulder height, you're moving backwards, or you're off balance. Then play a normal exit off the glass or a lob and fight for the net on the next ball. Our guide to the back glass covers those.

Who should use the cuchilla

Honestly, not most club players yet. Vitónica calls it a complicated shot, and the glossaries place it among the professional variations of the overheads. It assumes you already have:

  • A reliable bajada, so you can get behind high balls off the glass and attack them under control.
  • A víbora, so you know how to brush the outside of the ball for side spin.
  • Good reading of the back glass, so you can tell early which lobs will come out high.

If your bajada still goes long or into the net, the cuchilla isn't your next shot. Build the bajada first; the extra spin is a refinement, not a shortcut.

How to hit the cuchilla

Sources describe the cuchilla's shape rather than a coaching checklist, so the technique below combines the bajada and víbora fundamentals that the glossaries say it is built from.

Read and get behind it

As soon as the lob goes over you, move back fast and judge where it will come out of the glass. Get behind it, side-on, with your weight on the back leg. Short side steps, no backpedalling.

Racket up early

Bring the racket up beside your head as the ball rises off the glass, elbow up, free arm pointing at the ball, just as for a bandeja or víbora.

Contact high and in front

Let the ball come out and meet it as high as you comfortably can, around head height, in front of you and slightly to the side. If it has dropped to your shoulder, play a bajada instead.

Cut down and around the ball

Swing forward and down, brushing the outside and the back of the ball rather than hitting through its centre. That is the víbora-style action that adds side spin to the bajada's slice. Babolat's description of the víbora mentions a quick pronation of the forearm; the same turn helps here. Vitónica's key point is the direction: from top to bottom, with cut, so the ball doesn't rise after it lands.

Step in and recover

Transfer your weight onto the front leg through the shot and move forward with your partner if the reply is weak.

Where to aim

Vitónica's target is a ball that hits the floor hard but close to the opponents' back wall, so it is very difficult to defend. Aim deep, towards the corner, so the side spin carries the ball into the side glass and keeps it low. A cuchilla that lands in the middle of the court has none of the shot's benefits and gives a net pair an easy volley.

Why the cuchilla goes wrong

  • Trying it on a low ball. From shoulder height or below, the downward cut drives the ball into the net.
  • Contact behind the body. You waited for the ball to come to you off the glass. Get behind it earlier.
  • All spin, no depth. A short cuchilla sits up. Depth first.
  • Too much pace. Like the víbora, power without control sends the ball high off the opponents' glass.
  • Square stance. Without the side-on turn you can't swing around the ball.

Building up to it: a three-stage progression

  1. Bajada depth. A feeder lobs so the ball comes high off your back glass. Play bajadas into the last three metres of the far court. Aim for 7 of 10.
  2. Víbora brush. From fed lobs in the air, play víboras into the far side glass and count how many stay below the waist.
  3. Combine. Back to the high glass feeds, now adding the víbora brush to the bajada. Score only balls that land deep and stay low.

Frequently asked questions

What does cuchilla mean in padel?

Cuchilla means knife. In padel it is an attacking shot off your own back glass that combines the bajada with the víbora's side spin, so the ball lands deep and stays low.

Who is known for the cuchilla?

Spanish professional Paquito Navarro. Vitónica describes the cuchilla as the name given to his distinctive wall exit, and The Padel Weekly credits the shot to him.

Is the cuchilla the same as the víbora?

No. The víbora is hit in the air before the ball bounces. The cuchilla is hit after the ball has bounced and come high off your back glass, with similar side spin.

Is the cuchilla legal?

Yes. Under the FIP rules a ball that has bounced on your side may hit your glass and still be played before the second bounce. The cuchilla is simply an attacking way to play that ball.

Can beginners hit a cuchilla?

It isn't a beginner shot. Learn to play off the back glass, then the bajada and the víbora. The cuchilla comes after those are reliable.
