Padel equipment: what you need to play

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 30 Oct 2026

The padel equipment you need to play is short: a padel racket, padel balls and court shoes, which is exactly the list the LTA gives beginners. Everything else, from overgrips to bags, is optional. This guide covers what each item does, what to look for, which extras are worth buying, and one piece of kit most players overlook: eye protection.

What do you need to play padel?

The LTA lists three essentials: a padel racket, padel balls and suitable court shoes. Add comfortable sportswear and a bottle of water and you're ready. The club provides the court, net and walls, and most clubs will hire you a racket for your first sessions.

The Padel School gives the same advice and adds a sensible warning: don't buy the most expensive pro model, multiple rackets or specialist accessories before you know you'll keep playing.

The padel racket

A padel racket has no strings. It's a solid, perforated bat-like frame with a wrist cord. Under the FIP rules, a racket can be at most 45.5 cm long, 26 cm wide and 38 mm thick, and the wrist cord is compulsory. Every racket from a mainstream padel brand meets these limits.

For beginners, The Padel School recommends a round head shape for more control and a larger sweet spot, a medium or soft core, and steering clear of diamond-shaped rackets early on. It also suggests trying or borrowing a few rackets before buying, because comfort and feel matter more than the brand. Our guide to choosing a padel racket explains shape, weight and balance in detail.

Hire first, buy second

Play three or four sessions with club rackets. You'll learn whether you prefer a lighter or heavier feel before you spend anything.

Padel balls

Padel balls look like tennis balls but are slightly smaller and, as The Padel School puts it, slightly less pressurised, so they're a little slower. The FIP sets the official size, weight, bounce and pressure. Proper padel balls are cheap compared with the rest of your kit, and a fresh can makes a real difference to how the ball comes off the glass. Our full guide to padel balls covers specs, lifespan and the best brands.

Padel shoes

Shoes are the one item both the LTA and The Padel School tell beginners not to skimp on. Padel is all side steps, sprints, split steps and lunges on sand-filled artificial grass. The LTA's guide points to lateral support, a textured herringbone or mixed-pattern sole for traction, and cushioning to protect your knees and ankles.

Avoid running shoes. The Padel School says they don't give the right grip or stability and increase injury risk. Padel shoes or clay-court tennis shoes are the safe choice. Our padel shoes guide explains sole types.

Clothing

There's no uniform. The Padel School says any comfortable sportswear works when you're starting out. The LTA's guide recommends stretch fabrics, moisture-wicking material and breathable layers, with light warm-up layers in winter. Our guide to what to wear for padel covers hot weather, club dress codes and outfits for men and women.

Useful accessories

Overgrip

An overgrip is a thin, tacky tape wrapped over the racket's handle. The Padel School says overgrips improve comfort and stop the racket slipping, and suggests having one or two in your bag. Replace it when it feels slick, shiny or starts to tear. It's the cheapest upgrade you can make to a racket.

Wristband and headband

Optional, but useful in summer or on indoor courts. A wristband stops sweat running onto your grip; a headband keeps it out of your eyes.

Padel bag

The Padel School calls a basic padel bag useful for keeping your racket protected. You don't need a big thermo bag with racket compartments until you own more than one racket. A simple bag with space for shoes, balls and a towel is enough.

Frame protector

Low balls and wall shots mean rackets scrape the turf and glass. Protective frame tape, which The Padel School lists as a way to extend racket life, takes the knocks instead of the frame.

Eye protection for padel

This is the one piece of padel equipment most club players don't own. In 2021, ophthalmologists at the University of Gothenburg and Sahlgrenska University Hospital in Sweden described padel as a potential high-risk sport for eye injuries, in an article in the journal of the Swedish Medical Association. They reported three cases, all of which needed surgery, and said Swedish eye clinics were seeing more padel-related injuries.

Their reasoning: the ball is about the size of the opening of the eye socket, smashes can reach about 130 km/h, and the bounce off the walls makes the ball's path hard to judge. Most injuries were mild, but some caused lasting damage to vision. They recommended wearing protective goggles and said correctly used eye protection can cut the risk of eye damage by up to 90%.

Protective sports goggles are worth considering, especially for juniors, players who stand at the net a lot and anyone who has had an eye problem before. Whatever you wear, good habits help too: at the net, keep your racket up and in front of you at about chest height, the ready position in our volley guide, so you can react to a fast ball.

What not to buy yet

  • A pro-level racket. The Padel School says expensive rackets are not automatically better, and mid-range models usually offer the best value for beginners and improvers.
  • Several rackets. One is plenty until you play several times a week.
  • Specialist apparel and performance gadgets. Fun later, unnecessary now.

How much does padel equipment cost?

As a guide, Padel Browser's June 2026 US figures put a beginner racket at $60 to $130, padel shoes at $80 to $140, a can of three balls at $8 to $12, and a complete beginner setup at $100 to $250. Prices vary by country and change often. Our guide to how much padel costs adds court hire and lessons.

Frequently asked questions

What equipment do you need for padel?

A padel racket, padel balls and court shoes, plus comfortable sportswear. The LTA lists the first three as the essentials. Most clubs hire rackets, so you can start with just shoes and clothes.

Can I use a tennis racket for padel?

No. Padel is played with a solid, stringless padel racket with a compulsory wrist cord, as set out in the FIP rules.

Do I need padel shoes to start?

Not for your first session. Any court shoe with good side support will do. Avoid running shoes. If you play regularly, padel or clay-court tennis shoes are worth buying.

Should I wear eye protection for padel?

It's worth considering. Swedish eye specialists described padel as a potential high-risk sport for eye injuries in 2021 and recommended protective goggles, saying correct use can cut the risk by up to 90%.

What accessories are worth buying for padel?

An overgrip is the most useful and cheapest upgrade. After that, a simple bag, a wristband and frame protector tape. Skip expensive gadgets until you play often.
