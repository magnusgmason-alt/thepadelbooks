Padel wrist pain: why it happens and how to stop it

8 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel wrist pain usually builds up gradually from three things: squeezing the grip, using the wrist instead of the body on volleys and overheads, and a racket that's too heavy or head-heavy for your arm. Sudden pain after a fall onto your hand is a different story and needs checking. Here is how to tell the common types apart, what to change in your technique and equipment, the exercises that build a more resilient wrist, and when to see someone.

Not medical advice

This guide explains common wrist problems in racket sports in plain language. It can't diagnose you. If your wrist hurts after a fall, or the pain is severe, get it checked.

Why padel hurts the wrist

Most wrist trouble in padel is the slow-burn kind. In a 2025 study of 364 amateur players in Chile (Belmar-Arriagada and colleagues), 81.8% of wrist and hand injuries came on gradually rather than from a single incident. That points to repeated load, and padel offers plenty of it.

Grip tension

Squeezing the handle locks the wrist and makes the forearm muscles work all session. The tighter you hold on, the more every impact travels into the wrist and elbow. Our padel grip guide covers how firm is firm enough.

A wrist-led víbora, bandeja or volley

When the legs and trunk don't turn, the wrist ends up generating the power and spin. The Aspetar review of padel injuries notes that recreational players tend to hit backhands with a more flexed wrist than elite players, loading the forearm muscles in a way linked to overuse problems. Flicking the wrist to add spin to a víbora, or snapping it on a volley, adds load on every repetition.

A heavy or head-heavy racket

A physiotherapist-reviewed guide from Rehaboteket lists switching to a heavier racket, a grip size that doesn't suit your hand and a sudden rise in playing time among the causes of wrist pain in racket sports. A head-heavy racket also has more leverage to twist in your hand on off-centre hits, so your wrist works harder to hold the face steady.

TFCC and ulnar-sided wrist pain

The triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) is a group of cartilage, ligament and soft-tissue structures on the little-finger side of the wrist that stabilises it during gripping, twisting and weight-bearing. Princeton Medicine lists racket sports among the activities that can irritate it through repeated loading, alongside falling onto an outstretched hand or forceful twisting. Typical complaints are pain on the pinky side, clicking or catching, reduced grip strength and a wrist that feels weak.

In padel, that side of the wrist takes load when you roll the wrist over on topspin forehands, snap down on a víbora, or let the racket face twist on a mishit. Most TFCC problems start with non-surgical care: avoiding the painful twisting and gripping for a while, sometimes a brace in the early phase, and gradual rehabilitation focused on grip strength, forearm rotation and wrist control.

Tendon problems: thumb side and forearm

The NHS lists long-lasting pain, swelling and stiffness at the base of the thumb, with difficulty moving the thumb or fingers, as typical of de Quervain's, a tendon problem, or arthritis. In padel it tends to flare with a tight grip and a lot of wrist movement towards the thumb side. Pain on the back of the wrist that spreads up the forearm usually involves the same extensor muscles that cause padel elbow, so the fixes overlap: lighter grip, better technique and gradual strengthening.

How to prevent padel wrist pain

  • Build up gradually. Rehaboteket's physio advice is to raise playing volume step by step and read recurring or worsening pain as a sign the load is too high.
  • Relax the hand between shots. Firm up only around contact, then let go again.
  • Volley from the shoulder. A short punch with a firm wrist; the power comes from the incoming ball and your body, not a flick.
  • Turn your body on overheads. LTA Padel's coaching advice for the bandeja is to turn side-on early and swing across the body, taking pace off the ball. The Padel School stresses an early turn and contact in front. Both put the work into your trunk, not your wrist.
  • Don't chase spin with the wrist. On the víbora, spin comes from the swing path and contact point. If you're still learning it, The Padel Playbook: Intermediate & Advanced breaks down the bandeja and víbora, and the overhead drills in The Padel Drill Book: Intermediate & Advanced let you groove them at a pace your arm can cope with.
  • Warm up the forearm. Wrist circles, gentle opening and closing of the hand and a few minutes of soft hitting before you play.

Wrist exercises for padel players

Strength and control work for the wrist and forearm makes everyday padel load easier to cope with. Two or three times a week, with a light dumbbell or a water bottle:

Wrist flexionSame position, palm up2–3 × 12–15

Start light. Mild effort is fine; sharp pain is not. These are general starting points for healthy wrists. If your wrist already hurts, especially on the pinky side, ask a physiotherapist which exercises suit you, because some positions aggravate an irritated TFCC. Racket weight, balance and grip size

If your wrist complains, look at your racket. The usual advice, echoed in our elbow guide, is a racket towards the lighter end for your size, with an even or low balance and a softer feel. A grip that's too thin makes you squeeze harder to stop the racket turning; adding an overgrip builds the handle up. Our guides to soft vs hard padel rackets and changing an overgrip go into detail. There's little research on racket specs and wrist injuries, so treat this as sensible advice rather than proven fact.

Wrist supports and straps

The NHS suggests a supportive wrist splint, especially at night, as part of self-care for wrist pain, and Princeton Medicine notes a brace can reduce irritation in the early phase of a TFCC problem. As Rehaboteket points out, a support can relieve the wrist and add stability, but it can't treat the cause. Use it as a short-term aid while you fix the load, grip and technique, not as permission to play through pain.

If it already hurts

NHS self-care for wrist pain: rest the wrist when you can, keep gently moving the hand and wrist, use an ice pack wrapped in a towel for up to 20 minutes every two to three hours, cut back on activities that aggravate it, and avoid gripping things too tightly. A pharmacist can advise on pain relief. Avoid heat packs in the first two to three days after an injury. An NHS physiotherapy leaflet from Leicestershire adds that you shouldn't stop all activity, but reducing it at first helps.

When to see a doctor or physio

See a GP if wrist pain stops you doing normal activities, keeps getting worse or coming back, hasn't improved after two weeks of treating it at home, or comes with tingling or loss of feeling. The NHS says to get urgent help (NHS 111 in the UK) if:

  • the pain is severe, or you feel faint, dizzy or sick from it
  • you heard a snap, grinding or popping noise when it happened
  • you can't move your wrist or hold things
  • the wrist has changed shape or colour, or you've lost feeling in part of your hand
  • there's a very painful, hot or red lump on the wrist

Pain and difficulty moving the wrist after a fall, for example after a collision with the glass, should be seen in A&E or an urgent care centre.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my wrist hurt after padel?

Most often from repeated load: a tight grip, letting the wrist do the work on volleys and overheads, a heavy or head-heavy racket, or a sudden increase in playing time. Pain after a fall onto your hand is different and should be checked.

What is TFCC pain in padel?

Pain on the little-finger side of the wrist from irritation or a tear of the triangular fibrocartilage complex. It typically hurts when twisting or gripping and may click. It needs proper assessment if it doesn't settle.

Can the víbora cause wrist pain?

It can if you create the spin by flicking the wrist rather than with your body turn and swing path. Turning early and making contact in front takes load off the wrist.

Should I wear a wrist support for padel?

A support can relieve symptoms short term, but it doesn't treat the cause. Use it alongside reducing load and fixing grip and technique, and see a professional if pain persists.

Does grip size matter for wrist pain?

It can. A handle that's too thin for your hand makes you squeeze harder. An overgrip is an easy way to build it up.
