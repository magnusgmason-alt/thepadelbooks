Calf strain in padel: tennis leg, the Achilles and getting back on court

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

A calf strain in padel usually happens in one explosive moment: you push off to chase a drop shot and feel a sudden, sharp pain in the back of the lower leg, sometimes with a snapping sensation. In a 2025 survey of 645 French players, the calf and Achilles tendon together were the most commonly injured area of all. Here is how to tell a calf strain from an Achilles problem, what to do in the first few days, a calf raise programme to build resilience, and how to return safely.

Not medical advice

This is general information. A suspected Achilles rupture needs urgent assessment, so don't wait to see whether it settles.

How common is a calf strain in padel?

Very. In the French survey by Lecler and colleagues, the calf and Achilles tendon complex made up 18.8% of injuries, just ahead of the elbow. Studies in the 2023 systematic review of padel injuries found the same pattern: "tennis leg" accounted for 22.1% of injuries by body region in one study. Padel's stop-start movement, short sprints to the net and constant split steps make it a natural calf injury sport.

What is "tennis leg"?

Tennis leg is a tear of the inner (medial) head of the gastrocnemius, the large calf muscle, where it joins its tendon. Radiopaedia describes the usual mechanism as the knee straightening while the ankle is forced upwards, which is exactly the position of a hard push-off from the back foot. It typically affects active people in middle age.

The classic symptoms are a sudden, sharp pain in the back of the calf, sometimes with a snapping sensation, followed by tenderness and swelling. Early on, before swelling develops, there may be a palpable gap. It is usually managed without surgery. The Aspetar review of padel injuries notes the highest occurrence is in poorly conditioned men over 40.

Achilles tendon pain and rupture risk

The Achilles tendon connects the calf muscles to the heel. Two very different problems affect it.

Achilles tendinopathy

This is a gradual problem: pain and stiffness over the tendon that builds over weeks. The professional reference Patient.info describes the common form as tender about 2 to 6 cm above the heel, with risk factors including a rapid increase in activity and changes in footwear or training surface. It often eases as you warm up, which tempts players to keep going, and then flares afterwards.

Achilles rupture

A rupture is sudden. Mayo Clinic lists the feeling of having been kicked in the calf, a popping or snapping sound, pain and swelling near the heel, and difficulty pushing off or standing on your toes. The peak age is 30 to 40, men are up to five times more likely to be affected than women, and recreational sports with running, jumping and sudden starts and stops, such as tennis, carry more risk. Mayo also lists steroid injections and a group of antibiotics called fluoroquinolones among the risk factors, so mention your sport to a doctor if either is suggested.

Calf strain or Achilles? A quick comparison

WhereMiddle or upper calf, often the inner sideLower leg, just above the heel
Feels likeSharp pain, sometimes a snap; tender spot in the muscleLike being kicked or hit from behind; often a pop
AfterwardsPainful to walk, but you can usually still push off a littleHard to push off or stand on tiptoe on that side
What to doStop, first aid, get it assessed if severeStop, keep weight off it, urgent assessment the same day

Patient.info stresses that an Achilles rupture can be missed, and that delayed treatment has worse outcomes. If in doubt, assume it's the tendon and get it checked.

First aid for a calf strain: the first few days

NHS 111 Wales recommends PRICE for the first two to three days after a sprain or strain:

  • Protection: support the injury, for example with supportive shoes.
  • Rest: stop exercising and avoid putting weight on it where possible.
  • Ice: an ice pack or wrapped frozen vegetables for up to 20 minutes every two to three hours.
  • Compression: a bandage for support during the day.
  • Elevation: keep the leg raised on a pillow when you can.

For the first couple of days, avoid heat (hot baths, heat packs), alcohol and massage, which can increase swelling. Once you can move the leg without pain stopping you, keep it moving gently so it doesn't stiffen.

Warming up your calves

The Aspetar review lists inadequate warm-up among padel's injury risk factors, and calves are where a cold start shows up first. Before you sprint for a drop shot, raise your temperature with a jog and skipping, then do ten calf raises, some ankle bounces and a few split steps and short accelerations. A calf raise programme for padel players

Calf strength is your best long-term protection. Mayo Clinic's prevention advice for the Achilles is to stretch and strengthen the calf muscles and to raise training intensity slowly. Do this two or three times a week, moving to the next stage when the current one feels easy and causes no soreness the next day.

StageExerciseSets × reps
1Double-leg calf raise, straight knees, slow down3 × 15
1Double-leg calf raise, knees bent (works the deeper soleus)3 × 15
2Single-leg calf raise on the floor, straight and bent knee3 × 10–12 each
3Single-leg calf raise from a step, lowering below step height over three seconds3 × 10–12 each
4Stage 3 holding a dumbbell or wearing a loaded backpack3 × 8–10 each
5Skipping, pogo hops and split-step hops3 × 20 s

These are general starting points for healthy players. After an injury, follow a physiotherapist's plan; the stages are similar, but the timing depends on the injury.

Older players: why calves need extra care

Age matters here more than almost anywhere else in padel. The French survey found players over 40 had about four times the odds of injury of younger players, the Achilles rupture peak is 30 to 40, and tennis leg is most common in middle-aged men. If you took up padel in your forties or later, build up your number of sessions slowly, never skip the warm-up and treat the calf programme above as part of your padel. Our guide to padel over 40 covers the rest.

Returning to play after a calf strain

NHS 111 Wales says most sprains and strains feel better after about two weeks, but advises avoiding strenuous exercise such as running for up to eight weeks because of the risk of further damage, and severe strains can take months. Work back in stages:

  1. Walk normally without a limp and climb stairs without pain.
  2. Single-leg calf raises on the injured side feel about as strong and comfortable as on the other side.
  3. Jog in a straight line, then side shuffles and gentle changes of direction.
  4. Short sprints, stops and split steps at increasing speed.
  5. Drills before matches. Controlled rallies and footwork work, such as the drills in The Padel Drill Book, before a full match.
  6. A short match, then full play once the calf is no worse the next day.

Smarter positioning also reduces the frantic sprints that cause calf injuries in the first place; The Padel Playbook: Beginner & Intermediate covers where to stand. For the bigger injury picture, see padel injuries.

Get help urgently if…

Stop playing and get same-day help if you suspect an Achilles rupture: a kicked or hit feeling, a pop, and difficulty pushing off or standing on tiptoe. NHS 111 Wales says to go to A&E or call 999 if you heard a crack at the time of injury, the leg has changed shape, the area is numb or tingling, or the skin has turned blue or grey or feels cold. Don't drive yourself. See a GP if a strain is getting worse or isn't improving with self-care.

Frequently asked questions

What does a calf strain feel like in padel?

A sudden sharp pain in the back of the calf during a push-off or sprint, sometimes with a snap, followed by tenderness and swelling. A feeling of being kicked just above the heel points more towards the Achilles tendon.

How long does a calf strain take to heal?

Mild strains often feel better after about two weeks, but NHS advice is to avoid strenuous exercise such as running for up to eight weeks, and severe strains can take months.

Is tennis leg the same as a calf strain?

Tennis leg is a specific calf strain: a tear of the inner head of the gastrocnemius muscle where it meets the tendon, usually during a push-off.

How do I prevent calf injuries in padel?

Warm up properly, build calf strength with progressive calf raises, and increase your playing time gradually, especially if you're over 40.

How can I tell if I've ruptured my Achilles?

Typical signs are a feeling of being kicked in the lower calf, a pop, and difficulty pushing off or standing on your toes. If you suspect it, keep weight off the leg and get assessed the same day.
