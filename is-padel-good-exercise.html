Is padel good exercise? What the research says

4 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 17 Nov 2026

Yes. Padel is a genuinely good way to exercise: moderate aerobic work with short bursts, social enough that people keep coming back, and gentler to start than tennis. It isn't a complete fitness programme on its own, though, and knowing what it does and doesn't do helps you get more out of it.

Not medical advice

This guide explains common patterns in plain language. It can't diagnose you. If the pain is severe, came from a fall or twist, or isn't settling, get it assessed.

How intense is padel?

The Aspetar review of padel sports science (Ellis and colleagues, 2023) describes padel's metabolic demands as mostly moderate and lower than tennis, with studies reporting intensities of about 50% to 70% of maximum heart rate and players covering under 2,000 m in a match. In amateur players, an average heart rate of about 130 beats per minute was recorded over one-hour matches, with nearly 90% of playing time in the aerobic zone (Díaz García and colleagues, 2017). Rallies bring short bursts of sprinting, lunging and jumping on top of that.

Does padel count towards your weekly exercise?

It does. The NHS recommends adults do at least 150 minutes of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week, and lists doubles tennis as an example of moderate activity. Padel sits in the same range for most club players, and a hard match pushes into vigorous. Two or three 60 to 90-minute sessions a week meets the aerobic guideline comfortably.

What padel doesn't cover is the NHS's second recommendation: strengthening activities that work all the major muscle groups on at least two days a week. Padel loads the legs and the playing arm, but not in the way strength training does.

What the research says about health

  • Racket sports and long life. In a study of 80,306 adults in England and Scotland published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine (Oja and colleagues, 2016), people who played racket sports had a 47% lower risk of death from any cause and a 56% lower risk of death from cardiovascular disease than people who didn't. That study is observational and grouped racket sports together, so it can't prove padel itself causes the difference, but it's encouraging.
  • Mental health and wellbeing. A 2025 systematic review (Lauxtermann and Stubbs) found research on padel has mostly looked at performance psychology, such as confidence and match anxiety, while studies of pickleball, another social racket sport, link it to better life satisfaction, happiness and social connection, especially in older adults. Padel-specific wellbeing research is still thin.
  • Calories. An hour of club padel burns roughly 350 to 550 kcal for most adults; see how many calories padel burns.

Why padel works for so many people

Exercise only helps if you keep doing it. Padel is easy to start, the court is small, the ball is slower than in tennis, and it's played in doubles, so it's social by design. That's why it attracts people who've never stuck with a gym. The same review of wellbeing research lists low technical barriers, social engagement and enjoyment among the reasons for padel's popularity.

Frequently asked questions

Is padel a good workout?

Yes. It's moderate aerobic exercise with short bursts of high effort, and research shows it keeps most players in the aerobic zone for most of a match.

Is padel enough exercise on its own?

It can meet the weekly aerobic guideline, but the NHS also recommends strength training on at least two days a week, which padel doesn't replace.

Is padel healthier than the gym?

They do different jobs. Padel is great aerobic, social exercise; the gym builds strength. The best combination is both.

Is padel good exercise for older adults?

Generally yes: it's moderate in intensity, social and easy to start. Build up gradually and add strength training to protect calves, knees and shoulders.

Does padel improve heart health?

Regular moderate exercise is good for the heart, and a large UK study linked racket sports to a 56% lower risk of cardiovascular death, though it couldn't prove cause and effect.
