The víbora: padel's attacking overhead

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

The víbora (Spanish for "viper") is the overhead that bites. Where the bandeja is about keeping the net, the víbora is about pressure: a fast, side-spun ball that stays low off the glass and forces a weak reply. Here is how to hit it, and when to leave it in the bag.

What makes a víbora different

Padel has three main overheads, and they do different jobs:

ShotContactSpinGoal
BandejaUnder and behind the ball, around head heightSlice, controlledKeep the net
VíboraOn the outside of the ball, head height, in frontSide spin plus slice, fasterPressure: a low, awkward ball off the glass
SmashFull stretch, higherFlat or topspin, full powerWin the point or force a weak reply

The key difference from the bandeja is where the racket meets the ball. On a bandeja you cut underneath it. On a víbora you brush around its outside, which gives the side spin that makes the ball skid off the side glass and stay low in the corner.

When to play it

  • An easy lob while you're fairly close to the net. You have time and balance, so you can attack.
  • A lob that's too deep to smash cleanly but not good enough to force you all the way back.
  • Medium-height "dead" balls that sit up in mid-court.

If you're stretched, late or moving backwards, play a bandeja or let the ball bounce instead. The víbora punishes bad positions, including yours.

How to hit it, step by step

  1. Continental grip. The same "hammer" grip you use for volleys and the bandeja.
  2. Turn fully side-on. Shoulders and feet face the side wall. Get there with short side steps, not by backpedalling.
  3. Load the racket. Elbow up around shoulder height, racket behind your head, arm relaxed.
  4. Contact in front and away from the body. Arm extended, roughly at head height. Hit it higher and it becomes a flat smash; keep it at head height and the ball stays low.
  5. Brush around the outside of the ball. Swing forward and across, with a quick turn of the forearm. That creates the side spin and slice.
  6. Finish across your body and recover straight away towards the net with your partner.

Where to aim

  • The corner: into the side glass so the ball runs on into the back glass and comes out low.
  • The opponent's backhand corner, where returning a low, spinning ball is hardest.

The aim is a ball they can't attack, not a winner. A good víbora often wins the point on the next shot, when the reply floats up.

Common mistakes

  1. Hitting too hard. Power without control sends the ball high off the glass, straight back to the opponents.
  2. Contact above the head. That turns it into a flat smash with none of the víbora's bite.
  3. Square stance. Without the side-on turn you can't swing across the ball.
  4. Contact behind the body. Usually caused by late footwork. Move first, then swing.
  5. Abandoning the stroke halfway. Commit to the swing; a half-hit víbora is the easiest ball to counter.

Build it from the bandeja

Most coaches teach the víbora after the bandeja, and it makes sense: same grip, same side-on set-up, similar contact height. Once your bandeja is reliable, start moving the contact point from under the ball to its outside edge and add a little more speed. Feed easy lobs, aim for the side glass, and count how many come out below knee height.

Frequently asked questions

What does víbora mean?

It's Spanish for viper. The name comes from the way the ball "bites" off the glass with side spin.

Is the víbora harder than the bandeja?

Yes. It needs the same set-up plus more racket speed and precise contact. It's usually considered an intermediate-to-advanced shot.

Should beginners learn the víbora?

Learn the bandeja first. The víbora grows naturally out of a solid bandeja.

Where should a víbora land?

Deep in the opponents' corner, using the side glass, so the ball comes out low and close to the wall.

Footwork: the part everyone skips

A víbora is only as good as the position you hit it from. The ball should arrive slightly in front of you and to the side of your hitting shoulder, so you can swing across it with an extended arm.

  1. Read the lob early. As soon as the opponent's racket opens up for a lob, start turning.
  2. Side-step or cross-step back and slightly away from the ball. You want space between your body and the ball, not to be underneath it.
  3. Stop before you swing. A stable base gives you a clean contact; hitting on the run is how víboras end up in the mesh.
  4. Transfer the weight forward through the shot, and let that carry you back towards the net.

Bandeja or víbora? A quick decision guide

SituationPlay
Lob is good and deep, you're moving backwardsBandeja, or let it bounce
Lob is medium, you're balanced and fairly close to the netVíbora
Lob is short and easy near the netSmash
Opponents are already at the netBandeja to the feet or a safe deep ball

When in doubt, choose the bandeja. Losing the net with a safe shot is rare; losing the point with a rushed víbora is common.

How to practise it

  1. Shadow swings. Without a ball, practise turning side-on, loading the racket and swinging across. Ten slow reps, ten at speed.
  2. Hand-fed lobs. A partner throws easy lobs from the net. Play víboras into the side glass and count how many come out below knee height.
  3. Racket-fed lobs from the back. Your partner lobs from the opposite back line. Alternate: one bandeja, one víbora, focusing on changing only the contact point.
  4. Live points. Play points where the back pair may only lob. The net pair earns a bonus point every time a víbora forces a weak reply.

The Padel Drill Book has a full section on lobs and overheads (drills 41–55), including a dedicated side-spin overhead drill.

What a good víbora looks like

  • The ball crosses the net fast and fairly flat.
  • It lands deep, hits the side glass, then the back glass.
  • It comes out low and close to the wall, so the opponent has to dig it out.
  • You and your partner are back at the net before they hit it.
