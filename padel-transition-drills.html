Padel transition drills

12 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 31 Oct 2026

Padel transition drills train the moments when you move forward to the net, or get pushed back from it, so that you and your partner move on the right ball and arrive together. Below are ten drills: four for taking the net, two for the middle of the court, three for holding the net once you're there, and a scoring game that ties it all together.

All the drills below are adapted from The Padel Drill Book: Intermediate & Advanced. We have reworded and shortened them for the web; the book has the full versions, with a court diagram for every drill.

How to take the net in padel (and which balls let you)

Getting to the net leaves your opponents less space to hit into and less time to react, as LTA Padel puts it, so both pairs spend the rally trying to get there. The catch is that you can only move up behind a ball that gives you time. The Padel School names three: a high lob, a fast lob that passes the net players, and a chiquita that makes them volley upwards. A hard drive rarely buys time, because it comes back just as fast.

Three habits run through every drill on this page:

  • Move on the right ball. Your own shot decides whether you can go forward. A ball the opponents can attack sends you back.
  • Stop when they hit. Whatever you're doing, split-step as the opponent makes contact, then move to the ball.
  • Move as a pair. If your partner goes forward, you go forward; if they're pushed back, you go back. Padel Browser describes it as two players joined by an imaginary rope.

Net position itself is roughly two to three metres from the net, as in our positioning guide. These drills suit levels 3 to 6 on the 0–7 scale the book uses.

Padel transition drills for taking the net

1. Chiquita, then close (T2 in the book)

Players: 4–5 · Time: 15 min · Level: 3–5

Set-up: Your pair in the back third of the court, the opponents at the net. A feeder near the net post on their side feeds deep to you.

How it works: The feed sits up after the bounce. Play a slow, low chiquita that drops at a net player's feet. As it crosses the net, you and your partner both take two or three steps forward and split as the net player hits. If the chiquita did its job, their volley comes up; attack it firmly to the middle or the feet. Play the point out. Swap roles after ten rallies.

The key read: Move in only behind a chiquita that stays down. One that sits up above the net tape lets them volley down at you, so stay where you are.

Pass mark: In 10 rallies, your pair reaches the net together 7 times and wins 5 points.

Make it harder: The net players are allowed to let the chiquita bounce and then lob you as you approach.

2. Exit and climb (G10 in the book)

Players: 4 · Time: 15 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: Your pair at the back, the opponents at the net. One of them starts each point by feeding firmly into your back glass.

How it works: Play the ball off the glass with a lob over the net pair, or a low ball if the lob isn't on. If you can see the lob will pass them and make them turn, call "up" and both of you advance side by side. If it's short or they can play an overhead, call "stay" and hold the back. If you win the point at the net after calling "up", it counts double.

Pass mark: The right "up" or "stay" call on 8 of 10 points, judged by the opponents.

Make it harder: The net pair answers good lobs with a counter-lob, so you must arrive balanced.

3. When to follow the drive (T8 in the book)

Players: 4–5 · Time: 15 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: You start a step behind the service line on the right half, your partner level with you on the left, the opponents at the net. A feeder behind them sends you balls that bounce between knee and hip height.

How it works: Drive flat and low at a net player's feet or hip. Watch its height at the net. Below the tape, so they must volley up? Advance together. Above it, they can volley down or find an angle, so stay put or take a step back, and split. Points are played out, ten per set.

The key idea: What makes a drive a good approach is the upward volley it forces, not its speed.

Pass mark: In 10 rallies, you follow only low drives and your pair wins at least 6 points.

Make it harder: The net pair may answer with a chiquita at your feet while you move in.

4. Glass to net in three (T7 in the book)

Players: 4 · Time: 15 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: Your pair at the back, the opponents at the net. Each rally begins with one of them smashing or volleying firmly into your half.

How it works: Ball one only has to survive: let the attack come off the glass and reply safely, with a lob or something low through the centre. The second ball should cause them problems: a lob that gets past the net pair or a chiquita they have to volley up. If it works, move up together and play ball three from the service line or closer. A point won after reaching the net within three shots earns a bonus point.

Pass mark: In 10 rallies, your pair reaches the net within three shots at least 5 times.

Make it harder: The net pair may lob at any time, and you lose the bonus if you're caught moving up behind a weak ball.

Drills for the middle of the court

The area around the service line is where transitions go wrong. The Padel School calls it no-man's land: too far back to volley down, too close to defend. You pass through it; you don't stay there.

5. Back, middle, net (T4 in the book)

Players: 2 · Time: 12 min · Level: 3–5

Set-up: You start at the back on the right with a feeder on the far side. Three cones mark where you stop: 1.5 m off the back glass, on the service line, and at net position.

How it works: Ball one is a deep feed you play after the back glass, a lob or deep drive cross-court; then advance to the cone on the service line. For the second ball, split there as the feeder strikes, and send a low volley or half-volley deep through the centre. Ball three: split at the net cone and volley firmly to the feet or the open corner. Walk back and repeat, six times per set.

Pass mark: 5 sequences out of 6 where all three balls go in and you split at each cone.

Make it harder: The feeder may lob the third ball, so you have to retreat and start again.

6. Out of no-man's land (T6 in the book)

Players: 2–3 · Time: 12 min · Level: 3–5

Set-up: You start on the service line on the right; a feeder is on the far side. Cones mark net position and a spot a metre and a half from your back glass.

How it works: The feeder randomly sends a ball at your feet or at waist height. Play it from where you are. If your ball goes deep and low, move to the net cone; if it's short or high, drop back to the back cone. Your next ball arrives as you do: a drive if you went forward, a lob or deep ball if you went back.

Pass mark: 7 of 8 sequences where you choose the right direction and control the second ball.

Make it harder: The feeder sends the next ball before you reach either cone, so you must split and play from wherever you are.

Net play drills: holding the net once you get there

7. Pounce on the second ball (T5 in the book)

Players: 4–5 · Time: 15 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: Your pair at the net, the opponents at the back. Standing behind them, a feeder opens each rally with a medium-paced ball to you.

How it works: Play a firm first volley deep into a corner or at an opponent's feet. Read their reply as it leaves the racket. If it's weak (a short lob or a floater), the nearer of you steps in and attacks it early: a smash for the short lob, a firm volley for the floater. If it's a good reply, stay patient and play a neutral ball deep.

Pass mark: 8 of 10 weak replies attacked early, and at least 6 of those points won.

Make it harder: The opponents score double for any point they win after you attacked a reply that was actually good.

8. Answer the chiquita (T9 in the book)

Players: 4 · Time: 15 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: Your pair at the net, about three metres back. The opponents are in the back half and start each rally with a chiquita.

How it works: The target player steps in and plays it early, as a volley or half-volley. Your answer has to stay low as well: either a soft block to the feet of an opponent coming forward, or a deep volley that pins both of them back. Your partner holds net position and splits. If your reply rises, you both step back and get ready to defend.

Pass mark: 7 of 10 chiquitas answered below the opponents' waist, with your pair still at the net.

Make it harder: The opponents follow their chiquita in to the net.

9. Give up the net together (T3 in the book)

Players: 4 · Time: 15 min · Level: 3–5

Transitions go both ways. When a lob beats you, the net is gone, and the only question is whether you lose it as a pair or as two individuals.

Set-up: Your pair at the net; the opponents at the back with a basket, starting each rally with a lob.

How it works: The lob lands behind your service line. The player it passes calls "back" as soon as it's out of reach. You both turn and drop back at the same distance apart, and whoever is on the ball's side plays it once it has come off the back glass. The opponents move up behind their lob, and the point is played out.

Pass mark: 7 of 8 lobs where you retreat as a pair and land your first defensive ball.

Make it harder: The opponents mix in shorter lobs that you have to play as a bandeja, so the call becomes a real decision.

A game to finish: double points at the net

10. Double points at the net (T10 in the book)

Players: 4 · Time: 20 min · Level: 3–6

Set-up: A standard 2 v 2, starting with a serve. A pair counts as "at the net" if both players are inside their service line, nearer the net, as the point finishes.

How it works: An ordinary point is worth 1. Win it with both of you at the net and it's worth 2. Win it after climbing from the back to the net within the same rally and it's worth 3. Play to 21; change serve every four points and ends at 11. Between games, each pair agrees one transition rule, for example "we only move up behind a lob that passes".

Pass mark: Win the game, or collect four or more 3-point transitions.

Make it harder: If you lose a point while split (one back, one forward), the opponents get a bonus point.

Common transition mistakes

  • Moving up behind a ball that floats. If the opponents can volley down, you have nothing to approach behind.
  • Running through the split step. Stop as they hit, even if you're short of where you wanted to be, and play from there.
  • Stopping in no-man's land. Pass through the service-line area. The Padel School suggests attacking from around the second post or a step closer.
  • One partner moves, the other doesn't. The diagonal gap this opens is the easiest target in padel.
  • Staying back after a good lob. If the lob makes them turn, that is your ticket to the net. LTA Padel advises using the time a lob buys to move forward.

Frequently asked questions

How do you take the net in padel?

Play a ball that gives you time, such as a lob that passes the net players or a chiquita that makes them volley upwards, then move forward with your partner and split-step as the opponent hits. Don't move up behind a ball they can attack.

What is no-man's land in padel?

The area around the service line, between the back of the court and net position. From there you can't volley down and you're too close to defend comfortably, so pass through it rather than stopping.

When should you leave the net?

When a lob has clearly beaten you and you can't play an overhead. Call it early and retreat together with your partner, then defend off the glass and look to rebuild.

How far from the net should you stand at the net?

About two to three metres. That is close enough to volley at the opponents' feet and far enough back to cover a lob.

Can I practise transitions with two players?

Yes. Back, middle, net and Out of no-man's land need only you and a feeder. The approach and net-holding drills need four.
