Padel for seniors: questions and answers for players over 60

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel for seniors works because the court is small, it's always played in doubles and the walls keep the ball in play, so you get long, sociable rallies without needing the speed of a younger player. LTA Padel describes it as low impact with a short learning curve, and competitions now run for age groups up to 80+. Below are straight answers on safety, the benefits the evidence actually supports, falls near the glass, how to adapt the game and where to find sessions for older players.

Not medical advice

If you haven't exercised for a while, or have a heart condition, joint replacement, balance problems or another medical condition, talk to your GP before you start.

Is padel safe for older adults?

For most healthy older adults, padel played sensibly is a reasonable choice, but be aware that the research hasn't looked closely at this age group. A 2025 systematic review of padel and wellbeing (Lauxtermann and Stubbs) found that padel studies skew young, and none of the ones it included examined older adults. Injury studies in younger and middle-aged players show that injuries are common, mainly overuse problems in the elbow, legs, shoulder and back; our padel injuries guide summarises them.

So the honest answer is: padel isn't proven safe or unsafe for seniors specifically. What we do know is how to reduce the usual risks, which is what the rest of this page is about. Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust advises older adults to speak to a doctor first if they haven't exercised for a while or have medical conditions, and to choose exercise that suits their fitness level.

Can you start padel at 60, 65 or 70?

Yes. LTA Padel notes that the smaller court and doubles format mean padel doesn't require high fitness, especially at recreational level, and that many clubs run beginner intro sessions with on-site coaches. Starting with a few lessons is the best investment you can make: you learn to use the walls, which saves running, and good habits from day one. Our first time playing padel guide explains what to expect from your first session.

Previous racket-sport experience helps but isn't necessary. Tennis players should know that some tennis habits, such as big swings and chasing every ball before it reaches the glass, cost more energy in padel than they need to.

What are the health benefits of padel for seniors?

The NHS recommends that adults aged 65 and over aim to be physically active every day, do activities that improve strength, balance and flexibility on at least two days a week, and do at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity if already active. Regular padel can count towards the activity target.

Beyond that, be careful with claims. Heart-rate studies of padel have mostly involved younger players, and no controlled trials have tested padel's health effects in older adults. The strongest point in its favour is social: LTA Padel says the sociable format can help combat isolation and loneliness in later life, and doubles play means you're never on court alone. The wider case for regular exercise in later life is well established; padel is simply one enjoyable way to get it.

Does padel help balance?

It might, but it hasn't been tested. Padel involves constant small steps, changes of direction and reaching, which all challenge balance. The NHS says exercises to improve strength, balance and flexibility help make you stronger and more confident on your feet, which matters if you've fallen or worry about falling. The safest approach is to treat padel as a complement to dedicated balance and strength work, not a replacement for it. Simple options include standing on one leg while you brush your teeth, heel-to-toe walking and sit-to-stands from a chair.

What about falls and the glass walls?

The walls are padel's best friend for older players, but they're also hard. A few habits keep you safe:

  • Let the ball come to you. Wait for it to come off the back glass rather than chasing it into the corner.
  • Never back-pedal. Turn sideways and use side steps to move back for a lob. Walking backwards is an easy way to lose your footing.
  • Clear the court. Pocket or push loose balls to the side between points; stepping on a ball is an easy way to fall.
  • Wear padel shoes. Proper court shoes grip the sand-filled grass without sticking; running shoes are not designed for it. See padel shoes.
  • Protect your eyes. Balls come off the glass at awkward angles; some players choose protective glasses. See padel eye protection.

How can older players modify the game?

Padel adapts well. Agree the style of game with your group before you start:

  • Skip the smash. A controlled bandeja or a ball off the glass keeps the shoulder happier and the rally going.
  • Play shorter formats. A social Americano with short rounds gives you rest between matches and new partners each round.
  • Use the breaks. Take a drink and a breather every time you change ends.
  • Choose a lighter, softer racket. It's kinder to the elbow and wrist.
  • Look for 50+ and "walking padel" sessions. Some clubs advertise these; ask how the format works before you go.

Drills are a gentle way to build skills between games. The Padel Drill Book includes drills from level 0 upwards, and The Padel Playbook: Beginner & Intermediate explains the grip, the walls and where to stand, which is where older beginners gain the most.

Where can seniors find padel sessions and clubs?

LTA Padel says many clubs now run sessions for older age groups, including programmes such as Soul Padel's "Soul Timers" for older players regardless of previous racket experience, and its site has a "find a padel court" tool. Clubs also run inclusive sessions for people with conditions such as Parkinson's, and booking apps and club staff can match you with partners of a similar age and ability.

If you get the competitive bug, there's plenty of it. LTA competitions run for age groups from 35+ to 80+, with ranking categories for 40+, 50+ and 60+, and the international federation's senior events include a 60+ category; the 2026 FIP Senior World Cup was scheduled for Buenos Aires in September 2026. Legends Padel in the UK runs camps and competitions for players aged 60 and over.

When should an older player stop and get help?

If you get chest pain while playing, stop, sit down and rest. Moving Medicine, from the Faculty of Sport and Exercise Medicine UK, advises calling 999 if chest pain doesn't settle with rest. Stop playing and seek urgent help as well for severe breathlessness, feeling faint or dizzy, or any fall where you hit your head or can't put weight on a limb. For niggles that don't settle within a couple of weeks, see your GP or a physiotherapist before you play again.

Frequently asked questions

Is padel good for seniors?

For most healthy older adults, yes: it's sociable, can be played at a gentle pace and counts towards the NHS activity target. Padel-specific health research in older adults is still lacking, so start gradually and check with your GP if you have a medical condition.

Can a 70-year-old play padel?

Many do. Competitions run for age groups up to 80+, and clubs offer sessions for older players. Start with lessons and adapt the game, for example by skipping smashes and playing shorter formats.

Is padel easier than tennis for older players?

Many find it easier to start: the court is smaller, it's always doubles and the walls keep rallies going. It still involves quick movement, so warm up and build up gradually.

How do I avoid falling when playing padel?

Turn and side-step back for lobs instead of walking backwards, let balls come off the glass, keep loose balls off the court and wear proper padel shoes.

Are there padel sessions for over 60s?

Yes. Many clubs run older-adult and social sessions, and there are 60+ competitions at club, national and international level. Ask your local club or use LTA Padel's court finder in the UK.
