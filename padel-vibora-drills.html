Padel víbora drills

9 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 31 Oct 2026

The best padel víbora drills train placement and choice rather than power: a side-spun overhead that stays low in the corner, played only on a lob that allows it. The eight drills below cover the corner pocket, disguise, building the point for the víbora and defending it from the other side.

All eight are adapted from The Padel Drill Book: Intermediate & Advanced. The code after each name is its number in the book.

What you are training in víbora practice

The víbora is the attacking cousin of the bandeja. LTA Padel describes it as an overhead struck around head or shoulder height with side spin, so the ball kicks low and sideways off the floor and walls. Our víbora guide covers the stroke itself. The coaching points that matter in these drills:

  • Brush the outside of the ball. NOX and Padel Browser both describe contact on the side of the ball rather than underneath it, which is what makes it different from the bandeja.
  • Contact in front, around head height. Higher and it turns into a flat smash; behind you and it floats.
  • Placement over pace. The Padel School warns that trying to hit the víbora as hard as possible is a common error. The aim is a low ball the opponent has to lift.
  • Recover to the net. A víbora that keeps you stuck at the service line gives away the attack it created.

Learn the bandeja first. If it is not reliable yet, start with our padel bandeja drills.

Víbora exercises for placement

1. Víbora into the pocket (O3 in the book)

Players: 2–3 · Time: 15 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: You at the net on the right. The feeder lobs from the opposite end. Mark a pocket roughly two and a half metres wide along the side glass in your cross-court corner, near the back. C can stand there to defend.

How it works: The feeder lobs to your forehand side at a comfortable height, so you can meet it at about head height, slightly to the side, without reaching. Swing across and down through the ball, adding slice and side spin, so it bounces in the pocket and runs into the side glass. The feeder or C watches how it comes out: if it jumps off the glass towards the middle, it does not count, even after a good bounce. Recover to the net after every ball. Left-side players add a set from their side.

Pass mark: Six of ten land in the pocket and leave the side glass lower than the defender's waist.

Make it harder: C returns every víbora, and you score only when C is forced into a lob or a mistake.

2. Three-lane bandeja call (O1)

Players: 2–3 · Time: 12 min · Level: 3–5

Set-up: You at the net on the right, the feeder on the far baseline. Cones divide the far back third into left, middle and right lanes.

How it works: In the book this is a bandeja drill, and it is here because the víbora is built on the same base. The feeder calls a lane as the lob peaks, and you steer a deep overhead into it, staying side-on and changing direction only with the racket face. Players who direct the ball from a stable set-up find it much easier to aim a víbora at a corner later.

Pass mark: Nine of twelve in the called lane, with you back at the net each time.

Make it harder: The call comes just before your forward swing.

3. Late-call overhead (O9)

Players: 2 · Time: 15 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: You at the net on the right, the feeder on the far baseline. Mark three zones: deep middle (bandeja), cross-court corner (víbora) and open court (smash).

How it works: The feeder lobs a ball where all three overheads are possible. Turn, racket up, free arm pointing at the ball. When you are set, the feeder calls "bandeja", "víbora" or "smash" and you play it into the matching zone. Your set-up should look identical each time; only the swing path and the contact point change. This is what makes a víbora hard to read.

Pass mark: Twenty of twenty-seven correct shots in the right zone.

Make it harder: The feeder also calls "leave", and you let the ball pass to the back glass.

Padel víbora drills in live play

4. Set up the revés finisher (P1)

Players: 4 · Time: 15 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: A (right) and B (left) start at the net. C and D defend from the back, and every point begins with C lobbing towards A.

How it works: A builds with deep volleys, never forcing a winner. B waits for a lob on the left half that is short enough to attack, then plays a víbora into the corner or an x3. Anything else gets a deep bandeja. B's overhead winners count two, other points one.

Pass mark: Win to eleven, and win four or more of those points with B's overhead.

Make it harder: C and D lob anywhere, including the middle.

5. Cover the counter-lob together (O10)

Players: 4–5 · Time: 15 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: You and your partner at the net. The feeder lobs the first ball, and C waits near the far back glass to lob it back.

How it works: Play a víbora or bandeja deep towards C, then step forward at once. A good víbora often forces a lob in reply. C lobs back over one of you or the middle, and whoever it is passing makes the call, "mine" or "switch", before it peaks. On a switch, the partner crosses behind and the lobbed player slides across. Play the next ball live.

Pass mark: Six of eight sequences finish with one of you on each side and the next ball controlled.

Make it harder: C may drive low instead of lobbing.

6. Only the lob that passes (T1)

Players: 4 · Time: 15 min · Level: 3–5

Set-up: A and B play from the back, lobbing against C and D at the net. For víbora practice, take one of the net places.

How it works: In the book's harder version, the net pair is allowed a bandeja or víbora on every reachable lob, and the back pair loses two points if caught moving in behind a short lob. That makes it real víbora practice against live lobs: decide early whether the lob is short enough to attack, then send the víbora deep into the corner. The back pair only advances behind lobs that truly pass.

Pass mark: The back pair moves in behind at least six lobs in twelve rallies and never behind a short one, so at the net your job is to make every short lob cost them.

Make it harder: Swap roles after twelve rallies so both pairs practise both sides.

Defending the víbora

7. Rescue the corner pocket (D3)

Players: 2–4 · Time: 12 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: You start on the right, about two metres from the back glass. The feeder stands at the far net with a basket, left of centre.

How it works: The feeder plays víbora-style balls, sliced and spinning, that bounce close to your side wall and slide into the corner. When the ball is going to strike the side glass low and stay there, step forward and take it before the wall. When it bounces up enough to come off the side glass around hip height, let the glass do the work and lob cross-court. Practising against the víbora teaches you what a good one looks like, and why a flat one is easy to defend.

Pass mark: Seven replies in ten go deep or into the feeder's feet.

Make it harder: The feeder adds balls that reach the back glass too, so you read two walls at once.

8. Alone at the back (D6)

Players: 3 · Time: 15 min · Level: 4–6

Set-up: One player starts in the middle, about two metres from the back glass. C and D hold the far net.

How it works: The lone defender may only lob. C and D can volley or play bandejas and víboras, but flat put-away smashes are banned. The defender wins the point when a lob makes the net pair turn round and let the ball run to their glass; the net pair wins on any error or clean winner. For the víbora players, every reachable lob is a chance to keep the ball low in a corner. Rotate every eight points.

Pass mark: The defender wins three of eight points or sends the pair back four times. At the net, keep them below that.

Make it harder: The net pair may smash once per point.

Common víbora mistakes

  • Hitting it flat and hard. The ball comes off the glass at a nice height for the defender. Spin is what keeps it low.
  • Contact too high. It becomes a flat smash without the víbora's bite.
  • Square shoulders. Without the side-on turn you can't swing across the ball.
  • Playing it on every lob. If the lob is too deep or high to attack, play the bandeja.
  • Going for winners. A good víbora wins the next ball, when the reply floats up.

Frequently asked questions

How do I practise the víbora?

Have a partner feed medium lobs while you stay at the net, and aim every víbora at a marked corner pocket by the side glass. Count only the balls that come off the glass low. Then add a drill where the shot is called late, so you learn to disguise it.

When should I play a víbora instead of a bandeja?

When the lob is short or medium and you have time and balance to attack it. If it pushes you back or is too high, the bandeja is the safer choice.

Where should the víbora land?

Deep in the opponents' corner, using the side glass, so the ball skids and comes out low. Padel Browser suggests the corner behind the opponent's backhand.

Is the víbora harder than the bandeja?

Yes. It needs the same set-up plus more racket speed and precise contact on the outside of the ball. NOX describes it as a shot for medium to high-level players.

How do you defend a víbora?

Stay a step inside the court rather than backing into the corner. If it skids low, meet it before the side glass; if it sits up, let it come off the glass and lob.
