Can you serve overarm in padel? The waist-height rule explained

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

No: you cannot serve overarm in padel. At the moment you strike the ball it must be at or below your waist, and at least one of your feet must be touching the ground (FIP Rule 6.4). The ball must also be bounced in your own service area first (Rule 6.2), which is why every padel serve is an underarm or sidearm stroke.

The short answer

Bounce the ball inside your service box, with at least one foot behind the service line, then hit it with the ball at or below your waist and at least one foot on the ground. Overarm serves, contact above the waist and jump serves are all faults under Rule 7a.

What the rules say

The serve is covered by FIP Rule 6. Here are the parts that decide how you serve:

  • Rule 6.1, where you stand. The server stands with at least one foot behind the service line, inside their service box (between the extension of the central line and the side wall), and stays there until the ball has been served.
  • Rule 6.2, the bounce. Before striking it, you bounce the ball on the ground inside your own box, behind the service line. The ball may not cross the service line or the imaginary central line before it is struck; if it does, that is a fault under Rule 7a.
  • Rule 6.3, the lines. You may not touch the service line or the imaginary central line with either foot.
  • Rule 6.4, height and feet. At contact, the ball must be at or below waist height, and at least one foot must be in contact with the ground.
  • Rule 6.5, the target. The serve goes diagonally into the receiver's box and must land within its lines. You start each game from the right side and alternate boxes.
  • Rule 6.6, what counts as a serve. Any contact with the ball, or an intentional attempt to hit it, counts as a serve.

Breaking any of Rules 6.1 to 6.6 is a service fault under Rule 7a. Swinging and missing is a fault under Rule 7b. Two faults in a row lose the point (Rule 13q).

Good serve

Ball bounced behind the service line in your box, struck at or below waist height with one foot on the ground, landing in the receiver's box (Rules 6.2, 6.4, 6.5). Underarm or sidearm, flat or with spin.

Fault

Contact above the waist, overarm serving or jumping so both feet leave the ground (Rules 6.4 and 7a). Stepping on the service line or central line (Rules 6.3 and 7a). Swinging and missing (Rule 7b).

How to judge waist height

The FIP rulebook sets the limit at waist height but does not define the waist in centimetres or by a body landmark. In practice, players and umpires judge it against the server's own body, so a tall player can make contact a little higher than a short player. The common club reading is that the waist sits roughly between the belly button and the top of the hip bone. That is convention, not an FIP definition.

A few practical checks help:

  • Your elbow is a rough guide. If your hitting arm is bent upwards at contact, you are probably above the waist.
  • Bending the knees lowers your waist, too. Crouching does not let you hit higher; the waist moves down with you.
  • Do not let the ball bounce too high. Many accidental faults happen because the ball is bounced hard and struck on the way up, above the waist. Drop it softly and let it fall back towards hip height.

Real match situations

The tennis player's serve

A new player throws the ball up and serves overarm, tennis style. Verdict: fault (Rules 6.2, 6.4 and 7a). There was no bounce and the contact was far above the waist.

The high-contact sidearm serve

You bounce the ball well, then hit it at chest height with a flat, sidearm swing. Verdict: fault (Rules 6.4 and 7a). The swing shape is fine; the contact height is not.

The jump serve

You hop forward as you swing and both feet are off the ground at contact. Verdict: fault (Rules 6.4 and 7a). One foot has to be on the ground when the racket meets the ball. Lifting your back heel or swinging your back leg through is fine as long as the other foot stays down.

Stepping on the line

As you serve, your front toe lands on the service line. Verdict: fault (Rules 6.3 and 7a). The same applies to touching the imaginary extension of the central line.

The fluffed bounce

You drop the ball, it bounces badly off your foot, and you catch it without swinging. Rule 6.6 says a serve happens when you make contact with the ball or intentionally attempt to hit it. Catching it without a swing is neither, so the rulebook does not treat it as a serve. Just reset and bounce again, keeping within the 20-second limit between points (Rule 2).

The air shot

You bounce the ball, swing and miss it completely. Verdict: fault (Rules 6.6 and 7b). An intentional attempt counts as a serve even without contact.

Common misconceptions

  • "The serve must be underarm." The rule is about height, not swing shape. A sidearm serve is fine as long as contact is at or below the waist (Rule 6.4).
  • "You can serve without bouncing the ball if you hit it below the waist." No. Rule 6.2 requires the bounce in your own box first.
  • "You have to bounce it exactly once." The rulebook requires a bounce before contact but does not specify a number of bounces. Keep it brisk and within the 20-second limit (Rule 2).
  • "Spin serves are illegal." The serve rules govern height, feet, the bounce and where the ball lands. Nothing in them restricts spin or pace.
  • "You can walk in as you serve." Rule 6.1 says you stay in position until the serve is struck. The rulebook does not cover movement in more detail than Rules 6.1 to 6.4, so keep your feet within the box and off the lines until contact.

Social games vs official matches

With an umpire, the umpire judges the waist and the footwork. In social padel, the receiving pair usually calls an obviously high serve, but it is one of the most argued calls in the game because the waist is not a fixed line. Two habits keep it friendly: agree before you start that clear overhead serves are faults and borderline ones are given to the server, and if a regular partner keeps serving high, tell them between games rather than mid-point. Those are conventions; the FIP rule is simply at or below the waist.

A legal serve that works

Because you cannot hit down on the ball, the padel serve is about placement and depth, not power. Most good servers aim deep into the receiver's box so the ball runs onto the glass, or wide at the side glass so the receiver is pulled out of position.

  1. Stand side-on inside your service box, with at least one foot behind the service line and clear of the central line.
  2. Drop the ball softly slightly in front of you and to your racket side.
  3. Let it fall back to around hip height after the bounce.
  4. Swing low to high with a continental grip, keeping your front foot on the ground.
  5. Move forward to the net after the serve to take your volley position.

Frequently asked questions

Can you serve overhead in padel?

No. The ball must be at or below waist height at contact (FIP Rule 6.4). An overhead serve is a fault under Rule 7a.

Do you have to bounce the ball before serving in padel?

Yes. Rule 6.2 says the ball is bounced on the ground inside your own box, behind the service line, before you strike it.

Can you jump when serving in padel?

No. At least one foot must be in contact with the ground at contact (Rule 6.4).

Is a sidearm serve legal in padel?

Yes, as long as contact is at or below the waist and the other serve rules are met.

What is the penalty for a high serve?

It is a fault (Rule 7a). If it was your second serve, it is a double fault and you lose the point (Rule 13q).
