Best padel rackets for beginners (2026)

5 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

Your first padel racket should make the game easier, not harder. That means a round or soft teardrop shape, a manageable weight, a soft face and core, and a price that doesn't hurt if you end up wanting something else in a year. Here are six current rackets that tick those boxes.

What makes a racket beginner-friendly

  • Round shape (or a soft teardrop): the biggest, most forgiving sweet spot.
  • Manageable weight, roughly 345–375 g for most adults, with lighter options for smaller players.
  • Low or medium balance: easy to swing and gentle on the elbow.
  • Fibreglass face and soft core: comfortable, with easy power on slow swings.

What to avoid as a beginner

  • Diamond-shaped, head-heavy pro rackets. Powerful in the right hands, unforgiving in yours.
  • Very stiff, full-carbon rackets if you have any arm or elbow trouble.
  • Spending top-of-the-range money before you know your style. A mid-range racket and a few lessons will improve your game more.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I spend on my first padel racket?

Most good beginner rackets cost roughly €50–100. You don't need a premium racket to learn the game well.

Round or teardrop for a beginner?

Round is the safest choice for control and forgiveness. A soft teardrop suits beginners who want a little more power.

Do beginners need a carbon racket?

No. Fibreglass faces are softer and more comfortable, which suits most beginners better.

Lighter players and juniors

If you're smaller, have less arm strength or have had elbow trouble, look at the lighter end: around 340–355 g for adults, or a dedicated junior racket for children. A lighter racket is quicker at the net and easier on the arm, and the small loss of power matters far less than control when you're learning.

Is last year's model worth it?

Often, yes. Brands release new versions every year, and the previous model is frequently discounted. For a beginner, the differences between one year and the next are usually small. What matters is shape, weight, balance and materials, so check those specs rather than the year on the frame.

Before you buy: a five-point checklist

  1. Round or soft teardrop shape.
  2. Weight you can swing comfortably for an hour and a half.
  3. Low or medium balance.
  4. Fibreglass face and a soft core.
  5. Try it first if you can: many clubs rent demo rackets.

Look after your racket

  • Add an overgrip and replace it regularly for comfort and grip.
  • Use edge protector tape so scrapes on the glass and floor don't damage the frame.
  • Keep it out of hot cars: heat can affect the core and the face.

When to upgrade

Upgrade when your technique is consistent and you know what you want more of. If you want more power for overheads, try a teardrop with medium balance. If you want more feel, try a slightly firmer core. If you're happy, don't upgrade just because a new model came out.

Common questions

Can I use a tennis racket for padel?

No. Padel is played with a solid, stringless racket with holes in the face. A tennis racket is too long, and padel balls are slightly less pressurised than tennis balls, so the game is designed around the padel racket.

How long does a beginner racket last?

That depends on how often you play and how much the racket hits the glass and floor. For a beginner playing once or twice a week, a good racket often lasts a season or more. If the face cracks or the frame is damaged, it's time to replace it.

Should I buy a racket or rent one first?

If you're not sure you'll keep playing, rent or borrow first. Once you're playing regularly, your own racket gives you consistency: the same weight, balance and feel every session, which helps you improve faster.

Do pros' rackets help beginners?

Usually not. Professional models tend to be stiffer, more head-heavy and less forgiving. They're built for players who hit the sweet spot consistently. As a beginner, a forgiving round racket will help you more.
