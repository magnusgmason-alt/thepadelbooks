Padel video analysis: how to film yourself and what to look for

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel video analysis is the cheapest coaching you will ever get: a phone behind the back glass, one recorded set and an honest hour on the sofa will show you things you never feel on court. Here is how to film yourself playing padel, step by step, from asking permission and placing the camera to the positions, movements and numbers worth looking for.

Why padel video analysis works

On court you feel your shots. On video you see your positions, and the two rarely match. The Padel School's coaches use video because it is much more powerful to show a player what they are doing than to describe it, and slow motion lets you see the exact moment that went wrong.

Research supports the idea with some caveats. A 2022 systematic review by Mödinger, Woll and Wagner found that video-based feedback seems to help motor learning in physical education, mostly when it was paired with verbal feedback on key errors, and that watching an expert model sometimes helped more than watching yourself. In one study, combining both worked best. That shapes the method below: film yourself, compare with a good model, and turn what you see into one clear instruction.

How to film yourself playing padel: step by step

  1. Ask first. Check your club's rules on filming, and ask everyone on your court before you record. Players on neighbouring courts may end up in the frame too, so angle the camera to avoid them where you can. Rules on filming and sharing videos of people vary by country and club; if in doubt, keep the footage private and don't post other players without their permission.
  2. Charge up and clear space. A modern phone is enough. The Padel School's practical tip is to make sure you have enough battery and storage for a full set or match. An action camera with a wide-angle lens helps where the space behind the glass is tight.
  3. Place the camera behind the back glass, centred and high. A camera-angle guide from Visu recommends a position behind the back glass, centred on the court, roughly 3 metres up for clips and up to 4 metres for analysis. Below about 2 metres the glass reflects too much, and you may film yourself instead of the court. A tripod or a flexible mount on the fence keeps the angle the same every time.
  4. Frame wide. Get the whole court, both pairs and some headroom above the mesh so lobs and overheads stay in shot. Visu advises against digital zoom, which costs resolution; shooting slightly too wide is better than too tight, because you can crop later.
  5. For technique, change the angle. To study a single shot rather than the rally, The Padel School suggests filming from the net post on your open side, the forehand or bandeja side, so you see the front of your body rather than your back.
  6. Record a whole set. Five minutes of highlights shows your best shots. A full set shows your habits, which is what you are after.
  7. Watch it twice. Once as a fan, to get the emotion out of the way. Then again with one question and a notepad.
  8. Pick one fix and film again in four weeks. Review is only useful if you check whether the change shows up in real matches.

What to look for in your padel videos

On the second watch, follow yourself, not the ball. These are the habits that video reveals best:

Look atGood signWarning sign
Pair positioningBoth at the net or both at the back, moving togetherOne up, one back; long spells around the service line
Split stepA small hop as the opponent hits, landing ready to moveFlat-footed, or still moving when the ball is struck
Contact pointBall met in front of the body, side-on for overheadsBall behind you, cramped, or hit from the hip
Net positionAbout two metres off the net, racket up between shotsToo close to cover lobs, or racket dropping
Recovery after an overheadMoving forward to the net straight awayAdmiring the shot, then getting caught by the reply
Off the back glassTurning early, letting the ball come off and dropRushing the ball before it reaches the glass
The middleA clear call and one player taking itBoth moving, or both leaving it

Padel stats worth counting

Numbers turn "I played badly" into something you can fix. Count one or two of these per set; trying to count everything means you watch nothing.

Apps and tools

You don't need specialist software to start. Your phone's own camera and photo apps let you play back in slow motion and scrub frame by frame. If you want more, general sports video-analysis and editing apps let you draw lines, compare two clips side by side or tag moments as you watch; features vary, so check what a given app actually does before you pay. Some clubs have installed fixed court cameras that record matches, so ask at reception whether yours does. Whatever the tool, the method is the same: one question, a notepad, one fix.

Compare yourself with a model

Because watching an expert can help as much as watching yourself, put a clip of a good player beside your own. Pick a professional of a similar build playing the same side, and compare one thing: where they stand when their partner is lobbing, or how early they turn for a bandeja. Our guide on how to learn padel from watching the pros lists what to watch and what not to copy.

Where video reviews go wrong

  • Filming from inside the court or at ground level. You get reflections, half a court and no sense of spacing.
  • Watching only the highlights. Your best shots aren't the problem.
  • Trying to fix everything. Pick one habit. In the classic definition of deliberate practice, the value comes from feedback acted on, not feedback collected.
  • Forgetting your partner. Watch the pair, not just yourself; many errors start with a position, not a swing.
  • No follow-up. Without a second recording you don't know whether the fix worked.

Turn what you see into practice

Every review should end with a drill. If the video shows you are late to the net after a lob, train transitions; if it shows weak overheads, train the bandeja. Frequently asked questions

Where should I put my phone to film padel?

Behind the back glass, centred on the court and as high as you can manage, ideally around 3 metres. Low positions pick up reflections from the glass. Frame the whole court with some headroom for lobs.

Do I need permission to film a padel match?

Ask your club and everyone on your court before you record, and avoid filming neighbouring courts. Rules vary by country and club, so keep the footage private unless everyone agrees to it being shared.

What should I look for when I watch myself play padel?

Your positions more than your shots: whether you move with your partner, your split step, where you meet the ball, your distance from the net and how you recover after overheads.

Which padel stats should I track?

Start with unforced errors and winners for one set. Add first serves in, lobs that let you reach the net, or golden points won once you have a baseline.

How often should I film my padel game?

About once a month works well: often enough to check that a fix shows up in matches, rarely enough that you have time to practise between recordings.
