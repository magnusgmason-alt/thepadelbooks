Hindrance in padel: shouting, distraction and interference

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

In padel, hindering an opponent while they are playing a shot costs you the point if it is deliberate, and leads to a let (point replayed) if it is involuntary (FIP Rule 11). A second involuntary hindrance by the same pair also gives the point to the opponents. Separate let rules cover outside interruptions such as a ball rolling in from the next court (FIP Rule 10).

The short answer

Deliberately hindering an opponent's shot loses you the point; an accidental hindrance means the point is replayed, but a repeat by the same pair loses the point (FIP Rule 11). Outside interruptions, such as a stray ball entering the court, give a let, provided you call it immediately (FIP Rules 10b and 10e).

What the rules say

Rule 11: interference. Hindrance means a player interfering with an opponent who is executing a shot. The rulebook splits it into two kinds:

  • Deliberate: the point goes to the opponents.
  • Involuntary: a let is called and the point is replayed. If the same pair causes an involuntary hindrance again, the point goes to the opponents.

Rule 10: let points. A point is replayed when the ball splits (10a); a foreign object enters the court (10b); play is interrupted by unexpected circumstances unrelated to the players (10c); or the ball hits an object on the opponents' side, such as another ball, and that object moves so that it could interfere or endanger someone (10d).

How to claim it. A player who wants a let must tell the umpire immediately. If play continues, the right to the let is lost (10e). And if a let request turns out to be invalid, the player who asked loses the point (10f). That last rule matters: stopping play for a let that isn't one is expensive.

Let (point replayed)

A ball from the next court rolls in (Rule 10b). The ball splits (10a). An accidental hindrance, the first time for that pair (Rule 11). A ball you hit strikes a stray ball on the opponents' side and sends it moving where it could interfere (10d).

Point lost

Deliberately hindering an opponent's shot (Rule 11). A second involuntary hindrance by the same pair (Rule 11). Stopping play for a let request that turns out to be invalid (Rule 10f).

What counts as hindrance

The FIP rulebook defines hindrance by its effect, interfering with an opponent executing a shot, rather than listing specific actions. So the question is always: did something a player did get in the way of the opponent playing the ball? Whether it was deliberate or involuntary is a judgement call for the umpire, or for the players in a social game.

The rulebook doesn't specifically cover talking to your partner during a point. It doesn't ban communication, and calls such as "mine", "yours" or "leave it" are a normal part of doubles. The sensible reading is that ordinary communication with your partner is fine, while noise aimed at putting off an opponent as they hit is exactly the kind of thing Rule 11 is there for.

Real match situations

An opponent shouts as you hit an overhead

If the shout is aimed at you and interferes with your shot, it is hindrance under Rule 11. If the umpire judges it deliberate, you win the point. If they judge it involuntary, for example a startled yell, the point is replayed, and a second such incident from the same pair gives you the point.

Your partner calls "yours" as the ball crosses

Not hindrance. You are talking to your own partner, not interfering with an opponent's shot (Rule 11). The FIP rulebook doesn't address partner communication at all, and it does not prohibit it.

A ball from the next court rolls across the back of your court

That is a foreign object entering the court, so it is a let (FIP Rule 10b). Call it straight away. If you play on and then complain after losing the rally, you have lost the right to the let (FIP Rule 10e).

A spare ball is lying on the opponents' side and your shot hits it

If your ball strikes an object on the opponents' ground that is not part of the court, your return is still valid (FIP Rule 14f). But if that object then moves in a way that could interfere with play or endanger someone, the point is a let (FIP Rule 10d).

A loose ball sits on your own side during the rally

The FIP rulebook doesn't specifically cover a stationary ball on your own side that you might trip over. The sensible club convention is to clear every spare ball off the court or into a pocket before the point starts, because once play is under way you cannot rely on getting a let for it.

You stop play claiming the ball split, but it didn't

A split ball is a let (FIP Rule 10a). But if the umpire checks and the ball is fine, your request was invalid and you lose the point (FIP Rule 10f). Only stop when you are sure.

Your opponent at the net is crowding the middle and you hit them

Standing in a position on their own side is not hindrance; Rule 3 lets players stand anywhere on their own side. If your shot hits them, it is a correct return and your point (FIP Rule 14a). For more on that, see what happens if the ball hits you.

Common misconceptions

  • "Any noise during a point is a let." No. Rule 11 is about hindering an opponent executing a shot. Outside noise is only a let if it is an unexpected interruption unrelated to the players (FIP Rule 10c), and even then it must be claimed immediately (10e).
  • "You can't talk during a point." The FIP rulebook doesn't ban talking to your partner. Communication is a normal part of doubles.
  • "You can claim a let after the rally finishes." No. If play continues, the right to a let is lost (FIP Rule 10e).
  • "Asking for a let is free." Not if it's invalid. An invalid let request loses the point (FIP Rule 10f).
  • "Accidental hindrance is always just a replay." Only the first time. A second involuntary hindrance by the same pair gives the point to the opponents (FIP Rule 11).

Social games vs official matches

With an umpire, the umpire decides whether a hindrance was deliberate or involuntary and whether a let request is valid. In social padel the players make those calls, so a few conventions keep things friendly:

  • Call a let the moment it happens, with a raised hand and a clear "let". Waiting to see how the rally goes is exactly what Rule 10e is designed to stop.
  • Give the benefit of the doubt on involuntary hindrance and replay the point, but remember the "second time" part of Rule 11 if one pair keeps doing it.
  • Agree before playing how you treat balls from neighbouring courts. Most clubs replay the point, which matches Rule 10b.
  • Return stray balls to neighbouring courts between their points, not in the middle of a rally, so you don't become their foreign object.

Practical tip

Keep your communication short and early. "Mine" called as the ball leaves the opponent's racket is clear and harmless. A loud call just as the opponent is hitting is where friendly matches turn sour. Good pairs talk most between points, and for that you have up to 20 seconds (FIP Rule 2). For more on how points are won and lost, read the main rules of padel and our page on lets and net serves.

Frequently asked questions

Is shouting allowed in padel?

The rulebook doesn't ban noise as such. If a shout hinders an opponent executing a shot, Rule 11 applies: deliberate means you lose the point, involuntary means a let.

Can you talk to your partner during a point?

The FIP rulebook doesn't specifically address it and contains no prohibition. Normal calls between partners are part of the game.

What happens if a ball from another court comes onto yours?

It is a foreign object entering the court, so the point is replayed (FIP Rule 10b), as long as you claim the let immediately (FIP Rule 10e).

What is the difference between a let and hindrance?

Hindrance is interference by a player with an opponent's shot (Rule 11). A let is a replayed point, which can result from involuntary hindrance or from the outside events listed in Rule 10.

Can you lose a point for asking for a let?

Yes. If your let request is invalid, you lose the point (FIP Rule 10f).
