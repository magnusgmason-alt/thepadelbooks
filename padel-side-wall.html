The padel side wall: how to play off the side glass

8 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

To play the padel side wall well, let the ball come off the side glass, follow its new line with your feet and hit it as it moves away from the wall, not while it is still pinned against it. The side glass is trickier than the back glass because it changes the ball's direction as well as its speed. This guide covers how the ball behaves, the double wall in both orders, the footwork that makes it easy, and three drills to groove it.

What the side wall does to the ball

The rules are the same for every wall. Once the ball has bounced on your side it stays in play if it hits your glass or the mesh, and you just have to return it before it bounces a second time (FIP Rule 12). You may also hit the ball into your own side glass so it rebounds over the net, as long as it then lands in the opponents' court; your own mesh is off limits (Rules 14b and 13l).

What changes is the geometry. A ball that hits the back glass comes back towards you, more or less along the line it went in on. A ball that hits the side glass keeps travelling towards the back of the court but is turned away from the wall. Cross-court balls are the ones that usually find the side glass first, so they are the ones you need to read. Padel Browser notes that pace, spin and angle together decide how the rebound behaves.

As a rule of thumb, the steeper the ball comes into the side glass, the more it kicks out towards the middle. A ball that skims in at a shallow angle runs along the wall and stays close to it. Slice makes the ball skid and stay lower; a slow, high ball sits up.

Reading the angle off the side glass

You have less than a second, so ask the same three questions every time: where will it bounce, how hard is it travelling, and how close to the back glass will it hit the side? Then pick one of two plans. The Ely Padel Club coaching notes (on the LTA's ClubSpark platform) describe them neatly: take the ball early and play it as close to the glass as possible, or give it space and strike it once it has rebounded away from the wall. Which one depends on the height, speed and angle of the ball.

The padel side wall double wall: side then back, back then side

The hardest balls touch two walls. Spanish coaches call this the doble pared, and the order matters. Tennis Warehouse's guide to the walls sums it up: when the ball hits the side wall before the back wall it will likely kick back into the middle of the court; when it hits the back wall first, it kicks further up the court and along the side wall of the corner it came from.

Side then back (the opening double wall)

The ball comes out of the corner towards the centre and, often, towards your partner. Padel Addict suggests this is the more natural, less hurried of the two, provided the ball has enough height to reach the back glass at all. Wait a metre or so off the back glass, let the second wall push the ball out to you, and play it cross-court and deep. The classic error is chasing the ball into the corner and hitting it after the first wall, while it is still fast and moving away.

Back then side (the closing double wall)

The ball rebounds off the back glass, clips the side glass and runs out along the wall towards the net. Padel Addict says it arrives at the leg nearest the side wall, so you either open up and play it on that side (a backhand on the left for a right-hander) or follow the whole trajectory with your body and hit at the end of it. The Padel School adds a useful nuance: you don't have to turn the moment the ball hits the back glass. Let it come past you, then turn, keep the racket back with an open face and make contact in front with room from the side glass.

One sentence to remember

Side first, it comes to the middle; back first, it runs up the wall. Move to where the ball will finish, not to where it is now.

Footwork for the side glass

  • Split step as the opponent hits. You can't read a wall ball while your weight is on your heels. See the padel split step guide for the timing.
  • Use a semi-open stance. Padel Addict recommends waiting a little more closed than you would for the back glass, swinging from back to front and low to high, with the weight landing on your front foot.
  • Keep a racket's length from the wall. Most players crowd the side glass and end up cramped. Give the ball room to come out to you.
  • Small steps, late adjustments. Short, quick steps let you correct when the ball kicks more or less than you expected.
  • Contact in front. If the ball has passed your body when you hit it, you were too close to the wall or too late.

The same principles run through our guide to the back glass; the side glass simply asks you to move sideways and forwards as well as back.

Using the side glass to your advantage

The side glass is mostly a defensive tool for you. Tennis Warehouse points out that outright offence off your own side walls is difficult, but they are useful in defence: when both opponents are at the net and you have no time for a lob, you can play the ball into your own side glass so it rebounds over the net, as long as you still hit it forwards. That is a close cousin to the contra pared, played off the side rather than the back.

The opponents' side glass is where you attack. An overhead such as the víbora is designed to bounce in their court and skid into the side glass, staying low. On a short ball inside the service box you can also angle the ball so it bounces and then runs into their side glass. Padel Browser warns that these angled shots take months to develop, so learn the defensive version first. Remember the boundary: your shot must bounce in the opponents' court before it touches their glass, or the point is lost (FIP Rule 13g).

Side wall drills

All three work with a partner feeding from a basket. Start slowly and only add pace when the exits are clean.

1. Single-wall read

How it works: F feeds cross-court so the ball bounces and finds A's side glass a few metres in front of the back wall. A steps away from the glass, lets the ball come off it and plays cross-court into the last few metres of the far court. Twelve balls, then switch sides.

Pass mark: 7 of 12 clean exits into the target.

2. Call the order

How it works: F mixes side-then-back and back-then-side feeds into the same corner. Before the ball reaches the second wall, A calls "middle" or "wall" for where it will come out, then plays it. The call matters more than the shot at first.

Pass mark: 8 correct calls out of 10, then 8 correct calls plus playable returns.

3. Side glass to lob

How it works: F stands on the far service line as a net player. Every side-glass exit must be a lob over F that lands past the service line. This is the match version: in real points, a ball out of the corner is usually your chance to lob and win the net back.

Pass mark: 6 of 10 lobs that F can't reach with an overhead.

Where side-wall points are lost

  • Hitting after the first wall. The ball is still fast and moving away. Wait for the second wall or for it to come off the first.
  • Hugging the glass. No room to swing, contact behind the body.
  • Turning too early on the double wall. You end up facing the wrong way when the ball changes direction.
  • Trying to win the point from the corner. A safe lob or deep cross-court ball beats a low-percentage winner.
  • Hitting your own mesh. Using your own glass is legal; your own mesh is not.

Frequently asked questions

Can you play the ball off the side wall in padel?

Yes. Once the ball has bounced on your side, it can hit your side glass and you can still return it before the second bounce. You can also hit it into your own side glass to get it back over the net, as long as it then lands in the opponents' court (FIP Rules 12 and 14b).

Can you hit the opponents' side wall directly?

No. Your shot must bounce in the opponents' court first. If it hits their glass or mesh before bouncing, you lose the point (FIP Rule 13g). After the bounce, it can hit any of their walls.

What is the doble pared in padel?

It's a ball that hits two walls, the side and back glass, before you play it. Side then back usually kicks out towards the middle; back then side runs along the side wall towards the net.

Should I let the ball hit the side glass or take it before?

Both are valid. Take it early and close to the glass when it is high or slow enough to control; let it come off when it is fast or awkward, so the wall slows it down and gives you time.

Is the side glass the same as the mesh?

No. The glass gives a fairly predictable rebound; the mesh (metal fence) gives an unpredictable one. Both are in play after the ball bounces in your court, but you may not hit the ball into your own mesh.
