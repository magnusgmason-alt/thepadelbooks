Playing with a weaker padel partner

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Playing with a weaker padel partner works best when the stronger player takes more of the middle and most of the overheads, the weaker player keeps the ball in play and lobs, and you both stick to a simple plan instead of trying to cover each other's mistakes. Level gaps are everywhere in social padel. Here are straight answers to the questions pairs ask most, from who plays which side to what to do when you're the one being targeted.

Playing with a weaker padel partner: can you still win?

Yes, often. Padel is a team game, and pairs with one strong and one steady player beat pairs of two equal players regularly, because the court rewards roles more than individual brilliance. Our guide to left side vs right side describes the traditional split: one player builds the point and the other finishes it. A level gap fits that split naturally.

What loses matches is the opposite: the stronger player trying to win every point alone, and the weaker player trying to hit shots they can't yet make. The answers below are about avoiding both.

Which side should the stronger player take?

For two right-handers, usually the left. From the left, the forehand and the overhead face the middle, so the left-side player takes most lobs and smashes through the centre. That's the finishing role, and it gives the stronger player the most balls.

The right side suits a steady player: keep the ball in play, lob deep, play low balls at the feet. The Padel School calls the right-side player the strategist and the left-side player the one who typically finishes. If the weaker player is the more consistent one, that's a good fit.

Exceptions matter. If one of you is left-handed, see our guide to left-handed padel players. And if the weaker player is far more comfortable on the left, The Padel School and All For Padel both say personal comfort counts. Try a set each way and look at who misses less.

Who takes the middle?

The stronger player should take more of it, especially at the net and on lobs down the centre. Padel Addict's coaching piece on invading your partner's side says it happens above all at the net: if your partner is defending their line, don't be afraid to come into their side. It also says to decide in advance who is better at lobs to the centre, based on the match and the opponents.

The key word is "agree". Decide before the first point, and call every shared ball. Our guide to partner communication has simple calls that work.

Will the opponents target the weaker player?

Almost certainly, and they're right to. Padel Browser's advice to pairs facing a stronger team is to work out who the weaker player is in the first few games and send them around 70% of the ball, not 100%. Expect the same treatment in reverse.

Being targeted isn't a problem by itself. It becomes a problem when the weaker player panics or the stronger player abandons their side trying to protect them. Plan for it rather than reacting to it.

How do you protect the weaker player?

Four practical ways:

  • Cover the middle and the lobs. Lean a little towards the centre so the weaker player has less court to cover. Take lobs down the middle that you can reach in balance.
  • Keep the opponents back. Deep lobs from the stronger player push the net pair away, which means fewer hard volleys at your partner.
  • Finish quickly when you can. When a ball sits up for you, end the point. Long rallies give the opponents more chances to aim at your partner.
  • Don't over-invade. Padel Addict warns that taking balls that are clearly your partner's leaves your own side open, produces weaker shots, and causes friction. You can't leave your side unprotected.
The rope test

If the stronger player keeps sprinting across the court while the weaker one stands still, the pair has split. Move together: when you shift to the middle, your partner shifts towards their line, and vice versa.

What if I'm the weaker player?

Your job is simpler than you think: be hard to beat, not spectacular.

  • Make them play one more ball. Aim well inside the court, over the middle of the net, with margin.
  • Lob a lot, and lob deep. A deep lob from you does more for the pair than a risky drive. See our guide to the padel lob.
  • Play through the middle. It creates confusion between the opponents and gives your partner the next ball.
  • Leave the finishing to your partner. When a ball sits up in the centre, call it for them if they're better placed.
  • Say what's happening. "They're going to my backhand" is useful information; silence isn't.

Above all, don't apologise after every miss. It costs focus. A quick "next one" is enough.

What if I'm the stronger player?

Lower the temperature. Give one short, positive instruction at changes of ends, not a running commentary. Play your normal percentage padel rather than trying to hit winners from the back, which only adds errors. And trust your partner with their own balls: a partner who feels trusted plays more freely.

The Padel School also makes a good point about games against lower-level players in general: they're a chance to practise the lob, the low ball to the feet and the controlled bandeja, rather than to blast winners. The same mindset works when your partner is the lower-level player.

How do we train together to close the gap?

Drill the situations the opponents will create: lobs to the weaker player, balls through the middle, and the stronger player moving across. Frequently asked questions

Which side should a weaker padel player play?

For two right-handers, usually the right side. From there the backhand faces the middle, the stronger partner on the left covers more middle balls and overheads, and the right-side job of keeping the ball in play suits a steady player.

Should the stronger player take all the middle balls?

Most of them, especially lobs and volleys they can reach in balance. Agree it before the match and call every ball. Don't chase balls that are clearly your partner's, or you'll leave your own side open.

Is it fair to target the weaker player?

Yes. Targeting the weaker opponent is normal padel tactics. Coaches suggest doing it most of the time rather than every ball, because pairs adapt quickly to a single target.

How can I play better with a stronger partner?

Keep the ball in play, lob deep, play through the middle, and let your partner finish. Talk during points and don't apologise after every error.

Can we swap sides in the middle of a set?

The receiving order is fixed for the whole set (FIP Rules 8.2 and 8.3), so you can't swap who receives on each side until the next set. You can still change positions during points.
