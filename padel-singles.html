Padel singles: can you play padel one against one?

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

Yes, you can play padel singles, one against one, but it's an informal format: the official FIP rules are written for pairs. You can play on a dedicated singles court, which is 20 metres long and 6 metres wide, or on a normal 20 × 10 m court with a few agreed house rules. Here's how both work, and how to play it well.

Can you play padel 1v1?

You can, and plenty of people do, especially for training. But padel is a doubles sport. The FIP Rules of Padel are written around pairs: Rule 3 begins with each pair of players on either side of the net, the coin toss is won by a pair (Rule 4), and the rulebook doesn't mention singles at all. Professional tours are played as doubles.

So padel singles has no official rulebook. You play the normal rules wherever they still make sense, and agree the rest with your opponent before the first point.

Padel singles court size

Some clubs build dedicated singles courts. They keep the full length and the enclosure but are narrower:

Length20 m20 m
Width10 m6 m

Official FIP specificationYesNo FIP singles specification

The 20 × 6 m figure is reported consistently by Padel Market, Padel Addict and The Padel Paper. The FIP's court rules only define the 20 × 10 m doubles court (see our padel court dimensions guide). The Padel School notes that marked singles courts keep the same length and wall structure, with service boxes adjusted to the narrower width.

Singles courts are still uncommon. The Padel Paper counted only a handful of publicly accessible ones in the UK when it surveyed them, and said most players without one simply adapt a standard court.

Singles vs doubles court: how it changes the game

The Padel Paper makes a neat point: on a 6 m singles court, one player covers 6 m of width, compared with about 5 m each in doubles. That small difference makes passing shots easier and the net less dominant than in doubles. On a full 10 m court, one player has the whole width to cover, which is far more running.

The Padel School describes singles as far more physically intense than doubles, with less time to recover between shots, especially on the full doubles width.

Padel singles rules

Keep everything you can from the FIP rules:

  • Scoring: 15, 30, 40, game; sets to six with a two-game margin; tie-break at 6-6 (FIP Rule 1). Our scoring guide has the details.
  • Serve: underarm, bounced behind the service line, hit at or below waist height, diagonally into the box (FIP Rule 6).
  • Walls: the ball must bounce on the floor before hitting the opponent's walls, and you can use your own glass but not your own mesh (Rules 13 and 14).
  • Return: the receiver lets the serve bounce (Rule 8.1).
  • Serving order: players alternate serving games, as in doubles.

House rules for singles on a doubles court

On a normal 20 × 10 m court, agree these before you start:

  1. Which area counts. Padel Market describes a common version where each player defends a diagonal half, and a ball landing outside it is out. Others play the full width, which is a serious workout.
  2. Which side walls are in. If you play a half-court, decide whether a ball that bounces in and then hits the "out-of-play" side glass is still good. Agree this in advance; many players count it as good.
  3. Service boxes. Usually unchanged: diagonal, alternating boxes each point.
  4. Match length. Padel Market suggests shorter formats, such as first to four games or a single set with a tie-break. The Padel School suggests no-ad scoring or a match tie-break instead of a third set.
Three players?

Padel Market's suggestion: play two against one, or rotate one player out each game.

Padel singles tactics

Singles is a different game from doubles, so some doubles habits need to change.

Recover to the centre

In doubles you cover your half. In singles, The Padel School says to hold a central base and recover to the middle after each shot, the opposite of the doubles instinct to stay on your side.

Be patient and play cross-court

Padel Market recommends cross-court as your default, because it's the longest line with the most margin, and hitting deep while you wait for a short ball. Rallies are longer, so building the point matters more than quick winners.

Pick your moment at the net

Rushing the net from a neutral ball leaves you exposed, because there's nobody beside you to cover the pass. Go forward after a good, high lob that pushes your opponent onto the back glass, or a short ball you can attack. Then cover the middle of the net.

Use the lob

The lob still works: high and deep pushes your opponent to the back glass and gives you time to move forward.

Consistency first

With no partner to cover your mistakes, unforced errors cost more. The Padel School puts consistency ahead of power.

A simple singles session plan

If you've booked a court for two and want a mix of play and practice, this structure works well:

  1. Warm up together. Start with gentle cross-court rallies from the back, then volleys at the net. The FIP rules require a three-minute courtesy rally before a match (Rule 2), but for a practice session give yourselves longer.
  2. Play a cross-court game. Each of you defends one diagonal half. It's the format closest to doubles and a good way to groove your lob and your play off the glass.
  3. Play a full-width set. One set with a tie-break on the whole court, if you have the legs for it.
  4. Finish with a rule-based game. Padel Market suggests games such as cross-court only, or every point must include a wall.

Swap ends regularly so you both play from each side of the court, and agree before every game which house rules apply.

Why play padel singles?

  • Fitness: more running, more shots per player, less recovery.
  • More touches on the ball: every shot is yours, so you practise more in the same hour.
  • Glass play and defence: you'll play off the walls constantly.
  • Decision-making: The Padel School notes players often return to doubles sharper and more composed.

The trade-off, as Padel Market points out, is that singles doesn't teach doubles positioning or teamwork. Use it alongside doubles, not instead of it. Our padel drills for 2 players are another way to use a court with just two of you.

Frequently asked questions

Can you play padel with 2 players?

Yes. Play singles on a dedicated 20 × 6 m court, or on a normal court using agreed house rules such as a diagonal half each. Two players can also run partner drills.

Is padel singles official?

No. The FIP rules are written for pairs and don't mention singles, and professional padel is played as doubles.

How big is a padel singles court?

Usually 20 m long and 6 m wide, compared with 20 × 10 m for doubles. There's no FIP specification for singles courts.

How do you serve in padel singles?

The same way as in doubles: underarm, after a bounce, at or below waist height, diagonally into the service box, alternating boxes each point.

Is padel singles harder than doubles?

Physically, yes. You cover more court with less recovery time, especially on a full-width doubles court.
