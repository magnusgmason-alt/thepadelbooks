Round vs diamond padel racket: the shapes explained

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 30 Oct 2026

Round vs diamond padel racket comes down to control versus power: a round racket has a large, central sweet spot and usually a low balance, so it's forgiving and easy to handle, while a diamond racket has a smaller sweet spot high on the face and usually more weight in the head, so it hits harder but punishes mishits. Teardrop sits in between. Here's how each shape plays, who it suits, and how to choose.

Round vs diamond padel racket: shapes at a glance

Every padel racket has to fit inside the same limits set by the FIP rules, so the differences between shapes are about where the hitting area and the weight sit, not overall size. Brands, retailers and coaching guides agree on the basic picture:

ShapeSweet spotUsual balanceFeelBest for
RoundLarge, centred, low on the faceLow (towards the handle)Control and forgivenessBeginners, juniors, control and defensive players
TeardropMedium, a little higherMediumMix of power and controlImprovers and all-round players
DiamondSmaller, high on the faceHigh (towards the head)Maximum power, least forgivingAdvanced, attacking players

HEAD, Playtomic and Central Sports all describe the sweet spots and target players this way, and our guide to choosing a padel racket uses the same framework. The balance column is a tendency rather than a rule, as explained below.

What the sweet spot really means

HEAD defines the sweet spot as the area of the face that gives maximum performance, where the ball feels and sounds best. Hit it there and the ball comes off cleanly with little effort. Hit it outside that area and you lose power and accuracy, and more vibration travels into your hand, wrist and elbow.

The shape of the head moves the sweet spot. On a round racket the hitting area is spread wide around the centre, so off-centre contact is still reasonably good. On a diamond racket the face narrows towards the throat and the sweet spot sits higher and smaller, closer to the tip. That's where you get the most power on overheads, but there's less margin when you miss it.

Balance and shape: linked, but not the same thing

Balance is where the weight sits, usually measured in millimetres from the bottom of the handle. A head-heavy racket adds power to overheads but is slower to move and puts more strain on the arm. A head-light racket is quicker at the net and easier to control.

Shape and balance usually go together: round rackets tend to be low-balance and diamonds high-balance. But it's a tendency, not a rule. Padel Browser points to low-balance diamonds and head-heavy rounds on the market, and to round and teardrop models with the same medium balance. Its guide puts the published balance of US-stocked rackets between roughly 250 and 285 mm, though retailers and guides, including ours, draw the lines between low, medium and high slightly differently. When you're comparing two rackets, check the balance figure itself, not just the shape.

Quick rule

Shape tells you where the sweet spot is. Balance tells you how the racket swings. Check both.

Round padel racket: control first

A round racket is the easiest shape to play with. The large sweet spot means more of your shots go where you intended, and the low balance makes it quick on volleys and gentle on the arm. Central Sports lists less arm strain among its advantages.

The trade-off is power. On a high ball, a round racket won't add as much speed to a smash as a diamond. For most players below an advanced level that matters much less than it sounds, because most padel points are won through placement and patience, not raw speed.

Round isn't only for beginners. PadelShop argues that shape reflects playing style rather than level, and that control-minded players at any standard can benefit from a big sweet spot.

Diamond padel racket: power with a price

A diamond racket puts the weight and the sweet spot high on the face. When you hit it cleanly on a smash or a high volley, it gives you the most power of any shape. HEAD and Playtomic both describe it as a racket for attacking, more advanced players.

The cost is control. A diamond is less forgiving on off-centre hits, slower to bring round at the net and, according to Central Sports, can increase arm fatigue. If you buy one before your timing is reliable, expect more mishits and possibly a sore elbow.

Teardrop vs round padel racket

A teardrop racket is the middle ground. Its sweet spot sits a little higher than a round racket's, and its balance is usually medium, so it adds some power without losing much forgiveness.

  • Choose round if you want maximum control, play mostly from the back of the court, or have any history of arm or elbow trouble.
  • Choose teardrop if your strokes are consistent, you're starting to finish points at the net, and you want a racket you won't outgrow quickly.

Some rackets are sold as soft teardrops, which sit very close to round in feel. They can be a sensible step for a beginner who already hits the ball fairly cleanly.

Diamond vs teardrop padel racket

This is the decision most improving players face. A teardrop gives you most of a diamond's attacking potential with a larger margin for error. A diamond gives you more pop on overheads but asks for clean contact every time.

  • Choose teardrop if you play both sides of the court, rely on your bandeja and volleys more than your smash, or want one racket for everything.
  • Choose diamond if you play the left side, hit a lot of overheads and smashes, and your timing holds up under pressure.

Some brands also sell hybrid shapes that sit between teardrop and diamond. Treat the label as a starting point and check the balance and weight figures.

Which padel racket shape is best for beginners?

Round. Every source we checked, including HEAD, Playtomic, Central Sports and Smash UK, recommends a round racket for beginners because its larger, central sweet spot makes the ball easier to hit well. Central Sports adds a useful tie-breaker: if you're unsure, choose a shape more forgiving than you think you need.

Pair the round shape with a lightish weight and a low or medium balance. Our racket guide notes that most adult rackets weigh roughly 350 to 380 g and suggests beginners stay under about 370 g. HEAD says beginners and intermediate players usually prefer 340 to 360 g, so in practice aim for the lighter end of what's on the shelf. Matching the shape to your side of the court

Your position in the pair is a useful tie-breaker. Right-side players spend more of the point building rallies, playing low balls and returns, so control matters most: round or teardrop. Left-side players hit more overheads and finishing shots, so they often prefer a teardrop or diamond. If you switch sides, a teardrop is the safe all-rounder. Our guide to the left and right side explains the roles in detail.

How to test a shape before you buy

  1. Hire or borrow a round and a teardrop (or a teardrop and a diamond) and play a full session with each.
  2. Test the volleys first. If the racket feels slow at the net, the balance is too high for you.
  3. Count your mishits. If off-centre balls die or fly, the sweet spot is too small for your current game.
  4. Notice your arm. Any elbow or wrist discomfort is a sign to go lower in balance and softer. See padel elbow.

Frequently asked questions

Is a round or diamond padel racket better?

Neither is better overall. Round gives more control and forgiveness; diamond gives more power on clean hits but is less forgiving. Most players below an advanced level play better with round or teardrop.

Which padel racket shape has the biggest sweet spot?

Round. Its sweet spot is large and centred, which is why it's the standard recommendation for beginners.

Should a beginner use a diamond padel racket?

Generally not. A diamond's smaller, higher sweet spot and head-heavy feel make it harder to control, and it can put more strain on the arm.

What is a teardrop padel racket?

A shape between round and diamond, with a slightly higher sweet spot and a medium balance. It offers a mix of control and power and suits improving and all-round players.

Do professional players only use diamond rackets?

No. Professionals choose shapes to suit their style and position, and control-minded players use round and teardrop rackets too.
