How to beat lobbers in padel

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

To beat lobbers in padel, stand a little further from the net than feels natural, answer most lobs with a deep, controlled bandeja, let the really good ones bounce and come off the glass, and drop back together when you have to. Pairs who lob everything are hoping you'll get impatient and smash. This guide shows you how to stay calm, keep the net more often and turn their favourite shot into a waste of their energy.

Why some pairs lob everything

The lob is not a coward's shot. It's the main way the pair at the back wins the net, which is why our guide to the padel lob calls it the most important shot in the game. A pair that lobs on almost every ball is playing a sensible percentage plan: every lob forces you to turn, move back and hit an overhead, and every overhead is a chance for you to miss or to hit something short.

So beating lobbers doesn't mean stopping the lob. You can't. It means making each lob cost them more than it costs you. Three things do that: where you stand, what you do with the overhead, and how calmly you accept that some points will be long.

Net position: close, but not too close

Against a lobbing pair, the classic mistake is crowding the net. You feel aggressive standing a metre away, but every lob then sails over your head before you can turn.

  • Start at your normal volley distance. On our site we recommend about two metres from the net for volleys, and roughly two to three metres for a pair holding the net (see padel positioning in doubles). Against habitual lobbers, sit at the back end of that range.
  • Read the swing, then move. The Padel School's advice is to watch how the opponent strikes the ball: if they're getting underneath it, expect a lob and step back; if they're hitting down, get ready to volley.
  • Turn early. The Padel School lists the sequence for recognising a lob: see the opponent move under the ball, turn your body sideways, start moving back, then decide which overhead to play. The turn buys you more time than any amount of running.
The one-step rule

Each time your volley lands short and the opponent gets under the ball, take one step back before they hit. You'll lose a few drop shots, but you'll reach far more lobs in balance.

The bandeja: depth over power

Most lobs that don't push you all the way to the glass should be answered with a bandeja. Its job is not to win the point. It's to keep you at the net. Bounce's strategy guide puts it simply: use the bandeja for most deep overheads to keep your position, and smash only when the ball is high, slow and clearly finishable. It lists smashing every overhead among the most common mistakes, because a smash that comes back off the glass usually costs you the net.

The Padel School's breakdown by depth is a useful decision rule:

Lob landsBest answerWhy
Short (around the service line)Víbora, topspin smash or a flat attacking overheadYou're in balance and close enough to hurt them
Middle depthBandeja or an aggressive víboraControlled pace, deep target, you stay forward
Deep, over your headLet it bounce, play it after the back glassA rushed overhead from here is a gift

Aim the bandeja deep, towards the corner of the back and side glass, so the lobber has to play their next ball from a cramped position. A deep bandeja makes the next lob harder to hit well. A short one lets them lob again from comfort, and you are back where you started.

When to let it hit the glass

Sometimes the lob is simply good: it clears you comfortably and is heading for the back glass. Don't jump and flail at it. Turn, run back beside the ball, let it bounce and come off the glass, and then choose.

  • If the rebound sits up nicely, you may be able to attack it with a bajada, the overhead played off the back glass. The Padel School suggests a bajada or a flat shot as a way to surprise lobbers.
  • If the rebound is awkward, play your own lob. That's not giving up: it resets the point with them at the back and you ready to move forward again.

Move back together, then win the net back

If one of you chases a lob to the glass and the other stays at the net, you leave the diagonal gap our positioning guide warns about. When a lob beats your pair, you both go back. Then your job changes: you're now the defending pair, and the quickest route back to the net is a deep lob of your own that makes them turn.

That may feel like losing ground. It isn't. Babolat's coaching model treats the back of the court as the place to stay in the rally with high, deep balls, and the net as the place to build the attack. Moving between the two, as a pair, is normal padel.

Stop feeding them easy lobs

Lobbers need a comfortable ball to lob from. The Padel School points out that if you're lobbed all the time, you may be giving them easy shots earlier in the point. Two fixes:

  • Serve with more care. In The Padel School's words, an easy serve gives them an easy lob. A deep serve that skids off the side glass is harder to lob well than a soft ball in the middle of the box.
  • Volley deeper and lower. A volley that bounces short and sits up is perfect lobbing height. Volley deep into the corners or low at their feet and they have to dig the ball out instead of lofting it with control.

How to beat lobbers in padel: patience is the tactic

Every lobber is betting that you'll get bored. Accept before the match that rallies will be long and that a bandeja followed by another bandeja is a good sequence, not a failure. Keep a simple count in your head: if you've played three good bandejas and you're still at the net, you're winning the exchange, because they're working harder to run under each one.

When a lob does come up short and slow, finish it. Patience doesn't mean never attacking. It means attacking the right ball.

A drill to practise against lobbers

Set-up: You and your partner start at the net. A feeder stands at the far back glass with a basket and lobs over either of you at varying depths.

How it works: The player being lobbed calls it, turns and plays the right answer for the depth: bandeja or víbora for most, a bounce off the glass for the deepest. Both of you then recover to the net. Score a point for each ball that lands deep and leaves you both back in position.

Frequently asked questions

How do you beat someone who lobs all the time in padel?

Stand slightly further from the net, turn early when they get under the ball, answer most lobs with a deep bandeja and recover forward. Let the deepest lobs bounce off the back glass, and when you're pushed back, go back together and lob them in return.

Should I smash every lob?

No. Smash only when the ball is high, slow and clearly finishable. A hard smash that comes back off their glass usually costs you the net, which is exactly what the lobber wants.

How far from the net should I stand against lobbers?

Around two to three metres, at the back end of that range against a pair who lob a lot, and take a step back whenever an opponent gets underneath the ball.

When should I let a lob bounce?

When it's going to carry over you towards the back glass. Turn, move back beside the ball, let it come off the glass and then play a bajada or a lob of your own, depending on how it rebounds.

Is lobbing a lot a legitimate tactic?

Yes. The lob is the main way the defending pair wins back the net, and nothing in the FIP rules limits how often you can play it. Your answer is better overheads and positions, not complaining.
