How to choose a padel racket

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

Walk into any padel shop and you'll see a wall of rackets that all look similar and cost anywhere from a cheap night out to a weekend away. Only four things really matter: shape, weight, balance and materials. Get those right for your level and the brand barely matters.

What the rules say

Every padel racket is solid, with no strings, and perforated with holes. Under the FIP rules a racket may be at most 45.5 cm long, 26 cm wide and 38 mm thick, and the wrist strap is compulsory. Any racket sold as a padel racket by a mainstream brand will meet these limits, so you can focus on how it plays.

1. Shape: round, teardrop or diamond

ShapeSweet spotFeelBest for
RoundLarge, low, centredControl and forgivenessBeginners, defensive and right-side players
TeardropMedium, slightly higherBalance of power and controlImprovers and all-round players
DiamondSmall, near the topMaximum power, less forgivingAdvanced, attacking left-side players

If you're unsure, choose round. A big sweet spot means more balls go where you meant them to, and that matters far more than power while you're learning.

2. Weight

Most adult padel rackets weigh roughly 350 to 380 grams, and junior rackets are lighter.

  • Lighter (under about 370 g): quicker at the net, easier on the arm, less power. The right choice for beginners and anyone with arm or elbow trouble.
  • Heavier (over about 370 g): more power and stability on smashes, but slower to manoeuvre and harder on the elbow if your technique isn't solid yet.

3. Balance

Balance tells you where the weight sits, measured in millimetres from the bottom of the handle.

  • Low balance (below about 265 mm): weight near your hand. Fast and easy to control.
  • Medium (around 270 mm): the versatile middle.
  • High (above about 275 mm): weight in the head. More power, but harder to handle and more strain on the elbow.

Round rackets tend to have low balance and diamond rackets high balance, which is why shape and balance usually go together.

4. Materials: face and core

The face. Fibreglass is softer, absorbs vibration and feels comfortable, good for beginners. Carbon is stiffer and more durable, gives more precise feedback and suits players with solid technique.

The core. Soft foam cores feel comfortable and give the ball an easy push even on slow swings. EVA rubber cores are firmer and more durable, and give more control to players who swing fast. As a rule: softer for comfort and easy power, harder for control and players who swing fast.

What should you buy?

Beginner

Round shape, under 370 g, low balance, fibreglass or soft face. A mid-priced racket is plenty.

Improver / intermediate

Teardrop shape, medium balance, carbon face if your technique is consistent.

Advanced players choose by playing style: diamond and high balance for attacking smashers, teardrop or round for control players. Don't buy a professional's racket early. It's tuned for a technique you don't have yet, and it can slow your progress.

Protect your elbow

"Padel elbow" is real. The biggest risk factors from equipment are a racket that's too heavy, too head-heavy or too stiff for your level. If you feel pain on the outside of the elbow, go lighter, lower the balance, choose a softer face and core, and check your technique, especially on the bandeja and volley.

Small things that make a big difference

  • Overgrip: add one and replace it often. It improves grip, comfort and lets you fine-tune handle thickness.
  • Edge protector tape: protects the frame from scrapes on the glass and floor.
  • Try before you buy: many clubs rent demo rackets. Play a full session with one before you commit.

Grip and handle

Padel rackets come with a fairly standard handle, so you rarely choose a grip size the way you do in tennis. Instead, players adjust the thickness with overgrips: one for a slimmer feel, two for a thicker one. You want to hold the racket firmly without squeezing. If your knuckles go white, the handle is too thin.

Rackets for kids and juniors

Junior rackets are lighter and often slightly smaller. For players around 10 to 14, a light, round, soft racket makes learning easier and protects young arms. Avoid handing a child an adult's heavy, head-heavy racket.

Matching the racket to your side of the court

Your position in the pair is a useful tiebreaker between two rackets you like. Right-side players spend most of the point building rallies, playing low balls and returns, so control matters most: round or teardrop, lower balance. Left-side players hit more overheads and finishing shots, so they often prefer a teardrop or diamond with a little more weight in the head. If you switch sides often, a teardrop is the safe all-rounder.

Where your money goes

A higher price usually buys more carbon in the face and frame, more advanced core rubbers and lower weight for the same stiffness. Those differences are real, but they mostly help players who already hit the ball cleanly and consistently. For your first year of padel, a solid mid-range round racket will improve your game more than a top-of-the-range diamond. Spend the difference on coaching or court time.

When to replace your racket

Rackets wear out gradually. The core loses some of its liveliness, and cracks can appear in the frame after hits on the glass or floor. Signs it's time for a new one: a visible crack, a rattling sound, or the ball suddenly feeling dead off the face. Regular players often replace theirs around once a season; occasional players can go much longer.

Before you buy: a checklist

  1. What's my level, honestly: beginner, improver or advanced?
  2. Do I prefer control or power? (If unsure: control.)
  3. Have I had any elbow or wrist pain? Then go lighter, lower balance and softer.
  4. Round, teardrop or diamond, matched to the answers above.
  5. Have I played at least one session with it, or a demo of the same model?

Frequently asked questions

What is the best padel racket shape for beginners?

Round. It has the biggest, most forgiving sweet spot and usually a low balance, which makes it easy to control.

How heavy should a padel racket be?

Most adult rackets weigh around 350 to 380 grams. Beginners and players with arm problems should stay at the lighter end.

Carbon or fibreglass?

Fibreglass is softer and more comfortable, good for beginners. Carbon is stiffer and more precise, better once your technique is consistent.

Are padel rackets regulated?

Yes. The FIP rules limit rackets to 45.5 cm long, 26 cm wide and 38 mm thick, with a compulsory wrist strap.
