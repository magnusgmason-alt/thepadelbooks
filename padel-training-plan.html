Padel training plan

14 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 31 Oct 2026

A good padel training plan trains one part of your game at a time, in two sessions a week, with drills you can score, and retests the same drills at the end so you know whether it worked. This guide gives you a weekly padel practice routine, an 8-week plan for club players, a shorter four-week option for beginners and ten drills to fill them.

The plans and drills here come from our two drill books. The 8-week plans and eight of the drills are adapted from The Padel Drill Book: Intermediate & Advanced; the four-week plans and two drills come from The Padel Drill Book. Everything is rewritten and shortened for the web.

How often should you practise padel?

The Padel School puts it simply: training builds skills and matches test them, and consistency matters more than volume. Two or three focused sessions a week can make a real difference, as long as each one has a clear aim and you track what you work on.

Our books put numbers on that. The Padel Drill Book suggests at least one structured drill session for every three matches you play. The 8-week plans in The Padel Drill Book: Intermediate & Advanced use two sessions a week, alongside your normal matches. For most club players that means:

  • Two practice sessions a week, one long and one shorter.
  • Your usual matches, used to try out the week's focus rather than repair it.
  • At least one rest or easy day. Our padel exercises guide covers how strength work fits around this.

A weekly padel practice routine

The long session is 75 minutes, so it sits comfortably within a 90-minute court booking, including setting up. It follows the five-block structure from The Padel Drill Book: Intermediate & Advanced.

SessionMinutesWhat you do
Session 10–12Warm-up with the ball in play early (see our padel warm-up)
12–27Read block: the week's first drill, one decision, fed
27–45Choice block: the week's second drill, adding an opponent or a live ball
45–50Reset: water, scores in the log, one sentence each on what changed
50–68Pressure block: the week's scoring game
68–75Cool-down and agree the focus for next time
Session 245–60 in totalWarm-up, the week's third drill, your weakest drill again, the game
Matches–One decision from the week's focus, checked at each change of ends

The FIP rules allow up to 90 seconds at a change of ends (Rule 2), which is enough time for a quick word with your partner about whether you played the decision when the ball allowed it.

8-week padel training plan (level 3 to 4)

This is the first of three eight-week plans in The Padel Drill Book: Intermediate & Advanced, written for the club regular who wants to become a strong club player. Each week adds one area of the game; and in week 8 you go back to the first drills and compare the numbers.

If the first two weeks feel hard, give each of them a fortnight. Everything later in the plan builds on glass reading and net play, so a slower start usually pays off.

The book's other two plans take you from level 4 to 5 (attacking out of the glass, choosing the overhead, transitions after a weak reply, defending a strong attack, the first four shots of a point and patterns for the finisher) and from level 5 to 6 (more pressure on every one of those, plus serve plans and agreed pair plans for big points). A ten-question self-test at the front of the book tells you which plan to start with.

A 4-week plan for beginners and improvers

The Padel Drill Book has four shorter plans, one each for beginners, improvers, intermediate and advanced players. They work the same way: two 60-minute sessions a week, week 3 is a copy of week 1, so progress is easy to spot, and the last session of week 4 repeats the very first one as a test. A typical beginner session looks like this:

  1. Warm-up (10 minutes): movement, then mini-padel at the net.
  2. A solo glass drill such as the forehand wall rally below.
  3. A control drill with a partner, such as a cross-court rally.
  4. A game that rewards the same habit, such as calling every ball.

At level 1 to 2 the book's priority is the back glass: when in doubt, add five more minutes of glass work.

Ten drills from the plans

1. The ball that dies (G8 in the book)

Players: 2 · Time: 12 minutes · Level: 3–5 · Plan: week 1

Set-up: You at the back on the right; the feeder at the far service line. A deep cross-court target (T1) and a short one just past the net (T2).

How it works: The feeder slices hard so the ball skids into your glass and comes out low, often below the knee. Bend your knees early and get the racket face under the ball. If it is knee height or higher, lob deep cross-court into T1; lower than that, play a soft, safe ball into T2. The feeder mixes sliced and plain feeds without warning. Sets of ten.

Pass mark: Seven of ten back over, at least four in T1. Easier: less slice, fed from closer.

2. Two-speed volley (N2 in the book)

Players: 3 · Time: 12 minutes · Level: 3–5 · Plan: week 2

Set-up: You at the net on the right; the feeder deep in the far court; a third player on the far service line, cross-court.

How it works: The feeder drives at two heights in random order: below the net tape, or between waist and shoulder. A low ball gets a block with a slightly open face, deep into the target, borrowing the incoming pace. A high ball gets a step in and a punch down at the third player's feet. Fifteen feeds, then rotate. The question every time: is contact above or below the tape?

Pass mark: Twelve of 15 at the right speed. Easier: slower hand feeds with an early "high" or "low" call.

3. Back, middle, net (T4 in the book)

Players: 2 · Time: 12 minutes · Level: 3–5 · Plan: week 3

Set-up: Three cones: near your back glass, on your service line and at net position. The feeder on the far side.

How it works: Ball one comes deep: play it after the glass, deep cross-court, then move up. Ball two arrives as you reach the service line: split, then a low volley or half-volley deep through the middle, and move on. Ball three at the net: split again and volley firmly to the feet or the open space. Walk back and repeat; sets of six. Stop on the feeder's contact, even if you are short of the cone.

Pass mark: Five of six ladders clean. Easier: the feeder waits until you have split at each cone.

4. Back, strike, forward (O2 in the book)

Players: 2 · Time: 12 minutes · Level: 3–5 · Plan: week 4

Set-up: You at the net on the right. One cone a metre behind your service line, one at net position.

How it works: The feeder lobs high and deep. Turn your shoulders before your feet move, retreat side-on and plant your back foot by the rear cone. Play a deep cross-court bandeja with your weight moving forward, so your first step back to the net comes out of the swing itself. Sprint back to the net cone and split as the next lob is struck. Sets of eight. If the lob beats you, turn and let it reach the glass instead.

Pass mark: Six of eight full sequences. Easier: medium-depth lobs with a full reset between balls.

5. Buy time down the middle (D7 in the book)

Players: 2–4 · Time: 10 minutes · Level: 3–5 · Plan: week 5

Set-up: Your pair in defensive positions near the back glass. The feeder at the net on the far side. A cone in the centre of the far court, about a metre short of their back glass.

How it works: The feeder alternates balls into your corner and to the middle. Whenever you are stretched or late, lob high and slow through the middle towards the cone. While it is in the air, both of you get back to your spots and split before the next feed. When you are balanced, a corner lob is allowed; your partner confirms with "reset" or "go". Judge your own balance, not the ball.

Pass mark: Eight of ten reset lobs within two metres of the cone. Harder: the feeder answers every reset with a bandeja.

6. Second serve, called target (S1 in the book)

Players: 2–3 · Time: 12 minutes · Level: 3–5 · Plan: week 6

Set-up: You serving from the right. In the diagonal box, mark one zone against the side glass close to the service line and another at the T. A third player stands in as the body target.

How it works: Every serve must be legal (FIP Rule 6): ball bounced behind the service line, struck at or below the waist, at least one foot on the ground. As you bounce the ball, your partner calls "glass", "T" or "body". A glass serve must reach the side glass after the bounce; one that hits the mesh first is a fault. Sets of ten, and a fault sends the count back to zero. Keep the same swing speed under pressure and find your margin with height over the net.

Pass mark: Ten without a fault, seven on target. Easier: the target is called before the routine starts.

7. Same depth, same time (P10 in the book)

Players: 3 · Time: 12 minutes · Level: 3–5 · Plan: week 7

Set-up: Your pair at the net; the feeder on the far side with a basket.

How it works: The feeder sends sequences of six: volleys, a deep lob, a short ball and some wide ones. After each of your shots the feeder calls "freeze" and you both stop dead. Score a point if you are level (neither more than about a metre ahead) and both shifted towards the ball's side. Six sequences, then the feeder targets the other player. When your partner turns for a lob, you drop back too.

Pass mark: Thirty of 36 freezes passed. Harder: random freezes, two or three per sequence.

8. Glass first (M3 in the book)

Players: 4 · Time: 15 minutes · Level: 3–6 · Plan: week 1 game

Set-up: Your pair at the back; the opponents at the net, opening each point with a deep volley.

How it works: Play points, but any ball your pair takes before it touches your glass must be lobbed. Once a ball has come off your back or side glass, any shot is allowed: lob, chiquita, low drive or pass. Attack a ball that never touched the glass and you lose the point. First to 11, then change roles. Letting the ball go is a choice, not a fallback.

Pass mark: Five points won with an attacking shot off the glass. Harder: only the back glass unlocks the attack.

9. Forehand wall rally (Drill 1 in The Padel Drill Book)

Players: 1 · Time: 10 minutes · Level: 1–3 · Plan: beginner, week 1

Set-up: Stand side-on about four metres from your back glass, forehand side, racket back early.

How it works: Drop-feed and send a gentle forehand into the glass around waist height. Allow the rebound one bounce, then play it again, aiming for the same patch of glass each time. Restart with a drop feed when the rally breaks. Two-minute sets with 30 seconds' rest. If the ball jams you, take a step back.

Pass mark: Longest rally in two minutes: beat 20, then 40. Harder: alternate soft and medium pace. More in our solo padel drills.

10. Volley to the feet (Drill 26 in The Padel Drill Book)

Players: 3 · Time: 12 minutes · Level: 2–5 · Plan: improver, week 1

Set-up: You at the net, about 2.5 metres back. An opponent on the far service line, moving forward. A feeder with a basket.

How it works: The feeder sends medium balls at chest height. Angle the volley downwards and a little short, so it drops at the feet of the incoming player and they have to volley up. Racket head up, face slightly open, short follow-through. Fifteen balls, then rotate all three roles. If the opponent is on the service line, short beats deep.

Pass mark: Nine of 15 at the feet. Harder: the opponent plays every ball back and the rally continues.

How to track progress

A plan only works if you measure it. Keep it simple enough that you actually write it down.

  • Pass marks, twice. Tick a drill off only when you reach its pass mark in two separate sessions. One good day can be luck.
  • One set per match. Count four things for a single set: your unforced errors, points your pair wins while both of you are at the net, first serves in, and deciding points won or lost.
  • One goal per match. Pick one decision from the week's focus and score yourself from one to five afterwards.
  • Retest. Repeat the opening drills at the end of the plan and compare. Our padel levels guide helps you place yourself before and after.

Expect new habits to show up late in matches. Under pressure, old choices come back for a while; the new one appears first on easy balls, then on harder ones. That's expected, and it's why the plans run for weeks, not days.

Common training plan mistakes

  • Changing focus every week. Stay with one theme long enough to retest it.
  • Too many drills per session. Two drills done properly beat six done in a hurry.
  • No score. Without a number you can't tell whether the plan is working.
  • Only drills, no matches. Matches are where you find out whether the new habit holds under pressure.
  • Skipping the retest. The last week is the point of the whole plan.
  • Pushing on when you're far behind. If you fall well short of a pass mark, run the week again. Ten weeks instead of eight still counts as success.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I practise padel?

Two focused practice sessions a week alongside your normal matches suits most club players. The Padel School suggests two to three focused sessions a week with a clear aim; consistency matters more than volume.

What should a padel training plan include?

One focus at a time, drills with a score, a scoring game that rewards the same habit, your normal matches to test it, and a retest at the end using the same drills.

Is there an 8-week padel training plan?

Yes. The plan above, for level 3 to 4, is one of three eight-week plans in The Padel Drill Book: Intermediate & Advanced. The other two cover level 4 to 5 and level 5 to 6.

How long until a training plan shows results?

There is no guaranteed timeline. Drill scores usually improve first; match habits follow later, once the new choice holds up under pressure. That is why each plan ends with a retest of the same drills.

Can I follow a padel training plan on my own?

Partly. Solo glass and wall drills cover contact and timing, but most decision drills need a feeder or a partner. Our solo padel drills are a good start.
