Padel racket care: a checklist that keeps it playing longer

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Good padel racket care comes down to four habits: keep it out of heat, wipe it down after play, protect the frame from scrapes, and check it regularly for cracks. None of it takes more than a minute, and it keeps the racket playing the way it did when you bought it. Use the checklists below after every session, every month and whenever something looks wrong.

Padel racket care after every session

  • Wipe the faces and frame. PadelPROShop recommends a dry microfibre cloth after each game to remove sand and dust, because grit can wear the surface.
  • Dry the grip. A damp overgrip goes slick and smelly. Towel it off and leave the bag slightly open so moisture can escape.
  • Look at the edge. Check the frame protector for lifting corners or fresh chips.
  • Put it in its cover or thermal compartment. Not loose in the bag next to keys and bottles.
  • Take it inside. Not left in the car.

Heat and cold: the car boot problem

The quickest way to shorten a racket's life is to leave it in a hot car. Keepadel, a padel retailer, says the inside of a car can reach extremely high temperatures within minutes, even on days that don't feel especially hot, and calls this the most common and most damaging mistake. Heat reduces the rubber core's elasticity, so the racket feels dead and you have to swing harder for the same depth. It also stresses the fibres and adhesives. Keepadel says heat damage is gradual and can't be undone.

Cold is less dramatic but changes the feel too. PadelPROShop notes that heat softens the rubber and cold stiffens it, and advises against storing a racket in vehicles or direct sunlight. Padel Browser adds that an unheated garage in winter can make cores brittle.

  • Don't leave the racket in a parked car, summer or winter.
  • Keep it in the shade between matches on hot outdoor courts.
  • If you play somewhere very hot or very cold, see soft vs hard padel rackets for how temperature changes core feel.

Thermal bags and storage

Decathlon describes a thermal compartment as protection against temperature changes that rackets don't tolerate well, and calls it essential for regular players. Keepadel says thermal compartments help keep temperatures more stable, and suggests a thermal cover as a cheaper alternative if you don't have a bag with one.

  • At home, store the racket upright in its thermal cover, somewhere dry and ventilated (PadelPROShop's advice).
  • Keep it away from radiators, sunny windowsills and damp garages.
  • In your bag, use the dedicated racket compartment. Our padel bag checklist covers bag types.

Frame protector tape

Low balls near the glass and scoops off the turf mean the top of the frame takes most of the knocks. PadelPROShop notes that impacts against walls mainly damage the frame. A protector tape across the top edge takes those scrapes instead.

  • Fit it when the racket is new, on a clean, dry frame, pressing out any air bubbles.
  • Replace it when it's worn or peeling. PadelPROShop warns that debris can build up under worn adhesive.
  • Remove it carefully. Padel Browser lists improper protector removal among the modifications that warranties may not cover.

Cleaning rough-surface and textured faces

Many rackets have a rough, sandy texture on the face. Sand and dust lodge in it, and the texture wears smooth over time. PadelPROShop's cleaning advice applies to any face:

  • Use a dry microfibre cloth after play.
  • For stains, use a cloth barely dampened with mild soap, then dry straight away.
  • Don't use harsh chemicals, and never soak or submerge the racket.
  • Don't scrub a textured face with anything abrasive: you'll remove the grip you paid for.

Monthly inspection checklist

  1. Look along the frame in good light, especially the top edge and the throat (where the frame meets the handle).
  2. Check around the holes on both faces for hairline cracks running between them.
  3. Shake it gently next to your ear. PadelPROShop lists internal noises among the signs that a racket needs inspecting.
  4. Press the centre of the face with your thumb and compare with the edges. Padel Browser describes a dead core as one that gives and then feels like pressing a board.
  5. Check the cord and handle. The wrist cord is compulsory under the FIP rules; replace it if it's frayed. Make sure the butt cap is secure.

Can you repair a padel racket?

Sometimes, and sources disagree on whether it's worth it. PadelPROShop describes a home repair for surface cracks on the face, using sandpaper, epoxy resin and layers of carbon or fibreglass fabric, pressed and trimmed. It says the repair can work but may change the weight and the face's roughness, that a deep break in the rubber core means you should consider replacing the racket, and that it's not worth repairing an old racket because the rubber has already lost its properties.

Padel Browser takes a firmer line: cracked frames and faces aren't worth repairing, while cosmetic issues like grips and scuffs are. Both agree on one thing: a repair outside the brand's service will usually rule out a warranty claim. If the racket is new, try the warranty first.

Warranty basics

Warranty terms vary by brand, retailer and country, so read the ones for your racket. The general pattern, according to Padel Browser's review of warranties:

  • Usually covered: manufacturing defects. The typical approved claim is a clean break on a racket only a few months old, with no sign of impact.
  • Usually not covered: impact damage (hitting the glass, fence, net posts or floor), heat or sun damage, misuse, normal wear on paint, protector strips and grips, and repairs or modifications.
  • What you'll need: proof of purchase, clear photos of the damage and the serial number, and often the original packaging. Claims usually go through the seller, not the brand.
  • Stop playing with a racket you think is defective, so the cause can still be assessed.

Within the EU, buyers also have statutory guarantee rights against the seller. Padel Browser notes these are rights exercised within the EU, so claims are harder if you bought from abroad.

Habits on court that protect your racket

Most frame damage happens in the corners and on low balls. Getting your racket down early, with a stable wrist, means fewer desperate scoops off the turf and fewer clashes with the glass. Frequently asked questions

Can I leave my padel racket in the car?

Avoid it. Retailers and brands consistently warn that heat in a parked car degrades the rubber core and can make the racket feel dead, and cold can make cores brittle. Take it inside, or at least use a thermal bag.

Do I need protector tape on my padel racket?

It's worth it if you play regularly. It takes the scrapes from the glass and turf that would otherwise chip the frame. Replace it when it's worn or peeling.

How do I clean a padel racket?

Wipe it with a dry microfibre cloth after every session. For stains, use a cloth barely dampened with mild soap and dry it immediately. Avoid harsh chemicals, soaking and abrasive scrubbing.

Is a cracked padel racket covered by warranty?

Only if the crack comes from a manufacturing defect. Impact damage from the glass, fence or floor is usually excluded. Contact the seller with proof of purchase and photos, and stop playing with it.

Can a cracked padel racket be repaired?

Small surface cracks on the face can sometimes be repaired with resin and fibre layers, but the feel and weight may change. Frame cracks and deep core damage usually mean it's time for a new racket.
