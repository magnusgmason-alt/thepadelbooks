What to eat before padel: a match-day food and drink checklist

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

What to eat before padel comes down to timing: have a normal, carbohydrate-based meal two to four hours before you play, top up with a small, easy snack in the last hour if you're hungry, and sip fluids through the afternoon rather than gulping a bottle on court. Below are printable checklists for match day, tournament and Americano days, hot weather and the meal afterwards, all based on sports nutrition guidance.

General guidance

This is general sports nutrition information for healthy adults. If you have diabetes, a digestive condition, food allergies or specific goals, an accredited sports dietitian can tailor it to you.

The short version

  1. 2–4 hours before: a normal meal built around carbohydrate, with some protein and not too much fat or fibre.
  2. In the last hour: a small, easy snack only if you're hungry, and a few sips of fluid.
  3. On court: drink at changes of ends; add carbohydrate and electrolytes if you're playing for more than about two hours or in the heat.
  4. Afterwards: fluid, carbohydrate and protein, sooner rather than later if you're playing again.

Checklist: what to eat before padel (2–4 hours out)

Sports Dietitians Australia says most people can tolerate their last main meal two to four hours before exercise without stomach upset. The meal should be rich in carbohydrate, easy to digest, low in fibre if you're nervous or have a sensitive gut, and familiar. Foods high in fat or protein are slower to digest, so keep those moderate.

  • Cereal or porridge with chopped fruit, milk or yoghurt
  • A small bowl of pasta with a tomato-based sauce
  • Crumpets or toast with banana and honey
  • Rice with chicken or fish and a modest portion of vegetables
  • Creamed rice with tinned fruit
  • A fruit smoothie, if solid food feels heavy

Avoid: a large fry-up, a heavy curry or a big salad of raw vegetables and beans right before a match. And follow the dietitians' golden rule: don't try anything new on match day.

Checklist: the last hour

Sports Dietitians Australia suggests a small snack to top up energy stores in the hour or so before exercise if you need one. Good options:

  • a banana
  • a slice of toast or a crumpet with honey or jam
  • a cereal bar or a few rice cakes
  • a small yoghurt

Sip fluid over the few hours before you play rather than drinking a large volume just before you start, which helps avoid toilet trips and a sloshing stomach. Water is fine; an electrolyte or sports drink also works.

Caffeine before padel

The Australian Institute of Sport (AIS) says small doses of caffeine, around 2–3 mg per kg of body weight (about 200 mg for many adults), can improve performance, with benefits levelling off at around 3 mg/kg. A typical 250 ml cup of brewed coffee contains about 80 mg, but coffee shop drinks vary widely, and some large servings exceed 500 mg.

Two things matter for padel in particular. First, the AIS notes that higher doses can raise heart rate and impair fine motor control and technique, which is the last thing you want for a delicate drop shot or volley. Second, caffeine has a half-life of about five hours and can affect sleep even at low intakes, so think twice before an espresso ahead of a 9pm match. Effects vary a lot between people, so test it in training first. The AIS gives up to 400 mg a day from all sources as generally safe for adults (pregnancy excepted) and no more than about 200 mg at once. If you're pregnant, have a heart condition or have been advised to limit caffeine, skip it.

Checklist: snacks between matches (tournaments and Americanos)

Tournaments and Americanos mean several matches with short gaps. Research on tennis, a similar stop-start racket sport, gives a useful framework (Ranchordas and colleagues, Journal of Sports Science and Medicine, 2013):

  • Refuel straight after each match if the next one is soon. Carbohydrate eaten immediately helps restock your muscles, and the review notes that delaying carbohydrate by several hours can reduce muscle glycogen storage substantially.
  • Aim for 30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour once you're playing for more than about two hours.
  • Keep it small and frequent. Half a sandwich, a banana, dried fruit, a cereal bar or a sports drink between rounds beats one big lunch.
  • Drink at every change of ends. The breaks between points are short, so make the change of ends your drinks stop.

A full Americano typically lasts one and a half to two hours; see our Americano guide for formats. For a first competition, our first padel tournament guide covers the rest of the day's logistics.

Checklist: hydration and electrolytes in the heat

The LTA's hot-weather advice for racket sports is to drink at regular intervals whether or not you feel thirsty, because thirst isn't an effective warning system, and to replace roughly what you lose in sweat, which it puts at about 1 to 2.5 litres per hour of play. It also recommends avoiding the 11am to 3pm sun where possible and stopping to rest in the shade if you feel dizzy, unwell or overly tired.

  • Start hydrated. Sip through the day rather than forcing down a bottle just before you walk on court.
  • Add electrolytes when it's hot or long. The AIS says electrolyte replacement is useful with high sweat rates, long sessions, "salty sweat" and hot conditions. Its general guide for drinks is 0.5–0.7 g of sodium per litre.
  • Don't ignore cramps. The tennis review records sodium losses of 0.5 to 1.8 g in players and links sodium depletion with heat cramps, another reason to add electrolytes on hot days.
  • Bring more than you think. Two large bottles for a long, hot session is not overkill.

Playing outdoors brings sun and wind into it too; see playing padel outdoors.

Checklist: eating after padel

  • Protein: the International Society of Sports Nutrition's nutrient-timing position stand suggests 20–40 g of high-quality protein (about 0.25–0.40 g per kg) per meal, and notes that protein from immediately to two hours after exercise stimulates muscle repair. Examples: a chicken or tuna sandwich, eggs on toast, Greek yoghurt with fruit, or a glass of milk with a meal.
  • Carbohydrate: rice, pasta, potatoes or bread, especially if you play again the next day.
  • Fluid and salt: after a sweaty session, the AIS suggests drinking about 1.2 to 1.5 times the fluid you lost over the next two to four hours, with some sodium from salty foods, a salted meal or an electrolyte drink. Weighing yourself before and after a session tells you roughly what you lost.

Food won't fix a game plan, though. Frequently asked questions

How long before padel should I eat?

Most people do well with a main meal two to four hours before playing, plus a small snack in the last hour if hungry. Experiment in training to find what suits you.

What is a good snack before padel?

Something small and carbohydrate-based that you know sits well: a banana, toast with honey, a cereal bar or a small yoghurt.

Should I drink coffee before padel?

A small amount, around 2–3 mg per kg of body weight, can help performance, but higher doses can affect fine motor control and sleep. Test it in training and avoid it late in the day.

Do I need electrolytes for padel?

For an hour of social padel in mild weather, water is usually enough. Electrolytes help in the heat, in long sessions and if you're a heavy or salty sweater.

What should I eat between padel matches?

Carbohydrate straight after each match, then small, easy snacks such as bananas, sandwiches, cereal bars or a sports drink, and keep drinking.

What should I eat after padel?

A meal with 20–40 g of protein, some carbohydrate and enough fluid and salt to replace what you sweated out, ideally within a couple of hours.
