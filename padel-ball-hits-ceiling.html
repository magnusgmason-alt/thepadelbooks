What if the ball hits the ceiling or lights in padel?

8 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

It depends on whether the ball has already bounced in the opponents' court. If it bounces there first and then hits the ceiling, lights or another object above the court, it is a correct return (FIP Rule 14d), and since the ball has left the playing area via an element unrelated to the court, the hitter wins the point. If it hits the ceiling or lights before bouncing in the opponents' court, the hitter loses the point (FIP Rules 13g and 14).

The short answer

Before the bounce, the ceiling is out: a ball that hits the ceiling, lights or beams before bouncing in the opponents' court loses the hitter the point (FIP Rules 13g and 14). After a correct bounce in the opponents' court, a ball that is carried into the ceiling or lights by the force of the shot is a correct return (FIP Rule 14d), so in practice the point goes to the hitter.

What the rules say

The ceiling is not part of the court. FIP Rule 12 lists the parts of the court as the ground, the inside of the walls, the metal mesh fence (including its frame), the net and the posts. Everything else, including the ceiling, lights, beams and roof structure, is an element unrelated to the court. That is why these points matter:

  • FIP Rule 13g: a pair loses the point if a player returns the ball and, without bouncing in the opponents' court, it strikes the opponents' walls, metal fence or any element not related to the court. A lob that hits the roof over the opponents' half before landing falls under this rule.
  • Your own side: Rule 13l covers the metal fence, the ground and unrelated elements located on the ground on your own side, not overhead fixtures. A lob that clips a light or beam before bouncing in the opponents' court is not a correct return (Rule 14) and loses the point; the rulebook has no separate clause for overhead fixtures on your own side.
  • FIP Rule 14d: if the ball bounces in the opponents' court and then, by the force or direction of the shot, leaves the court by hitting the ceiling, lights or another object unrelated to the court, it is a correct return (Rule 14d), and since the ball has left the playing area via an element unrelated to the court, the hitter wins the point. Rule 14d defines a correct return; the “point won” rules in Rule 15 only cover holes in the fence and the top of the wall.

How high should the ceiling be?

The FIP rulebook's court specifications require a minimum free height of 6 metres across the whole court, with nothing such as spotlights projecting into that space. For new facilities it recommends more, 8 metres. Many indoor clubs meet the minimum but not much more, which is why ceiling and light hits are common indoors and almost unknown outdoors.

Good shot

Your ball bounces in the opponents' court and the force of the shot carries it up into the ceiling, a beam or a light. That is a correct return (Rule 14d), and since the ball has left the playing area, the point is yours.

Point lost

Your ball hits the ceiling, a light or a beam before it bounces in the opponents' court. It is not a correct return (Rule 14), whether it touched over your half or theirs (Rule 13g covers their side).

Real match situations

1. A defensive lob clips a light

Under pressure at the back, you throw up a high lob. It clips a hanging light above the opponents' service box before landing. The ball struck an element unrelated to the court before bouncing in the opponents' court, so you lose the point (FIP Rule 13g). It doesn't matter that it would have landed in.

2. A smash that bounces and kicks into the roof

You hit a big topspin smash. It bounces in the opponents' court, rebounds high off the back glass and strikes the roof structure. The ball bounced correctly first, and the force of your shot took it into the ceiling, so it is a correct return (FIP Rule 14d), and since the ball has left the playing area via an element unrelated to the court, you win the point.

3. A lob that touches the ceiling on your own side

You lob from close to your back glass and the ball brushes a beam while it is still above your half. Your shot is not a correct return (Rule 14), so your pair loses the point. Rule 13l doesn't apply here, because it covers unrelated elements on the ground on your own side, but the result is the same.

4. Right above the net

The ball touches a beam that runs directly over the net. The rulebook doesn't address the exact position above the net. In practice it makes no difference: the ball has touched something unrelated to the court before bouncing in the opponents' court, so it is not a correct return (Rule 14), and the hitter loses the point whichever side of the net you place it.

5. Your opponents' shot hits the ceiling after bouncing on your side

Your opponents hit a hard ball that bounces on your side, flies off the glass and into the lights before you can reach it. The same rule applies the other way round: it is a correct return under FIP Rule 14d, and in practice their point.

Common misconceptions

  • “Any ball that hits the ceiling is out.” Only if it hasn't yet bounced in the opponents' court. After a correct bounce, a ceiling hit is a correct return (Rule 14d).
  • “If it hits the ceiling, replay the point.” The FIP rulebook gives no let for ceiling hits. A let point comes from things such as a ball splitting or a foreign object entering the court (Rule 10), not from a shot hitting the roof.
  • “A ball that drops back into the court after hitting a light is still in play.” If it hit the light before bouncing in the opponents' court, the point was already lost (13g and 14). If it hit it after bouncing, Rule 14d makes it a correct return, so in practice the point goes to the hitter.
  • “The ceiling counts like the glass.” It doesn't. The glass is part of the court (Rule 12); the ceiling and lights are not.

Social games vs official matches

The FIP rules are clear, but some clubs with unusually low ceilings or obstructions, such as heating pipes or netting between courts, set their own house rules. If you play somewhere new, ask at reception or agree with your opponents before the first point. Common local arrangements include treating a particular beam differently or replaying points where the ball touched netting. Those are club conventions, not FIP rules, so agree them in advance rather than in the middle of a tight game.

Without an umpire, the hitting pair usually has the best view of whether a lob touched a light. The honest convention is that they call it on themselves.

Lobbing indoors: practical tips

  • Check the ceiling in the warm-up. Hit two or three lobs and see where the lights and beams are. The 3-minute courtesy rally (Rule 2) is the time to learn the building.
  • Flatten your lob. Indoors, a lower, deeper lob with a little topspin is more reliable than a towering one. You still push the opponents back without bringing the roof into play. Our guide to the lob covers the technique.
  • Aim the peak over the net, not over their service box. The highest point of a lob should come around the middle of the court, not above where it will land, so it isn't climbing into the lights over their half.
  • Use the roof on the smash. A smash that bounces and kicks high is a correct return if it reaches the ceiling (14d), which in practice wins you the point. On low-ceilinged courts, a solid topspin smash can be more effective than trying to put the ball out of the court. See our smash guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is it out if the ball hits the ceiling in padel?

If it hits the ceiling before bouncing in the opponents' court, the hitter loses the point (Rules 13g and 14). After the bounce, Rule 14d makes it a correct return, so in practice the point goes to the hitter.

What if the ball hits the lights?

The lights are treated like the ceiling: an element unrelated to the court. Before the bounce in the opponents' court, the hitter loses the point; after it, the hitter wins it (Rules 13g, 14 and 14d).

Is a ceiling hit a let in padel?

No. The FIP rulebook lists the reasons for a let point in Rule 10, and a ball hitting the ceiling is not one of them.

What is the minimum ceiling height for a padel court?

The FIP court specifications require at least 6 metres of free height across the whole court, with 8 metres recommended for new facilities.

Can you play the ball after it hits the ceiling?

No. Before the bounce in the opponents' court the point is already lost; after the bounce, Rule 14d makes it a correct return, so in practice the point goes to the hitter.
