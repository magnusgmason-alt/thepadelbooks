Playing padel in the wind, sun, heat and rain: a cheat sheet

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Playing padel in the wind means changing your lobs and overheads first, because the ball moves most once it rises above the glass. Sun, heat, cold and rain each change the game in their own way too. This cheat sheet gives you the adjustment for each outdoor condition in a table you can read between games, plus the safety points for hot days and wet courts.

Weather affects both pairs, but not equally. The pair that notices first and adjusts first usually wins the outdoor match. The Padel School's advice for outdoor play starts with patience: play a few safe balls before going for a winner, because winning overheads are harder to land outside. Before the match, look at the flags, the trees and the sun. For more on the differences between the two settings, see indoor vs outdoor padel.

Padel in the wind

The Padel School calls wind the most frustrating condition because it affects lobs and overheads most, and notes that it often has more effect above the height of the glass. A lob can travel much further, or stop much shorter, than you expect.

SituationBest adjustment
Wind at your back, lobbingHit the lob with less power and a little less height; aim shorter than usual
Wind in your face, lobbingHit harder and higher; aim deeper so it doesn't fall short
CrosswindAim lobs nearer the middle and let the wind carry them, rather than aiming at the side glass
Opponents lobbing with the wind behind themStart a step deeper at the net; be ready to turn and play it off the glass
Overheads in gusty windPrefer the controlled bandeja; let difficult lobs bounce and play them off the glass
At the net with the wind at your backThe opponents' lobs are going into the wind and tend to fall short: be ready to attack them
GeneralExtra small steps; even light wind moves the ball late

The first two rows come straight from The Padel School: with the wind behind you, hit with less power; facing it, hit harder. The rest are the sensible consequences. Track the wind every change of ends, because it rarely blows the same way for a whole match. Our padel lob guide covers the technique.

Sun in your eyes

SituationBest adjustment
Opponents facing the sunLob them more often; The Padel School says this often forces an easy reply
You facing the sun on an overheadMove so the ball isn't in line with the sun, shade your eyes with your free hand, or let it bounce and play it off the glass
Sun low behind the back glassWear a cap or visor; check reflections in the warm-up
Changing endsAgree a sun plan for each end of the court

Protect your skin as well as your eyes. The NHS recommends sunscreen of at least SPF 30 with at least 4-star UVA protection, reapplied every two hours and after sweating or towel drying, and sunglasses that meet recognised standards. In the UK it suggests staying in the shade between 11am and 3pm from March to October, which is worth remembering when booking summer courts. Heat and hydration

Heat changes the ball: The Padel School notes that balls are bouncier in hot conditions and the glass a little livelier, so lobs and smashes fly further. Shorten the lob a touch and expect more balls to come back off the back glass.

It also changes you. The American College of Sports Medicine's position stand on fluid replacement recommends starting exercise well hydrated and drinking enough during exercise to avoid losing more than 2% of body weight, with plans tailored to the individual. In practice: drink before you play, at every change of ends (you can't drink between points under FIP rules), and afterwards. In long, hot sessions, an isotonic drink helps replace the salt you lose in sweat, which the NHS also suggests for heat exhaustion.

When to stop and get help

The NHS lists these signs of heat exhaustion: tiredness, dizziness, headache, nausea, heavy sweating, cramps, a high temperature and intense thirst. Stop, move somewhere cool, drink water and cool the skin; the person should feel better within 30 minutes. Call emergency services if they don't improve, or if there's confusion, hot skin that has stopped sweating, a fast heartbeat or breathing, a seizure or loss of consciousness: those are signs of heatstroke, a medical emergency.

This section is general information, not medical advice.

Cold weather and ball bounce

EffectBest adjustment
Balls bounce lessBend lower and get closer to the ball; expect it to stay lower off the glass
Topspin smashes may not reach the back glassRely on flatter, deeper overheads or the bandeja; don't count on a smash coming out of the court
Volleys die near the back glassDeep volleys become more effective; attack the corners
Muscles take longer to warm upExtend your warm-up and keep a layer on between games

The first three rows are from The Padel School's weather guide. Fresh balls help in the cold, and our padel warm-up routine is worth doing in full on cold days.

Rain, damp and wet glass

On artificial grass, The Padel School says light rain usually doesn't make the surface much more slippery or change the bounce much, but heavy rain probably will. The bigger problem is the glass: when it's wet, flat balls can slide straight down instead of rebounding, and topspin behaves differently. Humidity can do the same, with moisture forming on the glass.

  • Wipe the glass. A towel between points or games usually restores a normal rebound, unless the rain is continuous.
  • Get behind the ball. The Padel School's advice for wet glass is to be ready to get under a ball that slides.
  • Expect a heavier, slower ball. A wet surface slows the game and lowers the bounce.
  • Stop if it's slippery. If you are sliding on the turf, the lines or the area around the court, end the session. A lost court booking is cheaper than a twisted ankle.

Outdoor match checklist

  • Check the wind direction and the sun position before the first serve.
  • Arrive early and, if you can, play 5 to 10 minutes on the court first, as The Padel School suggests.
  • Bring a cap, sunscreen, plenty of water, a towel for the glass and a warm layer.
  • Agree a plan for each end: who lobs with the wind, who watches for the sun.
  • Be patient: build the point and go for winners only when the ball is right.

Outdoor conditions reward good lobs, controlled overheads and confidence off the glass. Frequently asked questions

How do you play padel in the wind?

Adjust your lobs first: hit them softer with the wind behind you and harder into it. Prefer controlled bandejas to smashes, use extra small steps and recheck the wind direction at every change of ends.

How do you deal with the sun in padel?

Lob more when your opponents are facing it, and on your own overheads move so the ball isn't in line with the sun, or let it bounce off the glass. A cap or visor helps, and use sunscreen of at least SPF 30.

Does cold weather affect padel balls?

Yes. In the cold, balls bounce less, so topspin smashes may not reach the back glass and volleys die near the back. In heat, balls are bouncier.

Can you play padel in the rain?

In light rain, often yes on artificial grass, though wet glass makes balls slide down instead of rebounding. Wipe the glass with a towel, and stop if the surface becomes slippery.

How much should I drink when playing padel in the heat?

Start well hydrated and drink at every change of ends, aiming not to lose more than about 2% of your body weight in sweat, as sports-medicine guidance suggests. Needs vary, so adjust to how much you sweat.
