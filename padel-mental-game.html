The padel mental game: 9 habits for calm, clear matches

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Your padel mental game is what you do with the 20 seconds between points, the error you just made and the partner who is having a bad day. You don't need a sports psychologist to improve it. These nine habits come from padel coaching and sports-psychology research, and every one fits inside the time the rules allow.

Padel is a pairs game with constant pauses. Points are short and most of a match is spent between rallies, which gives your mind plenty of time to wander towards the score, the last mistake or what your partner is thinking. The habits below give that time a job.

1. Expect nerves in padel matches, and give them a job

Nerves are normal, even welcome: they mean the match matters to you. The Padel School puts mental toughness well: it isn't about being emotionless, but about managing nerves, resetting quickly after mistakes and making good decisions when it matters. Much of the pressure comes from fear of missing or of letting your partner down.

Give the nervous energy something simple to do. Agree a first-game plan with your partner, such as "first serve in, lob deep, everything through the middle", and judge yourself on that plan rather than the score. Nerves fade fastest once you are busy with a task.

2. Use the 20 seconds: a between-point routine

Under FIP Rule 2, play is meant to be continuous: you get a maximum of 20 seconds between points, and you can't eat or drink in that time. Twenty seconds is enough for a routine, and a routine is the single most useful mental tool in padel.

Sports psychologists describe pre-performance routines as a sequence of task-relevant thoughts and actions. A 2015 review by Stewart Cotterill links them to better focus, less distraction and recalling the state you want to play in, while noting that many of the studies behind them have been questioned on design. They are low-cost and low-risk, which is reason enough to try one. The Padel School suggests a breath, eye contact with your partner, or a short cue word. Here is a simple four-step version:

StepRoughlyWhat you do
Release0–5 sTurn away from the net, switch the racket to your other hand, let the last point go
Reset5–10 sOne slow, full breath; loosen your grip and shoulders
Plan10–15 sOne sentence with your partner: "Middle", "Lob the right", "Serve wide"
Ready15–20 sReady position, cue word ("feet", "deep"), eyes on the server or the ball

Do the same routine after winners and errors. That consistency is the point.

3. Breathe slower

When you're nervous your breathing speeds up and gets shallow. A 2023 narrative review in the journal Sports describes slow voluntary breathing as a way to reduce stress and anxiety in athletes, while being honest that the evidence in sport is limited and not yet conclusive. In practice, one or two slow, full breaths during your routine cost nothing and give your body a clear signal to settle. Don't count elaborate patterns during a match; keep it to one breath you can do on autopilot.

4. Reset after errors in three beats

The Padel School's advice after a mistake is to acknowledge it, identify why it happened and move on. That gives you three beats:

  1. Acknowledge: "Late on the volley." No drama.
  2. Identify: one cause you can fix, such as "racket was down" or "I tried to win it from the back".
  3. Move on: do your routine and turn your attention to positioning, tactics or your partner.

What you don't do is replay the miss, apologise five times or change your whole game because of one ball.

5. Control the controllables

You can't control the score, the opponents' best shots or the net cord. You can control shot selection, positioning and communication. Set one or two process goals for each match: "lob when both are at the net", "first serve in", "split step on every opponent's hit". Count them afterwards. A match where you lost but hit your process goals is a good match for your development.

6. Handle a bad partner day

Every partner has days when nothing goes in. The opponents will notice and send every ball their way. Your job is to keep your partner in the match, not to win it alone.

Help

Give information, not emotion: "high ball next time, let's lob". Encourage straight after their error, then talk about the next point. Give them the safest job, such as deep lobs to one opponent, and take the middle balls you can reach cleanly.

Hurt

Sighs, shrugs, silence, "why did you hit that?", or poaching everything and leaving holes behind you. A tight partner misses more, not less.

HEAD's coaching advice is a neat summary: after your own error, look ahead; after your partner's, encourage them quickly. Our cheat sheet on padel communication has the exact phrases, and our guide to playing with a stronger or weaker partner covers the tactics.

7. Focus on the golden point: make them play

At 40–40 under golden point scoring, one rally decides the game. It is easy to try something special. Don't. An analysis of professional padel by Escudero-Tena and colleagues (2022) found that 61.5% of men's and 59.4% of women's golden points ended in an error rather than a winner. If the best players in the world mostly lose golden points by missing, your plan at club level is clear: get the first serve in, play through the middle, lob deep if they are both at the net, and let them make the mistake.

Decide your golden point plan before the match, including which of you receives. The receiving pair chooses, and our golden point guide explains the rules.

8. Use the changeovers for a quick review

FIP Rule 2 allows up to 90 seconds at a change of ends (except after the first game of a set, when you change without a rest) and up to 120 seconds between sets. Use part of it for three questions with your partner: What is working? What is costing us points? What is one change? Then drink, towel off and stop talking tactics. One clear change beats five half-ideas.

9. Train pressure, don't just hope for it

Mental skills improve the same way shots do: with practice. The Padel School recommends practising under mild pressure, with process goals and an honest review afterwards. Add a consequence to your drills: a "yellow card, red card" game, where a second error in the same game loses it, is a good start. Building your padel mental game on match day

Pick two habits for your next match, not nine: usually the between-point routine and the three-beat reset. Once they feel automatic, add the golden point plan and the changeover review. Our padel match day routine shows where each fits, and if you are about to enter your first competition, read what to expect at your first padel tournament. Keep a note of how the habits went after each match: did you do the routine, did the reset work, did you stick to the golden point plan? Mental skills are easy to forget the moment you lose a few points in a row, and a one-line note is enough to remind you next week. Frequently asked questions

How do I stop being nervous in padel matches?

You probably won't stop entirely, and you don't need to. Give the nerves a job: a simple first-game plan, a between-point routine and one slow breath. Nerves fade fastest once you are focused on a task.

How long do you have between points in padel?

A maximum of 20 seconds under FIP Rule 2, and you can't eat or drink in that time. Changes of ends allow up to 90 seconds, and the break between sets up to 120 seconds.

What should I do after a bad mistake in padel?

Acknowledge it, name one cause you can fix, then move on with your routine. Turn your attention to the next point, your position or your partner rather than replaying the miss.

How do I help a partner who is playing badly?

Encourage quickly, give clear information rather than emotion, simplify their job and take the middle balls you can reach cleanly. Avoid sighs, silence and blame.

How should we play the golden point?

Simply. In professional padel most golden points end in an error, so get the first serve in, play through the middle, lob deep when both opponents are at the net and make them play one more ball.
