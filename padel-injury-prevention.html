Padel injury prevention: the 15-minute routine and the habits behind it

4 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 15 Nov 2026

Most padel injuries aren't accidents. They happen when the game asks more of a calf, knee, shoulder or elbow than it is ready to give, usually after a jump in playing time, a cold start or a long layoff. That makes them largely preventable. Below is a 15-minute routine you can do twice a week, the habits that matter on match days, and what the research says about each.

General information, not medical advice

This page explains common patterns and general training principles. It can't diagnose you or replace an assessment. If you have a heart condition, a recent injury or any medical concern, check with a doctor or physiotherapist before starting a new programme.

Why padel players get injured

A 2023 systematic review of padel injuries (Dahmen and colleagues) found the elbow and knee among the most frequently injured areas, with the shoulder, back, calf and ankle also common. A narrative review in the Aspetar Sports Medicine Journal lists the risk factors that keep coming up: age, a poor warm-up, low conditioning, too much play too soon, and equipment such as heavy rackets or the wrong shoes.

Most of those are things you can change. The biggest lever is how much and how suddenly you load your body. A calf that copes with two matches a week may not cope with five in a holiday week.

The habits that matter most

  1. Build up gradually. Add playing time a little at a time, and avoid doubling your weekly matches overnight. See how often to play padel.
  2. Warm up every time. Ten minutes of raise, mobilise, activate and potentiate. Our warm-up guide has the full routine.
  3. Strength train twice a week. A 2014 meta-analysis (Lauersen and colleagues) found strength training reduced sports injuries to less than a third, a much bigger effect than stretching.
  4. Wear padel shoes for your surface. Running shoes don't support lateral movement. See padel shoes.
  5. Clear loose balls. Stepping on a ball is one of the classic ways to roll an ankle in padel.
  6. Respect early warning signs. Pain that builds during play or lingers the next morning means reduce the load, not push through.

The 15-minute prevention routine

Do this twice a week, on days you're not playing, or after a light session. It targets the areas the injury research flags most often.

ExerciseDoseWhat it protects
Single-leg calf raise (straight knee, then bent knee)2 × 12 eachCalf and Achilles
Single-leg balance, eyes open then closed2 × 30 s eachAnkle sprain recurrence
Split squat or slow step-down2 × 8 eachKnee
Copenhagen side plank (short lever, knee on bench)2 × 15–20 s eachGroin
Nordic hamstring curl (partner holds ankles)2 × 4–6Hamstring
Side plank2 × 20–30 s eachTrunk and lower back
Band external rotation2 × 15 eachShoulder (rotator cuff)
Wrist extension with a light weight, slow lowering2 × 15Elbow (padel elbow)

Several of these have trial evidence from other sports. Balance training lowered ankle sprain recurrences in a 2009 BMJ trial (Hupperets and colleagues). Programmes including the Nordic hamstring exercise roughly halved hamstring injury rates in a 2019 meta-analysis (van Dyk and colleagues), although a later reappraisal judged the evidence less certain. The Copenhagen adduction exercise lowered groin problems in a 2019 football trial (Harøy and colleagues). None of these trials were in padel, so treat them as the best available guide rather than a guarantee.

On match days

  • Arrive early enough to warm up properly, not just hit a few balls.
  • Drink regularly, especially in heat. See what to eat before padel.
  • In a tournament, keep moving gently between matches and repeat a short warm-up before the next one.
  • Play smart: good positioning means fewer desperate lunges.

When to stop and get checked

NHS advice on sports injuries is to get urgent help if you heard a snap or crack, can't put weight on a leg, a joint looks deformed, or there is numbness. Pain that isn't improving after a week or two of sensible rest deserves a GP or physiotherapist.

Frequently asked questions

How do I prevent padel injuries?

Build up playing time gradually, warm up before every game, do two short strength sessions a week, wear padel shoes for your surface and clear loose balls from the court.

What is the most common padel injury?

Studies most often report the elbow, followed by the knee, shoulder, lower back, calf and ankle. The exact order varies between studies.

Does stretching prevent padel injuries?

On its own, stretching has little effect on injury risk. Strength training and good warm-ups have much stronger evidence.

How long should a padel injury prevention routine take?

About 15 minutes, twice a week, plus a 10-minute warm-up before you play.

Are padel injuries more common over 40?

Yes, some are. Calf strains in particular are typical of less-conditioned players over 40, which makes strength work and a proper warm-up more important with age.
