Padel vs squash: what's the difference?

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 30 Oct 2026

Padel vs squash, in short: both use walls, but padel is a doubles game played over a net on a 20 × 10 metre court with tennis scoring, while squash is usually a singles game where both players hit against the same front wall in a much smaller room, scoring rally points to 11. That one difference, net versus front wall, changes almost everything else. Here is a side-by-side comparison based on the official FIP and World Squash rules.

Is padel like squash?

A little. Padel borrows the idea of walls from squash: the ball can come off the glass and stay in play, and reading rebounds is a big part of the game. Many people describe padel as tennis with squash walls.

But the way the walls work is different. In squash, the walls are the target: every shot has to reach the front wall, and you can use the side and back walls to get it there. In padel, there's a net in the middle and the walls are mostly your defence. Your shot must bounce on the opponents' floor before it touches their walls. Hit their glass directly and you lose the point.

The court and the walls

A padel court is 20 metres long and 10 metres wide, enclosed by glass at the ends and a mix of glass and metal mesh along the sides, with a net 88 cm high in the middle. The service lines sit 6.95 metres from the net.

A World Squash singles court is 9.75 metres long and 6.4 metres wide. On the front wall there's an out line at 4.57 metres, a service line at 1.78 metres, and a tin at the bottom, 48 cm high (43 cm in PSA events). A good shot must hit the front wall above the tin and below the out line before it bounces on the floor.

Padel: allowed

The ball bounces on your side, comes off your back glass, and you return it over the net. You can also hit it into your own glass so it rebounds over the net.

Padel: point lost

Your shot hits the opponents' glass or mesh before bouncing on their floor. The squash-style shot into the opponents' side wall doesn't exist in padel.

Rackets and balls

Squash rackets are long, light and strung: up to 68.6 cm and 255 g under the rules, though most weigh far less. A padel racket is solid, with no strings and a perforated face, no longer than 45.5 cm. It's much heavier: our racket guide uses about 370 g as the line between light and heavy padel rackets.

The balls feel nothing alike. A squash ball is small and soft. The competition ball, the double yellow dot, barely bounces until it's warm, which is why squash players spend the warm-up heating it. A padel ball looks like a tennis ball, slightly smaller and with a little less pressure, and bounces from the first hit.

Serving and scoring

In padel you bounce the ball and serve underarm, at or below waist height, diagonally into the service box. You get two attempts, and the serve passes to the other pair after each game. Points are counted like tennis, and at 40–40 you play advantage, the golden point or the Star Point. See padel scoring.

In squash, the server stands with one foot in a service box and hits the ball straight onto the front wall, between the service line and the out line, so that it lands in the back quarter of the court on the opposite side. The server keeps serving for as long as they win rallies. Every rally scores a point, games go to 11 with a two-point margin at 10–10, and matches are usually best of five.

Which is harder, padel or squash?

There's no measurement that settles this, so here's an honest comparison rather than a verdict.

  • Harder to start: squash, for most people. The small, soft ball, the low tin and the speed of the game make early rallies short. In padel, the underarm serve, bigger ball and walls that keep the ball in play mean most beginners can rally in their first session.
  • Harder physically: usually squash. You cover the whole court on your own, with very little rest in a rally. Padel is shared with a partner and has more pauses, although a competitive padel match is still demanding.
  • Harder to master: both. Padel's difficulty is tactical and technical: playing off the back glass, overheads such as the bandeja and the víbora, and moving as a pair. Squash's is movement, fitness and precise length and width.

Padel vs squash: which burns more calories?

Squash, almost certainly, but be careful with precise figures. The standard reference, the 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities, lists "squash, general" at 7.3 METs and a second squash entry (Taylor Code 530) at 12.0 METs. It has no entry for padel at all. The closest values are for doubles tennis: 4.5 and 6.0 METs.

Padel for squash players

Squash players usually take to padel quickly. Some of your habits help; others need unlearning.

What transfers

  • Reading the walls. You already know how a ball comes off glass. Letting the ball rebound off the back wall and playing it as it drops is natural to you.
  • Movement and fitness. Quick first steps and recovering to a central position after each shot both carry over.
  • The grip. The Padel School notes that squash players usually arrive with a natural continental grip, which is the standard grip in padel too.

What to change

  • Land it first. The biggest adjustment: the ball must bounce on the opponents' floor before it touches their walls. Squash instincts to play into the side wall cost points.
  • Shorter, firmer strokes. The Padel School lists wristy shots, swinging volleys and the high elbow of squash groundstrokes as the habits squash players most often have to change, and says habits built over years can be hard to break. LTA Padel recommends a short backswing on both sides.
  • Control over power. LTA Padel's advice for beginners is that padel is more about accuracy and placement than power.
  • Play as a pair. You share the court. Move with your partner and don't chase every ball. Our doubles positioning guide shows where to stand.
  • Learn the overheads. The bandeja, víbora and smash have no real equivalent in squash. Start with how to hit a bandeja.

Which should you play?

Choose squash if you want an intense workout in a short time, enjoy one-on-one competition and have a court nearby. Squash will also be on the Olympic programme at Los Angeles 2028. Choose padel if you want a social game with friends, longer rallies sooner, and a sport that is quick to pick up. Many players do both.

Frequently asked questions

Is padel like squash?

Partly. Both use walls, but in padel there's a net and your shot must bounce on the opponents' side before it hits their walls. In squash, every shot must reach the front wall.

Is padel easier than squash?

Easier to start, for most people. The underarm serve and the bigger, bouncier ball let beginners rally quickly. Both take years to master.

Does squash burn more calories than padel?

Usually. The 2024 Compendium of Physical Activities rates general squash at 7.3 METs, higher than doubles tennis at 4.5 to 6.0, which is the closest listed activity to padel. Padel itself isn't listed.

Can you use a squash racket for padel?

No. Padel requires a solid, stringless racket no longer than 45.5 cm with a compulsory wrist cord, under the FIP rules.

Are squash players good at padel?

They often adapt quickly, because they read walls well and move well. The main adjustments are letting the ball bounce before it reaches the opponents' walls, shortening the swing and playing as a pair.
