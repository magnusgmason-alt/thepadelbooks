Padel drills for beginners

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

The best padel drills for beginners are simple, repeatable exercises that build control first: short rallies, soft volleys, a reliable serve and comfort with the back glass. Below are eight drills you can run in a normal one-hour court booking, with the set-up, the scoring and how to make each one harder once it feels easy.

How to use these padel drills for beginners

Beginners improve fastest when every drill has a number attached. A number turns "having a hit" into practice: you know whether you are getting better, and you know when a drill is too easy. Pick three or four drills per session, play each for ten to fifteen minutes, and finish with points so the new habits meet a real game.

  • Control before power. Every drill below rewards keeping the ball in play. Speed comes later.
  • Use a continental grip for volleys and the serve. It's the grip coaches teach for net play because you don't have time to change it there (see our volley guide).
  • Warm up first. Five minutes of easy mini-rallies and some movement is enough. Our padel warm-up has a full routine.
  • Restart quickly. Keep a few balls in your pocket. Less time collecting balls means more touches.

Control drills

1. Ladder rally

Players: 2 · Time: 10 minutes

Setup: Use one half of the court, split lengthways. Both players stand around the service line on opposite sides of the net.

How it works: Rally co-operatively. Your first target is two shots in a row, then three, then four, and so on. Any mistake sends you back to two. This is a format the LTA recommends for building consistency.

Goal: Reach a rally of ten. Write down your best ladder at the end of each session.

Progression: Put one player at the net volleying and the other at the back letting the ball bounce. Swap after every completed ladder.

2. Soft volley chain

Players: 2 · Time: 10 minutes

Setup: Both players at the net, about two metres back, racket up in front of the body.

How it works: Volley gently to each other without letting the ball bounce. Think of each volley as a firm block, not a swing, with the contact just in front of your body. Keep the ball below head height so nobody has to reach.

Goal: Twenty volleys in a row as a pair. Count out loud.

Progression: Alternate forehand and backhand volleys on purpose, or aim every volley at your partner's feet so they have to bend their knees.

Shot drills

3. Serve to the box

Players: 1 or 2 · Time: 10 minutes

Setup: A bucket of balls. Place a target such as a racket cover or towel deep in the diagonal service box.

How it works: Serve the way the rules require: bounce the ball behind the service line in your own box, then strike it at or below waist height with at least one foot on the ground (FIP Rule 6). Hit ten serves from the right, then ten from the left. A serve that bounces in the box and then hits the mesh is a fault; one that comes off the glass is fine. Our guide to the serve rules covers the details.

Goal: Eight out of ten in the box, then three out of ten landing near the target.

Progression: Add a receiver who returns cross-court. The server scores if the serve lands in the back third of the box, or the return lands short of the server's service line.

4. Back-glass catch

Players: 2 · Time: 10–15 minutes

Setup: Both players on the same side of the net. The feeder stands near the service line; the hitter starts a couple of metres in front of the back glass.

How it works: The feeder throws or gently hits the ball so it bounces just in front of the back glass. The hitter lets it rebound, turns side-on, and returns it softly so the feeder can catch it. Coaches teach the same pattern: turn side-on, take the racket back early, use small steps, and swing low to high.

Goal: Ten clean returns into the feeder's hands, alternating forehand and backhand.

Progression: Feed into a back corner so the ball comes off two walls, then return over the net instead of to the feeder. Our back glass guide explains the footwork.

5. Lob ladder

Players: 2 · Time: 10 minutes

Setup: One player at the back, one at the net. Mark the zone between the net player's service line and the back glass.

How it works: The net player feeds a gentle ball. The back player lobs over them into the zone. The net player does not try to intercept; they just watch where it lands.

Goal: Count lobs that clear the net player's reach and land beyond the service line without hitting the back glass on the full. Seven out of ten is a strong score for a beginner.

Progression: Let the net player reach with a raised racket. Any lob they can touch doesn't count. Our lob guide covers the technique.

Movement and teamwork drills

6. Split-step shadow

Players: 2 · Time: 5 minutes

Setup: One player at the net with a ball, the other at the net on the opposite side, no racket needed at first.

How it works: The leader pretends to hit, pointing left, right or up. The mover does a small hop, landing just as the leader "makes contact", then moves two quick steps in the direction shown. The Padel School describes the timing: land the split step as your opponent hits.

Goal: Ten reps where the landing matches the contact exactly.

Progression: Add rackets and a real feed, so every split step is followed by a volley.

7. Call the middle

Players: 3 (two hitters and a feeder) · Time: 10 minutes

Setup: The pair stands at the net side by side. The feeder stands on the other side with a basket.

How it works: The feeder sends balls down the centre. Before every shot, one player calls "mine" or "yours". Agree the rule first: usually the player with the forehand in the middle takes it.

Goal: Any ball that isn't called, or that both players go for, is a point to the feeder. Play to ten.

Progression: Mix in lobs down the middle so the pair also has to decide who takes the overhead. Our positioning guide covers the usual agreements.

8. Yellow card, red card

Players: 2 or 4 · Time: 15 minutes

Setup: Half-court singles for two players, the full court for four.

How it works: Play normal points. Your team's first mistake in a game is a yellow card (in doubles, either partner's mistake counts). A second mistake in the same game is a red card, and you lose that game. This is an LTA game, built around the idea that padel is a game of consistency.

Goal: Win the most games in fifteen minutes.

Progression: Our own variation: make it one card only, so a single mistake loses the game.

A simple one-hour beginner session

MinutesDrillFocus
0–8Warm-up and mini-ralliesFeel for the ball
8–18Ladder rallyConsistency
18–28Soft volley chainNet control
28–40Back-glass catchWalls
40–48Serve to the boxStarting the point
48–60Yellow card, red cardApplying it under pressure

Change the middle drills from week to week, but keep the warm-up and the game at the end. Over a month you'll cover every drill on this page at least twice.

Track three numbers

Your best ladder rally, your volley chain record and serves in out of ten. If all three are going up, your padel is too. When they stop moving, add the progression.

Common mistakes in beginner drills

  • Hitting too hard. A drill that keeps breaking down teaches nothing. Slow the ball until you can reach the target.
  • Chasing the ball into the glass. In the back-glass drills, step back early and wait for the ball to come out to you.
  • Standing on the service line. Mini-rallies near the service line are fine; in game drills, don't park there. Pass through mid-court on your way to the net or the back.
  • Too many drills. Three drills done properly beat eight done in a rush.

Once these feel easy, move on to our intermediate drills, and read up on beginner tactics so the skills fit into a game plan.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best padel drill for beginners?

If you only do one, make it the ladder rally. It builds control, rewards patience and works with any partner. Add back-glass practice as soon as you can, because the walls are what make padel different from tennis.

How long should a beginner padel drill session be?

An hour is plenty. Spend about five to ten minutes warming up, forty minutes on three or four drills, and the rest playing points.

Can I do padel drills without a coach?

Yes. Every drill on this page can be run by players themselves. A coach helps with technique and feeding, but drills with clear targets are useful on their own.

Should beginners practise the bandeja?

Not first. Build reliable volleys, lobs and back-glass returns, then learn the bandeja once you can hold a rally at the net.

How many balls do I need for drills?

At least six, and ideally a basket of twenty or more for feeding drills. More balls mean less time collecting and more repetitions.
