The padel backhand: technique, grip and volley

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

A good padel backhand is short, side-on and controlled: you turn your shoulders early, keep the backswing compact with your free hand on the racket, and push through the ball in front of your body using the continental grip. It doesn't need tennis power. Here is how to hit it, how to volley on the backhand side and when two hands make sense.

Why the padel backhand is different from tennis

In tennis, the backhand is a big shot from the baseline. In padel, the court is smaller, the ball is slightly slower and the walls give you a second chance. LTA Padel stresses getting prepared and in position early, and using a short backswing because you don't need much power. The job of most backhands is to keep the ball low and deep, or to set up a lob or chiquita, not to hit winners.

That is good news for beginners. A compact, reliable backhand is easier to learn than a tennis backhand, and it wins more points.

Padel backhand grip

Use the continental grip, the same grip you use for volleys and overheads (see our padel grip guide). The face sits slightly open, which helps you keep the ball low and lets you play a flat ball, a lob or a chiquita from the same set-up.

The Padel School warns against a tennis-style backhand grip. It produces topspin backhands that work, but aren't very effective in padel, and it tells your opponents what's coming. An open face keeps your options hidden.

Padel backhand technique, step by step

These steps are for a right-hander. Left-handers mirror them.

  1. Ready position. Feet about shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent, weight forward, and keep the feet moving. NOX stresses that you shouldn't stay still while you wait for the ball.
  2. Turn early. As soon as you read the ball, turn your shoulders so your right shoulder points towards the ball and your feet face the side wall. The shoulder turn is your main source of power.
  3. Free hand on the throat. Take the racket back with your left hand on the throat. It guides the racket and keeps the backswing short.
  4. Keep the backswing compact. The racket shouldn't go higher than your shoulder. Coaches commonly suggest around waist height for a normal groundstroke.
  5. Adjust with small steps, then plant your feet before you hit.
  6. Contact in front. Hit the ball level with your front foot or slightly ahead of it, never beside or behind your hip. For low balls, bend your knees more rather than dropping the racket head.
  7. Release the free hand and push through. Your left hand lets go as you swing, and your weight moves from back foot to front foot.
  8. Finish towards the target, arm extended and racket face pointing where you want the ball to go, then recover.

Padel backhand slice: chop or push?

Many padel backhands carry a little slice, which keeps the ball low and makes it die off the glass. But coaches warn against overdoing it. The Padel School's view is clear: a steep downward chop gives you spin but very little power, and it often comes from holding the racket too high in the backswing. Their fix is to swing more horizontally through the ball and to keep the end of the racket level with the ball.

Other guides describe glass and defensive backhands as usually being played with slice for control. Both views fit together: a gentle high-to-low path with an open face is fine; a big vertical chop is not. If your backhand keeps floating or dying in the net, flatten the swing.

The padel backhand volley

At the net, the backhand volley is a punch, not a swing. Our volley guide covers it in full, but the essentials are:

  • Same continental grip, so there's no change between forehand and backhand volleys.
  • Turn the shoulders a little more than on the forehand side and keep the elbow in front of the body.
  • Tiny backswing. On fast balls the shoulder turn is enough.
  • Contact in front, face slightly open, short follow-through high to low.
  • Stand about two metres from the net, so you can step into the ball and still cover the lob.

Many players find the backhand volley easier to control than the forehand volley once the grip is right, because the arm naturally stays in front of the body.

Two-handed backhand in padel

Two hands are allowed. The FIP rulebook contains no rule against two-handed shots or switching hands. Whether you should use one is a coaching question, and the sources weigh it up like this:

Two-handed: pros

More stability and leverage on body-height balls, natural for former tennis players, useful on returns and some side-wall balls. NOX also notes it may reduce strain on the elbow.

Two-handed: cons

Less reach on wide and deep balls, awkward off the back glass, harder on low balls, no real backspin, and padel handles are short for two hands.

NOX suggests beginners without a racket-sport background start one-handed, and recommends the two-handed version mainly for balls that don't involve the wall. Padel Browser suggests a hybrid: two hands when the ball sits up near your body, one hand when you have to reach or play off the back glass. If you come from tennis, that hybrid is a sensible middle ground.

Padel backhand vs forehand

Most club players feel more confident on the forehand, but in padel the backhand is often just as reliable, for three reasons. The grip doesn't change. The free hand on the throat makes the backswing naturally compact. And the arm sits in front of the body, which helps control. The forehand usually gives you a little more power and reach. For the other side, see our padel forehand guide.

Where to aim your backhand

  • Cross-court and deep when the opponents are at the back: the longest distance and most margin.
  • Low at the feet of net players, so they have to volley upwards.
  • A lob when both opponents are at the net and you're under pressure.

Common padel backhand mistakes

  1. Late preparation. Turn as soon as you read the ball, and shorten the backswing.
  2. Hitting beside or behind the hip. Contact belongs in front, near the front foot.
  3. Racket too high in the backswing, which turns the stroke into a chop.
  4. Reaching instead of moving. Use small steps to bring your body to the ball.
  5. Slowing the racket down at contact. Commit to a smooth follow-through.
  6. Taking steps during the swing. Plant, hit, then move.

A simple backhand practice routine

Ask a partner to feed balls to your backhand from the net. Start with ten balls hit cross-court past the service line, then ten low down the line, then ten lobs from the same set-up. Finish with balls off the back glass, where many backhands are played in matches.

Frequently asked questions

What grip should I use for a padel backhand?

The continental grip. It keeps the face slightly open and is the same grip you use for volleys, so you never need to change at the net.

Is a two-handed backhand allowed in padel?

Yes. The FIP rules don't prohibit two-handed shots or switching hands. Whether it suits you depends on your background and the ball you're playing.

Should a padel backhand be sliced?

A little slice with a gentle high-to-low swing is common and helps keep the ball low. A steep downward chop is a mistake: it gives spin but little power.

Where should I contact the ball on a backhand?

In front of your body, roughly level with your front foot. Contact beside or behind your hip makes the shot late and weak.

Is the backhand harder than the forehand in padel?

Not necessarily. Many players find the padel backhand compact and reliable, especially on volleys. The forehand usually has slightly more power and reach.
