Where can players stand in padel? Positions and the Australian formation

8 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

In padel only the server's position is fixed; the receiver, the receiver's partner and the server's partner may stand anywhere on their own side of the net (FIP Rules 3 and 6.1). That freedom is what makes tactics such as the Australian formation legal. The limits come from other rules: where the server's feet go, staying off the opponents' court, and being inside the court when you hit.

The short answer

Only the server's position is fixed (Rule 6.1: at least one foot behind the service line, inside the service box). The receiver, the receiver's partner and the server's partner may stand anywhere on their own side (Rule 3). The receiver just has to let the serve bounce in the box (Rule 8.1). During the rally you can move anywhere on your side, but you must not touch the net or the opponents' court (FIP Rule 13a), and you must be inside the court when you hit unless out-of-court play is authorised (FIP Rule 13p).

What the rules say

Rule 3: positions. The rule describes the server and receiver as diagonally opposite, but "diagonal" only defines which opponent receives the serve. Who receives each point is fixed by the receiving order (Rules 8.2 and 8.3) and the alternating service boxes (Rule 6.5). The receiver, the receiver's partner and the server's partner may all stand anywhere on their own side of the court.

Rules 6.1 to 6.4: the server's feet. The server stands with at least one foot behind the service line, inside the service box area (between the extension of the central line and the side wall), and stays there until the serve is struck (6.1). The server may not touch the service line or the imaginary extension of the central line with either foot (6.3), and at least one foot must be on the ground at contact (6.4).

Rule 8.1: the receiver. The rulebook does not fix a spot for the receiver, but the receiver must let the serve bounce in the receiving box before hitting it. That is why receivers stand back, usually behind the service line, giving the ball room to bounce and come up.

Rule 13: the limits during the point. You lose the point if you, your racket or anything you wear or carry touches the net, the posts, the tension cable or any part of the opponents' court while the ball is in play (13a). You lose it if you jump over the net (13n). And you lose it if you hit the ball with one foot, both feet or any part of your body outside the court, unless out-of-court play is authorised (13p, with Rule 16 on when that is possible).

Allowed

Server's partner at the net on the same half as the server. Receiver's partner at the net or at the back. Both players on the same half of the court during a rally. Crouching at the net in the middle of your own side (FIP Rule 3).

Not allowed

Server standing on or across the service line or central line (Rule 6.3). Touching the net or stepping into the opponents' court during the point (Rule 13a). Hitting with a foot outside the court when out-of-court play isn't authorised (Rule 13p).

The Australian formation

The Australian formation is a tactic, not a rule. The server's partner stands at the net on the same half of the court as the server, instead of the usual diagonal position. After the serve, the server covers the other half. The aim is to block the receiver's favourite crosscourt return and force them to play down the line, often into a less comfortable shot.

It is completely legal, because Rule 3 lets partners stand anywhere on their own side. The only extra risk is Rule 7d: if the serve hits the server's partner, it is a fault. With your partner standing between you and the net on your half, aim your serve accordingly.

A close relative is the "I" formation, where the server's partner crouches near the middle of the net and moves left or right after the serve, sometimes using a pre-agreed signal. This is also legal under Rule 3, as long as the partner stays on their own side and doesn't touch the net (Rule 13a).

Real match situations

Your partner stands right in front of you at the serve

Legal (FIP Rule 3). If your serve clips them before bouncing, though, it is a fault (FIP Rule 7d). Either serve wider of them or ask them to move half a metre.

The server's foot is on the central line

Fault. The server must not touch the service line or the imaginary extension of the central line with either foot (FIP Rules 6.3 and 7a). The rule applies to the server only; partners can stand where they like.

The receiver stands right up at the service line to take the ball early

Allowed, as the rulebook does not fix the receiver's spot. What matters is that the receiver lets the serve bounce in the box and hits it before the second bounce (FIP Rule 8.1). Stand too close and you will struggle to let a fast serve bounce and still play it well.

Both players end up on the same half during a rally

Allowed. Rule 3 places no restriction on partners' positions on their own side. It is usually poor tactics, since it leaves half the court open, but it is never a rule breach.

You chase a ball and your foot lands in the opponents' court

If this happens while the ball is in play, you lose the point (FIP Rule 13a). Reaching over the net without touching anything is a different question, covered in can you hit the ball on the other side of the net.

You run out through the door to hit a ball

Only legal if out-of-court play is authorised, which requires the court and safety area to meet the specifications (FIP Rules 16 and 13p). On most club courts it isn't, so hitting from outside loses the point. See out-of-court play for the full rules.

Common misconceptions

  • "The server's partner must stand on the opposite half." No rule says so. Partners may stand anywhere on their own side (FIP Rule 3).
  • "The receiver's partner must stay back until the serve is returned." No. They can stand at the net if they like (FIP Rule 3).
  • "The Australian formation is a trick that pros get away with." It is fully within the rules. The only catch is that hitting your own partner with the serve is a fault (FIP Rule 7d).
  • "You can stand anywhere as long as you don't hit the ball." Not quite. Touching the opponents' court is a lost point whether you hit or not (FIP Rule 13a), and a ball from the opponents that touches your body is a lost point (FIP Rule 13k). Rule 13k applies wherever you are standing; it makes no inside/outside distinction.

Social games vs official matches

Position rules rarely cause arguments in social padel, with two exceptions. First, server foot faults on the central line are almost never called without an umpire. If you want to play properly, agree at the start that the receiving pair can call obvious foot faults. Second, out-of-court play: at most clubs it is not authorised, so agree before playing that the court boundary is the limit.

Tactical tip

Try the Australian formation for a few points when the receiver is beating you with the same crosscourt return. It changes their picture, and many club players have never practised the down-the-line return. Pair it with a serve to the middle so the angle to the open side is narrow. Frequently asked questions

Is the Australian formation legal in padel?

Yes. Rule 3 allows partners to stand anywhere on their own side, including on the same half as the server.

Where must the receiver stand?

Anywhere on their own side. Only the server's position is fixed (Rule 6.1: at least one foot behind the service line, inside the service box). The receiver, the receiver's partner and the server's partner may stand anywhere on their own side (Rule 3). The receiver just has to let the serve bounce in the box (Rule 8.1).

Can the server's partner stand in the service box?

The partner may stand anywhere on their own side (FIP Rule 3). Just remember that a serve hitting your partner is a fault (FIP Rule 7d).

Can I stand outside the court during a rally?

You must be inside the court when you hit unless out-of-court play is authorised (FIP Rule 13p). A ball hit by the opponents that touches your body counts against you (FIP Rule 13k); that rule applies wherever you are standing and makes no inside/outside distinction.

Can the server step over the service line?

No. The server keeps at least one foot behind the service line and must not touch it or the central line with either foot until the serve is struck (FIP Rules 6.1 and 6.3).
