Tennis to padel: 9 tennis habits that hurt your padel

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Moving from tennis to padel feels easy for the first few weeks and then strangely hard, and the reason is usually a handful of tennis habits that work against you on a walled court. Below are nine to unlearn, each with a fix you can take to your next game. For the full list of differences between the sports, see padel vs tennis; for structured practice, our padel drills for tennis players.

None of this means tennis is a bad background. The Padel School lists good power and the split step among the strengths tennis players bring, and hand skills carry over quickly. The trouble is that some tennis instincts are so automatic you don't notice them. The Padel School's warning is blunt: it is much easier to shape the technique of a new player than to change bad habits, so fix these early.

1. The big, loopy swing

The habit: a full tennis take-back on every groundstroke. The Padel School names "the loopy swings of tennis groundstrokes" as one of the first things to change.

Why it hurts: in a 10 by 10 metre half with the ball coming off walls, you rarely have time for it, and the LTA notes that padel is built on short, sharp racket movements while tennis uses bigger swings for power.

The fix: take the racket back no further than your shoulder turn allows and finish towards the target. If you're late, the swing was too long.

2. Hitting hard from the back

The habit: trying to drive through the opponents from the baseline.

Why it hurts: the LTA puts it simply: in padel, more powerful shots are more likely to come back after rebounding off the glass. A hard, deep drive gives the net pair a ball at a perfect height.

The fix: from the back, play controlled balls that stay low after the bounce, and use the lob to change the point. Save pace for balls you can hit downwards.

3. Avoiding the glass

The habit: rushing forward to take every deep ball early, including half volleys behind the service line, because in tennis a ball that gets past you is gone.

Why it hurts: the LTA calls the back glass the biggest challenge for tennis players at first, and The Padel School lists wall avoidance among their main difficulties. Taking the ball early gives you less time, not more.

The fix: turn side-on, let the ball come off the glass, wait for it to drop and play it in front of you. The LTA's encouragement is worth remembering: once you get used to it, the back glass becomes a huge support. Start with our back glass guide.

4. Playing every ball in front of you

The habit: moving forward to meet the ball wherever it is. The LTA lists "your instinct might be to play every ball in front of you" as a tennis reflex.

Why it hurts: in padel, moving back and to the side to let the ball come to you is often the better option, especially on balls heading for the corners.

The fix: on any deep ball, your first move is a turn and a step back, not a step in.

5. Changing grips all the time

The habit: a semi-western forehand, a different backhand grip, another for volleys. Padel Browser notes that tennis teaches you to rotate between four or five grips mid-rally.

Why it hurts: exchanges at the net are too fast for grip changes, and a closed tennis forehand grip makes volleys and overheads awkward.

The fix: make the continental grip your base for almost everything. Coaches do differ slightly here: Padel Browser and some other guides allow a slightly more eastern forehand grip for drives from the back when you have time, but advise sticking to continental for everything in your first months. Our padel grip guide shows how to find it.

6. Treating the serve as a weapon

The habit: going for aces and big kick serves, then charging in behind anything.

Why it hurts: the padel serve is underarm, after a bounce, hit at or below waist height (FIP Rule 6). It is a set-up shot, not a point-winner, and serving hard just makes it more likely the ball comes back off the glass at a comfortable height for the returner.

The fix: serve for placement and consistency, usually deep towards the glass or into the body, and move in to your net position as you would in any padel point. The goal is a weak return, not an ace.

7. Smashing every lob

The habit: treating every lob as a smash opportunity. The Padel School lists controlling the smash among tennis players' typical difficulties.

Why it hurts: a smash that stays in the court often bounces off the back glass and comes straight back, while you've lost your balance and position.

The fix: learn the bandeja, the controlled overhead whose only job is to keep you at the net. Smash only the short, easy lobs. See how to hit a bandeja.

8. Rushing the net, and staying there no matter what

The habit: coming in behind a weak ball, then holding the net even when the point has turned. The Padel School notes tennis players "often find it difficult changing their mindset during a point": once they attack, they continue regardless.

Why it hurts: in padel the pair at the net is usually favoured, but only if they get there behind a good ball. A deep lob over your head means it's time to give up the net together and regroup.

The fix: come forward behind lobs and low, slow balls, not behind drives. When you're lobbed and can't play a good overhead, retreat with your partner and fight for the net again.

9. Going for winners too early

The habit: drop shots and outright winners from difficult positions. The Padel School lists going for drop shots and winners too early as a common tennis-player trap.

Why it hurts: the walls keep the ball alive, so most padel points are won by the pair who make fewer errors and build better positions.

The fix: count to three before you go for it: the third good ball in a sequence is usually the one that creates the chance.

Habits worth keeping

Your volleys, your hand skills and especially your split step. The Padel School notes that tennis players who were taught the split step already do it, so keep landing as the opponent hits.

Tennis to padel: a plan for your first month

  • Week 1: back glass only. Every warm-up, let ten balls come off the glass on each side.
  • Week 2: one grip. Play a whole session with the continental grip, even on the forehand drive.
  • Week 3: no smash. Play bandejas on every lob, even the easy ones, to learn the shape.
  • Week 4: lob to win the net. Count how many times you and your partner take the net after your own lob.

Frequently asked questions

Is it easy to go from tennis to padel?

The start is usually quick, because ball skills and volleys transfer. The harder part is the walls, the positioning and unlearning the habits above, which takes longer than most tennis players expect.

Can I use my tennis grip for padel?

Padel coaching generally teaches the continental grip for almost every shot. Some coaches allow a slightly more eastern forehand grip for drives from the back, but a semi-western tennis forehand grip makes volleys and overheads awkward.

Why do tennis players struggle with the back glass?

Because tennis teaches that a ball that gets past you is lost. In padel, letting it come off the glass gives you more time. The LTA calls it the biggest challenge for tennis players at first.

Should tennis players take padel lessons?

A few early lessons help a lot. The Padel School's advice is to learn padel technique at the start, because it is easier to build good habits than to fix ingrained ones.

Which tennis skills help in padel?

Volleys, reactions at the net, clean striking and the split step all carry over. Power helps too, once you learn when to use it.
