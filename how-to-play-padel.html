How to play padel: a complete beginner's guide

8 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

How to play padel, in one sentence: you and a partner rally against another pair on an enclosed court, serving underarm and letting the ball bounce once on your side before you hit it back, off the walls if you like. That's the whole game in one sentence. This guide takes you from that sentence to your first proper match, using the official rules of the International Padel Federation (FIP).

What is padel, in thirty seconds

Padel is a racket sport that mixes tennis-style scoring with squash-style walls. It was invented in 1969 in Acapulco, Mexico, by Enrique Corcuera, who built a smaller, walled court at his home because he didn't have room for a tennis court, according to the FIP. It spread to Spain and Argentina in the 1970s and has been growing worldwide ever since. Our full explainer, what is padel, covers the history and how big the sport has become.

You need four things to play: a court, four players, a padel racket each, and padel balls. You don't need to be fit or experienced. Most people manage short rallies in their first hour.

The court and the equipment

A padel court is 20 metres long and 10 metres wide, fully enclosed by glass and metal mesh. The net is 88 cm high in the middle and 92 cm at the posts. On each side, a service line runs across the court 6.95 metres from the net, and a centre line splits that area into two service boxes. Those lines only matter on the serve. See padel court dimensions for every measurement.

  • Racket: solid, with no strings, a perforated face and a maximum length of 45.5 cm. It has a wrist cord, and the rules make wearing it compulsory. Our guide to choosing a padel racket helps if you're buying your first one.
  • Ball: it looks like a tennis ball but is slightly smaller, with a little less pressure.
  • Shoes: court shoes with a sole suited to the surface. See our guide to padel shoes.

Padel how to play: the basic rules

Here is how a point works, from first serve to last bounce.

  1. Serve underarm. Stand with at least one foot behind the service line, bounce the ball behind the line, and hit it at or below waist height, with at least one foot on the ground.
  2. Serve diagonally. The ball must land in the service box diagonally opposite. You start each game from the right and switch boxes after every point. Like tennis, you get two attempts.
  3. Let the serve bounce. The receiver must let the serve bounce before returning it. After that, anyone can volley.
  4. One bounce, then anything goes. Once the ball has bounced on your side, it can hit any wall or the mesh and you can still play it, as long as you hit it before it bounces a second time on the floor.
  5. Bounce first on their side. Your shot must land on the opponents' floor before it touches their walls or mesh. If it hits their glass directly, you lose the point.
  6. Your own glass is allowed. You may hit the ball into your own glass walls so it rebounds over the net, as long as it then bounces in the opponents' court. Your own mesh isn't allowed.
Allowed

The ball bounces on your side, comes off the back glass, and you return it before it bounces again.

Point lost

Your shot hits the opponents' wall or mesh before bouncing, you touch the net, or the ball bounces twice on your side.

A few more rules worth knowing on day one. You lose the point if you, your racket or your clothing touches the net or posts while the ball is in play, or if the ball hits you or your partner. A serve that clips the net and lands in the right box is replayed, unless it then touches the mesh before its second bounce. And on the serve only, a ball that bounces in the box and then hits the mesh is a fault, while the glass is fine. Our padel rules guide covers everything else.

Padel for beginners: rules that confuse everyone

Three situations catch out almost every new player:

  • Waiting for the glass. Tennis instinct says a deep ball is gone. In padel it's coming back. Let it bounce, come off the glass and drop, then play it. Practising off the glass is one of the LTA's three tips for first-timers for exactly this reason.
  • The mesh behaves differently. The ball dies or flies at odd angles off the metal fence. It's legal in a rally after the bounce, just less predictable.
  • Volleying the return. You can't volley the serve, but from the return onwards you can volley everything.

Padel how to score

Padel uses tennis scoring. Points go love, 15, 30, 40, game. A set is first to six games with a two-game lead, with a tie-break at 6–6, and a match is usually best of three sets. Pairs change ends after the first game and then after every odd-numbered game.

The one twist is what happens at 40–40. The FIP rulebook offers three options: classic advantage, the golden point (one deciding point), and the Star Point, which the professional tour adopted in 2026. Agree which you're playing before the first serve. Our padel scoring guide explains all three, plus tie-breaks and super tie-breaks.

Who serves when?

One player serves a whole game, then the serve passes to the other pair, then to the first server's partner, then to the remaining player. At the start of each set, the receiving pair decides which of them receives the first serve of every game, and that order stays fixed for the whole set.

Padel doubles or singles?

Padel is a doubles sport. The FIP rules are written for pairs throughout, and nearly every court and club game is two against two. That's part of the appeal: four people share a small court, the ball stays in play, and the social side comes built in.

Singles does exist. Some venues build narrow singles courts, the same 20 metres long but only 6 metres wide, though they're rare: in 2024 The Padel Paper counted only six publicly accessible ones in the UK. You can also play one against one on a half court as practice, which is a common drill. For the details, see our guide to padel singles.

How to play padel well: the beginner's game plan

Knowing the rules gets you through a match. These habits help you win one.

Keep the ball in play

Most beginner points end with an error, not a winner. The LTA's first-session advice is simply to get the ball over the net and into the court, with less power and more control. Hit through the middle, aim deep, and let the other pair make the mistake.

Move as a pair

You and your partner should be level: both at the net, or both at the back. When one of you is up and the other back, the diagonal gap between you is the easiest target on court. See where to stand in doubles.

Want the net

The pair at the net usually wins the point. From the back, a deep lob over the net players is your way forward. At the net, stand about two to three metres back, racket up, so you can volley and still cover the lob.

Prepare early

Racket up, body turned sideways as the ball comes. The Padel School lists late preparation among the most common beginner mistakes, and fixing it improves consistency quickly.

Talk

Call "mine" or "yours" on every ball down the middle, and agree before the match who takes lobs and middle balls.

Your first session, step by step

  1. Warm up for a few minutes: light movement first, then arm and shoulder swings. Before a match the rules require a three-minute warm-up rally.
  2. Rally cross-court in pairs, just keeping the ball going.
  3. Feed each other balls off the back glass.
  4. Practise the serve and the return.
  5. Play games, scoring as you go.

A lesson with a qualified coach shortens the learning curve a lot, especially on the walls. Group beginner sessions are a good way to meet people to play with, too.

Frequently asked questions

Is padel easy to learn?

Yes. The underarm serve, small court and walls mean most beginners can rally within their first session. Learning to play off the glass takes longer, and that's where most of the improvement happens.

Can you hit the ball off the walls in padel?

Yes, after it has bounced on your side. You may also hit it into your own glass so it rebounds over the net, as long as it then bounces in the opponents' court before touching their walls.

How many players do you need for padel?

Four is standard: two pairs. You can practise with two, and some venues have narrow singles courts, but the rules and most courts are designed for doubles.

Is the padel serve overarm?

No. The serve is always underarm: bounce the ball behind the service line and hit it at or below waist height, diagonally into the opposite box.

How long does a game of padel last?

It depends on the format. A best-of-three-sets match with advantage scoring can run well over an hour, which is why many leagues use the golden point or a super tie-break to keep matches shorter.

Do I need my own racket to start?

Not usually. Many clubs rent rackets and sell balls. Buy your own once you know you'll keep playing.
