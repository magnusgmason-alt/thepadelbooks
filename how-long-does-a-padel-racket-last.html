How long does a padel racket last?

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

How long does a padel racket last? Mostly, it depends on how often you play: published rules of thumb range from roughly one to two years for a once-a-week player to just a few months for players on court almost every day. Rackets rarely die suddenly; the core slowly loses its pop long before anything cracks. Below are the answers to the questions players ask most, with the sources and where they disagree.

How long does a padel racket last on average?

There's no measured industry figure, only rules of thumb from retailers and padel sites. They point in the same direction but don't agree on the numbers:

How often you playBouncePadel BrowserJustpadel (average-quality racket)
Once a week18 to 24 months10 to 14 months (1 to 2 times a week)12 to 18 months
2 to 3 times a week12 to 18 months5 to 12 months6 to 10 months (twice a week)
4 to 5 times a week5 to 8 months3 to 5 months3 to 6 months (3 to 4 times a week)

Bounce suggests counting sessions instead, putting most recreational rackets at roughly 80 to 120 sessions. Padel Browser estimates that a mid-range racket's EVA core starts breaking down at around 50 to 70 hours of play. These are the authors' own estimates rather than lab tests, so treat them as a rough guide. Your playing style, the court and how you store the racket can move the number a long way.

Why do padel rackets wear out?

The core. Every impact compresses the rubber or foam inside, and Padel Browser explains that a small part of that compression becomes permanent as the cells collapse. Over time the racket returns less energy to the ball: it feels flatter, the sweet spot seems smaller and you have to swing harder for the same depth. Bounce adds that resting the racket won't restore its feel, because compressed foam stays compressed.

Frames wear in a more visible way, through scrapes against the glass and turf and the occasional clash with a partner's racket.

What are the signs of a dead padel racket?

Look out for these, drawn from Bounce, Padel Browser and Justpadel:

  • Loss of pop. Shots that used to reach the back glass now land short, even with the same swing.
  • A dull or hollow sound on contact.
  • A rattle or click when you gently shake the racket.
  • Soft or dead spots. Press the centre of the face with your thumb. Padel Browser says a healthy racket springs back, while a dead one gives and then feels like pressing a board.
  • More vibration reaching your hand or elbow on clean hits.
  • Cracks. Any crack in the frame, or cracks spreading between the holes on the face.

Padel Browser suggests that two of the non-visible signs together mean the racket is done. Bounce recommends repeating the thumb test every six to eight weeks so you notice the change.

Can a racket look fine and still be dead?

Yes. Both Bounce and Padel Browser stress that the core can lose its feel well before any damage shows. A racket that looks new on the outside can still play flat. Trust how the ball comes off the face, not how the paint looks.

When should you replace a padel racket?

Replace it when:

  • the frame is cracked, however small the crack;
  • it rattles, or has obvious soft spots;
  • you're losing depth and feel and a fresh overgrip doesn't help;
  • it's starting to bother your arm when it didn't before.

If your arm hurts, a worn racket is only one possible cause. Our padel elbow guide covers the others, and pain that persists needs a professional.

Also replace it when your game has outgrown it. A soft, round first racket that served you well for a year may simply be the wrong tool once you're finishing points at the net. That's a different decision, covered in our guide to choosing a padel racket.

Do expensive padel rackets last longer?

Sources disagree. Padel Browser says yes, generally: a premium carbon face over a dense EVA core outlasts a fibreglass and soft-foam beginner racket by a wide margin, because dense EVA resists permanent compression better. Justpadel's rules of thumb also give high-quality rackets a longer life than average ones, and it rates carbon as more durable than fibreglass.

Bounce disagrees, calling price a weak predictor of durability. It points out that materials, build quality, foam density and how you play matter more, and that a mid-range racket stored well can outlast a more expensive one left in a hot car.

Both views can be true. Dense cores and carbon do last longer, but they're also firmer and less forgiving. Buying a stiff, expensive racket only for durability doesn't make sense if it doesn't suit your game. See carbon vs fiberglass padel rackets for that trade-off.

What shortens a racket's life?

  • Heat. Keepadel calls leaving a racket in a hot car the most common and most damaging mistake, and says heat damage is gradual and irreversible.
  • Cold storage. Padel Browser warns an unheated garage in winter can make cores brittle.
  • Playing style. Padel Browser says wall players and heavy smashers wear frames fastest. Justpadel notes powerful smashes add stress.
  • Impacts. Hitting the racket on the ground or fence can crack the frame or core, PadelPROShop warns.
  • Outdoor courts. UV and big temperature swings shorten lifespan, according to Padel Browser.

How long do padel balls last compared with a racket?

Much less time. A racket lasts months; a tube of pressurised balls lasts a few sessions before the bounce drops noticeably, and competitive players often open a new tube for every match. Our padel balls guide has the sourced figures and explains how to tell when a ball is flat.

Get more from the racket while it lasts

A tired racket is easier to spot when you have a benchmark. Drills you repeat regularly with a clear target give you one: if the same drill starts landing short, the racket may be fading. Frequently asked questions

How many hours does a padel racket last?

There's no tested figure. Padel Browser estimates a mid-range racket's EVA core starts to break down after roughly 50 to 70 hours of play, and Bounce puts most recreational rackets at about 80 to 120 sessions. Treat both as rough guides.

How often should I change my padel racket?

Rules of thumb range from about 10 to 24 months if you play once or twice a week to a few months if you play four or five times a week. Replace it sooner if the frame cracks or it has clearly lost its pop.

How do I know if my padel racket is dead?

Look for loss of depth and pop, a dull sound, a rattle, soft spots when you press the face, more vibration in your arm, or cracks in the frame or face. A racket can be dead without visible damage.

Do carbon rackets last longer than fiberglass?

Generally, yes, and dense EVA cores resist compression better than soft foam. But the core is what usually wears out first, and storage and playing style matter as much as materials.

Can I still play with a cracked padel racket?

A cosmetic paint chip is fine. A crack in the frame is structural and likely to spread, so replace the racket or check whether it's covered by warranty.
