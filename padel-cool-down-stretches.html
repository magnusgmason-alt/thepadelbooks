Padel stretches: a 9-minute cool-down for after you play

5 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel stretches belong after you play, not before: hold each one for 20 to 30 seconds once your muscles are warm, without bouncing or pain, and cover the areas padel works hardest, from calves and hip flexors to shoulders and forearms. Below is a 9-minute cool-down you can do at the side of the court, plus an honest look at what the research says stretching does, and doesn't do, for injuries and soreness.

Do you need to stretch after padel?

Stretching after padel is worth doing for comfort and range of motion, but it isn't the injury shield many people think it is. Three pieces of research are worth knowing:

  • It doesn't do much for soreness. A Cochrane review (Herbert and colleagues, 2011) concluded that stretching before, after, or before and after exercise does not produce clinically important reductions in delayed-onset muscle soreness. Stretching after exercise cut soreness by about one point on a 100-point scale the next day.
  • It doesn't appear to prevent injuries. A 2014 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine (Lauersen and colleagues) found no injury-prevention effect from stretching programmes, while strength training and balance training did reduce injuries.
  • A cool-down isn't magic either. A 2018 review in Sports Medicine (Van Hooren and Peake) found an active cool-down generally doesn't substantially reduce muscle soreness or prevent injuries, although many athletes feel it helps.

So why bother? Stretching does improve range of motion over time, which helps you reach low balls and turn for overheads. Many players find it relaxing. And five quiet minutes after a match is the perfect moment to notice a calf that's tighter than usual or a shoulder that's grumbling, before it becomes an injury. Just don't let it replace the strength work in our padel exercises guide.

Static or dynamic stretching?

Use both, at different times. Before you play, keep it mostly dynamic: leg swings, lunges with rotation, arm circles and shadow swings, as in our padel warm-up. A 2016 review (Behm and colleagues) found static stretching tested straight afterwards caused a small-to-moderate drop in performance on average, mostly with holds of 60 seconds or more. After you play, your muscles are warm and you're not about to sprint, which makes it the right time for static holds.

Padel New Zealand's cool-down advice is a short, gradual slowdown, such as walking around the court for about five minutes, followed by 5 to 10 minutes of static stretching, holding each stretch for 20 to 30 seconds without bouncing or pain.

The 9-minute padel cool-down

Do it at the side of the court or at home within an hour of playing. Times include switching sides.

7–8Thoracic open book5 slow reps each sideLying on your side, knees bent, rotate the top arm and chest open

Short on time? Keep the calves, hip flexors and forearms: they're the areas padel loads most and the ones most players skip. If you can't stretch at the club, do the routine at home later that evening; your muscles will be cooler, so ease in gently and hold for the full 30 seconds.

What these padel stretches do on court

Calves and soleus

Every split step, sprint and stop goes through your calves and Achilles tendon, which together are among padel's most commonly injured areas. Stretching keeps the ankle moving freely; strength work, as in our calf strain guide, is what protects them.

Hip flexors and glutes

Hours in a low ready position and lunging for balls leave the front of the hip tight, which can pull on the lower back when you arch for a smash. The glute stretch helps the hips rotate, so the spine doesn't have to.

Hamstrings

Tight hamstrings make it harder to get low with a long back. Hinge from the hips rather than rounding your back to reach your toes.

Upper back, shoulders and forearms

The open book restores rotation through the upper back, which takes load off the shoulder on overheads. The chest stretch counters the forward, racket-up posture of net play, and the forearm stretches ease the muscles that grip the racket all session.

Stretching habits to drop

  • Bouncing. Ease into the stretch and hold it still.
  • Stretching into pain. You should feel a pull, not a sharp or burning pain.
  • Long static holds before you play. Keep those for afterwards.
  • Stretching a fresh injury. A suddenly painful calf or hamstring needs first aid and rest, not a stretch.
  • Holding your breath. Breathe slowly and let the muscle relax a little further on each breath out.
  • Treating stretching as injury prevention. The evidence favours strength and balance work for that.

Making a habit of the cool-down is easier if your sessions have structure. If a stretch reveals a problem

This routine is for healthy players and isn't medical advice. If a stretch reproduces a sharp pain, a muscle feels as if it has tightened suddenly, or pain lasts more than a couple of weeks, stop stretching that area and see a physiotherapist or doctor. Get urgent help for a sudden pop or kicked feeling in the calf or Achilles. More on common problems in padel injuries.

Frequently asked questions

Should I stretch before or after padel?

Mostly dynamic movement before you play and static stretches afterwards. Long static holds straight before a fast sport can slightly reduce performance.

How long should I hold padel stretches?

About 20 to 30 seconds per stretch, without bouncing or pain, which is what Padel New Zealand recommends for the cool-down.

Does stretching after padel prevent soreness?

Not by much. A Cochrane review found stretching after exercise reduced next-day soreness by only about one point on a 100-point scale.

Does stretching prevent padel injuries?

The evidence says no: a large meta-analysis found no injury-prevention effect from stretching, while strength and balance training did reduce injuries.

What muscles should I stretch after padel?

Calves, hip flexors, hamstrings, glutes, the upper back, the chest and front of the shoulder, and the forearms.
