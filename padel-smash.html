The padel smash: when to go for it (and how)

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

The smash is padel's most spectacular shot, and its most overused. Because of the walls, a hard smash that isn't perfect often comes straight back. The trick is knowing which ball to smash, how, and when to play a bandeja instead.

Smash, bandeja or víbora?

Padel has three overheads, and choosing the right one is half the battle:

BallBest overhead
High, short lob, you're close to the net and balancedSmash
Medium lob, you're near the service lineVíbora or a controlled smash
Deep lob, you're moving backwardsBandeja, or let it bounce

A common coaching rule: if you have to force the smash, it's the wrong shot. Smash the easy balls; play the bandeja on the rest.

Why smashing is harder in padel

In tennis, a hard smash that lands in the court usually wins. In padel, the ball bounces, hits the back glass and comes out again, often at a comfortable height. The harder and flatter you hit towards the back, the more the glass gives back. So padel smashes either have to leave the court, or be placed so the rebound is awkward.

Flat smash technique

  1. Turn side-on early and move under the ball with short steps.
  2. Point at the ball with your free hand to track it and keep your balance.
  3. Load the racket behind your head, elbow up.
  4. Contact at full extension, slightly in front of your hitting shoulder.
  5. Turn the forearm through the ball (pronation) rather than chopping down.
  6. Recover forward. If the ball comes back, you want to be at the net.

Most players use a continental grip, the same as for the volley and bandeja.

The por tres (x3)

"Por tres" means "by three" in Spanish. It's a smash that bounces in the opponents' court, rebounds off the back wall and then goes out of the court over the side, named after the 3 m height of the side enclosure. Players usually hit it from close to the net with plenty of height and spin, so the ball bounces before the service line and kicks off the back wall near the corner and out over the side.

The por cuatro (x4)

"Por cuatro" means "by four." It's a flat, powerful smash hit from very close to the net, aimed down into the court so the ball bounces high and goes out over the back of the court, which is 4 m high (3 m of wall plus 1 m of mesh). It needs a high, short ball and a lot of power.

What the rules say about balls leaving the court

According to the FIP rules:

  • If a ball bounces correctly in the opponents' court and then leaves the court, the opponents lose the point unless they can legally play it.
  • Where the court and its safety area allow out-of-court play, players may run out through the door and return a ball that went over the side wall or through a door, as long as it hasn't bounced a second time or touched anything outside the court. That's how professionals sometimes rescue a por tres.
  • A ball that goes over the back wall after bouncing correctly can't be played. The point is lost, even where out-of-court play is allowed. That's why a good por cuatro can't be returned.

At many clubs, leaving the court during a point isn't authorised. Then any ball that bounces and leaves the court, through a door or over a wall, wins you the point. Check the rules where you play. More in our padel rules guide.

Where else to aim your smash

  • The side glass, so the ball comes off at an awkward angle.
  • Between the opponents, especially if they're slow to communicate.
  • Not at the back glass with a flat smash from the service line. It usually comes back at a comfortable height.

Common mistakes

  1. Smashing from too deep. From the back third, the ball can't leave the court and comes back off the glass.
  2. Smashing balls you can't reach at full stretch. If the ball has dropped to around head height, it's a bandeja or víbora ball.
  3. Watching your own shot. Recover immediately; a padel smash often comes back.
  4. Going for x3 and x4 too early. Start with smashes you can place reliably, then add height and power.
  5. Losing the net. A failed smash that leaves you stuck in mid-court is worse than a safe bandeja.

How to practise the smash

  1. Shadow swings. Practise the side-on turn, pointing hand and full extension without a ball.
  2. Fed short lobs. A partner feeds easy, short lobs. Smash to the side glass, then recover to the net.
  3. Choose the shot. The feeder mixes short and deep lobs. Smash the short ones, bandeja the deep ones.
  4. Por tres practice. Once your smash is consistent, try sending a few short, high balls out over the side wall.

The smash and your partner

When you go for a smash, your partner's job is to stay alert. If the smash comes back, they need to cover the volley or the next lob. Many pairs agree that the player who smashes moves straight back towards the net while the partner holds position. If your smash fails and you're stuck in mid-court, your partner should be ready to cover the lob over your head.

Defending against the smash

If you're on the receiving end, step back and give the ball room. Most smashes that stay in the court come off the back glass. Turn, let the ball come out, and reply with a lob or a low ball. Our guide to the back glass shows how.

Smashing in the wind and sun

Outdoors, conditions change the smash. In the wind, the ball can drift late, so take smaller adjustment steps and keep your pointing hand on the ball for longer. Against a low sun, a bandeja is often the safer choice: you can play it with a shorter look at the ball. Many players simply avoid big smashes when they can't see the ball clearly, and that's sensible padel.
