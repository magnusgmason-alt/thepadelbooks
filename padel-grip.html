Padel grip: how to hold a padel racket

8 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

The standard padel grip is the continental grip, often called the hammer grip: you hold the racket as if you were about to knock a nail in with its edge, and you use that one grip for almost every shot. Get it right and volleys, bandejas and wall shots all become easier. Get it wrong and you will fight your racket all session. Here is how to hold a padel racket, how tightly, and how to set up the handle so it fits your hand.

Why the continental padel grip is the default

Padel is played close to the net, at speed, with very little time between shots. A grip change costs time you don't have. The continental grip solves that problem: it gives you a fairly neutral racket face that works on the forehand, on the backhand and overhead, so you can go from a forehand volley to a backhand volley without moving your fingers.

That is why coaching guides from retailers and academies alike describe it as the base grip for nearly everything in padel: volleys, low volleys, lobs, balls off the glass, slices, the serve and the overheads. The face sits slightly open, which suits padel's controlled, sliced style of play far better than the closed face of a modern tennis forehand grip.

How to hold a padel racket, step by step

Here is a simple way to find the grip every time. Instructions are for a right-hander; left-handers mirror them.

  1. Hold the racket by the throat with your free hand, with the edge of the frame pointing at the floor and the face pointing sideways, as if it were a small axe.
  2. Shake hands with the handle. Put your hitting hand on the handle as if you were about to hammer a nail in with the edge of the frame.
  3. Check the V. The V shape between your thumb and index finger should sit on the top edge of the handle, or just to the left of it.
  4. Set the heel of your hand level with the end of the handle, so you're not choking up halfway up the grip for normal shots.
  5. Spread the index finger slightly, a little like a trigger finger. It gives you more feel for the face.
  6. Leave a small gap between your fingertips and your palm. If your fingers dig into your palm, the handle is too thin or you're squeezing.

Now hold the racket out in front of you. The face should be roughly upright with a slight tilt backwards. That small tilt is what puts gentle slice on volleys and keeps the ball low.

Padel grip types: continental, eastern and others

You will hear about several grips. In padel they are not equal partners: the continental grip does most of the work, and the others are occasional tools.

GripHow to find itUsed for
Continental (hammer)V on the top edge of the handle, as if holding a hammerAlmost every shot: volleys, serve, bandeja, víbora, smash, lobs, wall shots
Eastern forehandTurn the hand slightly towards the forehand side from continental, palm more behind the handleSome coaches allow it for a flatter forehand from the back when you have time
Eastern backhandTurn the hand slightly the other way, knuckles more on topOccasionally for a firm one-handed backhand drive; many players never use it
Semi-western (tennis forehand)Palm well under the handleNot recommended in padel: it closes the face and makes volleys and overheads awkward

Sources don't completely agree on how much room the eastern grips deserve. One coaching guide suggests sticking with continental for your first six months before experimenting at all. Others mention small adjustments for specific shots once your technique is settled. The safe rule for most club players: learn everything with continental, and only adjust if a coach sees a clear reason.

Padel grip technique: how tight should you hold it?

Grip pressure matters as much as hand position. Squeezing the handle locks the wrist, kills your feel for the ball and tires the forearm, which is one of the habits linked to padel elbow.

  • Relaxed while you wait. One coaching guide puts it at about 5 out of 10 during the rally: firm enough that the racket doesn't twist, loose enough to move the wrist.
  • Firmer at contact. Around 7 out of 10 as you hit, then relax again straight away.
  • Never white knuckles. If your knuckles go pale, you're gripping far too hard.

A useful image from one guide: hold the handle like a small animal you don't want to crush and don't want to let escape.

Padel grip size: is there one?

Not really, at least not in the way tennis players expect. Tennis rackets are sold in numbered grip sizes. Most padel rackets come with one standard handle, and you adjust the thickness yourself with overgrips. The FIP rulebook sets the limits: the handle can be no more than 20 cm long and no more than 50 mm wide and 50 mm thick (FIP equipment rules).

How to tell whether the thickness is right for you:

  • Too thin: the racket turns in your hand on off-centre hits, and you end up squeezing to stop it.
  • Too thick: the racket feels clumsy, you lose manoeuvrability at the net and the wrist can start to ache.
  • About right: a small gap between fingertips and palm, and you can hold the racket securely with a relaxed hand.

For more on choosing the racket itself, see how to choose a padel racket.

Padel grip tape: overgrips and base grips

Every padel racket has a base grip, the original covering on the handle. An overgrip is a thin tape you wrap over it. It's cheap, quick to change and does three jobs: it absorbs sweat, improves your hold and protects the base grip from wearing out.

  • Thickness: standard overgrips are roughly 0.4 to 0.6 mm thick. Many players add two to build up a slim handle.
  • Tacky or dry: tacky overgrips feel slightly sticky and suit dry hands. Dry, absorbent or perforated ones suit players who sweat a lot.
  • How often to change: one retailer guide suggests every four to six sessions for club players, or sooner if it feels slippery or hard.
  • Base grip: if the handle still feels wrong with overgrips, the base grip itself can be replaced.
Don't forget the cord

The wrist cord is part of the racket specification. It must be non-elastic, no longer than 35 cm and worn on the wrist, and it is mandatory. Drop the racket or break the cord during a point and your pair loses it (FIP Rule 13r). See the racket strap rule.

Common padel grip mistakes

  1. Keeping a tennis forehand grip. The closed face makes volleys and overheads awkward and encourages topspin, which padel rewards far less than placement.
  2. Changing grip mid-rally. At the net there's no time. One grip for everything removes the fumble.
  3. Holding it in the palm like a frying pan. With the handle pressed flat into the palm, the wrist locks and the face points the wrong way.
  4. Squeezing all the time. Tension belongs at contact, not between shots.
  5. A grip that gives away the shot. The Padel School points out that a grip which changes for each shot tells your opponents what's coming. A neutral grip keeps the lob, the chiquita and the flat ball all possible from the same set-up.

Practising the grip

Grip habits form fast, so it's worth a few minutes of deliberate practice. Bounce the ball on the racket face, then on the other side of the face, without changing your hand. Then volley gently against a wall, alternating forehand and backhand. If you notice your hand creeping round, reset with the hammer image. Our volley guide covers the next step.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best grip for padel?

The continental grip, also called the hammer grip. It works for forehands, backhands, volleys, the serve and overheads without changing hands, which is why coaches teach it first.

How do you hold a padel racket for beginners?

Shake hands with the handle as if holding a hammer, with the V between thumb and index finger on the top edge. Keep a relaxed hand and a small gap between fingers and palm.

Do padel rackets come in grip sizes?

Generally no. Most come with one standard handle, and you adjust the thickness with one or two overgrips. The FIP limits the handle to 20 cm long and 50 mm wide and thick.

How often should I change my padel overgrip?

When it starts to feel slippery or hard. One retailer guide suggests every four to six sessions for club players, more often if you play a lot or sweat heavily.

Can I use a tennis grip in padel?

You can, but it causes problems. A semi-western tennis grip closes the face, which makes volleys, the bandeja and wall shots harder. Most players improve faster once they switch to continental.
