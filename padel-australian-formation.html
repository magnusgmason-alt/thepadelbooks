The padel Australian formation, step by step

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

The padel Australian formation is a serving set-up where the server's partner stands at the net on the same half of the court as the server, instead of diagonally opposite the receiver. It's completely legal, because the FIP rules let the server's partner stand anywhere on their own side. Pros use it mainly so each player can stay on their favourite side; club players can also use it to take away a receiver's best return. Here is how to set it up in six steps.

What the padel Australian formation is

In the normal serving formation, the server serves from one half of the court and their partner stands at the net on the other half, diagonally opposite the receiver (our guide to the serve and return covers the basics). After the serve, the server follows the ball in and plays the half they served from.

In the Australian formation, both players start on the same half. The Padel School describes it like this: the server starts close to the centre line, the partner stands at the net on that same side, and after the serve the server moves quickly across to cover the other half while the partner covers the centre and their own side.

Is the Australian formation legal in padel?

Yes. FIP Rule 3 lets the receiver, the receiver's partner and the server's partner stand anywhere on their own side of the court. Only the server's position is fixed: at least one foot behind the service line, inside the service box, without touching the service line or the imaginary extension of the centre line (Rules 6.1 and 6.3). Our page on where players can stand covers every position rule.

Two rules matter more in this formation than usual:

  • Rule 7d: if the serve hits the server's partner, it's a fault. With your partner standing on your half, make sure your serve has a clear path.
  • Rule 13a: the partner must not touch the net or the opponents' court during the point, however close to the net they crouch.

Why pros use it: keeping your side

Here's the padel-specific reason, which tennis players often miss, and it ties in with choosing a left or right side. You serve alternately from the right and left boxes (Rule 6.5). In the normal formation, that means a left-side player who serves from the right box ends up playing the right half for that point. Spanish site Padel Addict explains that the Australian serve lets each player keep and defend one side for the whole match, which suits players who only play the right or only the left. Its main drawback, they note, is the longer run to the net.

The Padel School agrees: it describes the Australian formation as used at professional level and most useful when one player clearly prefers, or is clearly more effective on, one side. At recreational level, where both players may want to volley from both sides, the normal formation works fine.

The formation has also been studied. A 2024 paper in the European Journal of Human Movement analysed 1,089 points from World Padel Tour men's matches and found serve directions changed with the formation: in the conventional set-up more than two-thirds of serves went towards the side wall, while in the Australian set-up serves to the T became the most common choice in one of the configurations.

How to set it up, step by step

  1. Agree why you're using it. Either to keep your preferred sides all set, or as a surprise to stop the receiver's favourite cross-court return. The set-up is the same; how often you use it differs.
  2. Partner takes position. The server's partner stands at the net on the same half as the server, roughly in their usual volley spot, about two metres from the net. Keep low enough that the serve has a clear line.
  3. Server moves towards the centre. Serve from close to the centre line, inside your box with at least one foot behind the service line and neither foot touching the lines. Starting near the middle shortens your run across.
  4. Serve with a plan. Serve to the T or the body more often than usual: a serve wide to the side wall opens the angle the receiver needs to hit into the empty half. Your partner's position already blocks the cross-court return.
  5. Cross straight after the serve. As soon as the ball leaves your racket, move forward and across to the empty half. The receiver's obvious target is the space you're running into, so don't admire your serve.
  6. Settle into a normal net position. Within a shot or two you and your partner should be side by side at the net, each on your preferred half, as in any good doubles position.

Signals for a surprise formation

If you only use the formation sometimes, agree a signal so the server knows where the partner will stand and move. Tennis doubles players do this all the time: in the tennis I-formation, the net player gives hand signals behind their back to tell the server where to serve and which way they'll move. Borrow the idea. Keep it to two signals, such as an open hand for "stay" and a fist for "Australian", and use the same signals every match.

Australian formation vs the I-formation

The I-formation is a tennis term. In tennis the net player crouches on the centre service line, in a line with the server (hence the "I"), and moves left or right after the serve, often after a hand signal. Our positions page describes a padel version where the server's partner crouches near the middle of the net; under Rule 3 that's just as legal, provided the partner stays on their own side and off the net.

A note on names: we found the Australian formation discussed widely by padel coaches, but "I-formation" appears far less often in padel coaching material, and "tandem" is used in tennis rather than as a standard padel term. If someone at your club means something different by these names, agree what you mean before the match.

Mistakes that make it backfire

  • Serving wide. A wide serve hands the receiver an angle into the half the server hasn't reached yet.
  • A slow server. If you can't get across quickly, the receiver will hit into the gap.
  • Clipping your partner. A serve that hits your partner is a fault (Rule 7d).
  • Overusing the surprise. As a surprise tactic it works best occasionally; opponents adapt. As a "keep our sides" set-up, use it every point and it becomes your normal.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Australian formation allowed in padel?

Yes. FIP Rule 3 lets the server's partner stand anywhere on their own side. Only the server's position is fixed, and a serve that hits the partner is a fault (Rule 7d).

Why is it called the Australian formation?

The name comes from tennis doubles, where the same set-up has long been called the Australian formation. The FIP rulebook doesn't use the name; it's a coaching term.

Why do padel pros use the Australian formation?

Mainly so each player can keep their preferred side for every point, whichever box they serve from. It costs the server a longer run but keeps the pair's best shots where they want them.

What is the difference between the Australian and I formation?

In the Australian formation the partner stands at the net on the server's half. In the I-formation, a tennis term, the partner crouches in the middle of the net and moves to one side after the serve, usually after a hand signal.

Can we switch between formations during a set?

Yes. Nothing in the FIP rules stops you changing your serving formation from point to point. What stays fixed for the set is the serving order and the receiving order (Rules 6.8, 8.2 and 8.3).
