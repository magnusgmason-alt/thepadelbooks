Padel tactics for intermediate players

7 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

Padel tactics for intermediate players come down to one idea: win the transitions. You already know to move as a pair and lob to get forward. The next step is knowing when to go, when to wait, how to keep the net once you have it, and how to build a point instead of forcing it.

Padel tactics for intermediate players: think in three zones

A useful padel strategy for intermediate players is to read the court as three zones. Babolat's coaching model describes them like this:

ZoneWhereYour jobTypical shots
BackRoughly the last two metres in front of the back glassStay in the rally, buy timeDeep lob, high ball to the centre, slow cross-court ball
MiddleBetween the back zone and the service line areaDecide: build or attackBall at the feet, cross-court drive on a waist-high ball, chiquita
NetFrom the net to about three metres backAttack and keep the positionVolleys, bandeja, víbora, smash

The zones move with the ball. A high, sitting ball in the middle zone is an attacking chance; a low ball at your feet near the net calls for caution. The question to ask before every shot is: what is this ball, from this position, allowed to do?

Taking the net: go on the right ball

Intermediate players rarely fail to go forward. More often they go on the wrong ball. Coaching notes from Ely Padel Club sum it up: move to the net after a good lob or a serve, not after a hopeful one. If your lob is short, the opponents are still at the net and you'll be running into a smash.

Good triggers to move up together:

  • Your lob gets past the net players and they turn to chase it.
  • Your chiquita forces a soft, upward volley.
  • The opponents are pinned against their back glass.
  • You've just served and your partner is already at the net.

When you go, go all the way. Stopping halfway leaves you in no-man's-land, which the Ely coaches call the space where players often get trapped.

Keeping the net: the bandeja habit

Once you're at the net, the opponents will lob you. Your answer decides the point. If the lob doesn't push you deep, play a controlled bandeja, recover forward and keep the position. The Padel School describes exactly this: one player steps back for the overhead and gets straight back to the net so the pair stays together.

Two habits make this work:

  1. Expect to hit several. The Ely coaching notes that rallies may need three, four or even five bandejas before you can finish. That's normal, not failure.
  2. Don't admire the shot. Watching your bandeja instead of moving back to the net is one of the most common intermediate errors.

If the lob is genuinely good, accept it. Drop back together, let it come off the glass, and lob back. Fighting a deep lob with a weak overhead usually costs the net anyway.

Building a point instead of forcing it

At intermediate level you have enough shots to try anything, and that's the danger. The Padel School's advice is to hold back on attacking shots and play a set-up ball first: when you sense the point is there, hit one more good ball, then finish on the next. Pros rely heavily on bandejas and lobs and pick the right ball to end the point.

A simple pattern for the net:

  1. Deep volley to keep them back.
  2. Volley at the feet or down the middle to force them to lift the ball.
  3. Finish the high, weak ball that comes back, to an angle or the middle.

Padel tips for intermediate players: where to aim

  • The middle. Babolat's coaching calls it the "divorce" zone for a reason. Attacks through the middle cause hesitation and also reduce the angles the opponents can use against you.
  • The feet. A ball that lands at the feet of a net player forces them to hit up.
  • The weaker player. HEAD recommends focusing on one opponent. It puts pressure on the target, and the partner left out of the rally can lose rhythm.
  • The side glass, not the mesh. Deep balls into the side glass give an awkward but predictable rebound; mesh rebounds are unpredictable for you as well as them.

Change pace and height

HEAD's tactical advice includes varying pace and placement so opponents can't settle. A sudden slow ball breaks rhythm; a quicker one rushes them. Intermediate players often have one speed. Learn three: a soft ball to the feet, a medium ball deep, and a fast ball only when you're balanced and the target is open.

Covering the angles as a pair

Movement sideways matters as much as forward and back. The Padel School gives two examples worth drilling:

  • At the back: if you're pulled into the corner, your partner shifts towards the centre to cover the easy volley down the middle.
  • At the net: if you volley down the line, your partner moves across to cover the middle. The opponents are left with a difficult cross-court ball into a small space, or a lob.

A good intermediate checklist

Did we go forward on a good ball? Did we go all the way? When lobbed, did we keep the net if we could? Did we set up before finishing? Were we level with each other?

Padel tactics advanced: a short look ahead

Once these basics are automatic, advanced tactics add layers on top rather than replacing them:

  • Serve formations. In the Australian formation, the server's partner stands at the net on the same half as the server, to block the receiver's favourite cross-court return. It's legal because partners may stand anywhere on their own side (FIP Rule 3). See our guide to player positions.
  • Attacking overheads. The víbora and the smash out of the court, "por tres", become real weapons instead of risks. Our smash guide explains when.
  • Constant micro-adjustments. The Padel School notes that advanced and professional players adjust position several times per point to close gaps.
  • Match plans. Babolat suggests keeping aggressive opponents pinned at the back with lobs and deep balls, and moving patiently forward against defensive pairs before accelerating.

Frequently asked questions

What separates intermediate from beginner padel tactics?

Beginners learn to move together and lob to get forward. Intermediate players learn when to go forward, how to keep the net with bandejas, and how to build points instead of forcing winners.

How do I stop getting stuck in no-man's-land?

Commit. After a good lob or serve, move all the way to the net. After a good lob against you, drop all the way back. Don't stop around the service line.

Should intermediate players smash or bandeja?

Mostly bandeja. Smash the easy, short balls when you're balanced and close to the net, and play a controlled overhead on everything else so you keep the net.

Is it fair to target one opponent?

Yes. Playing to the weaker player or the weaker side is standard tactics at every level and is recommended by coaching sources such as HEAD.

What is the Australian formation?

A serving formation where the server's partner stands at the net on the same side as the server, so the receiver has to change their usual return. It's legal under FIP Rule 3.
