The padel half volley: how to play the ball at your feet

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

A padel half volley is a shot you play a split second after the ball bounces, usually at your feet as you move from the back of the court to the net. Spanish players call it the bote pronto. You can't avoid it completely, but you can face it less often, and when it comes, a short swing, a low body and a sensible target turn it from a point-loser into a shot that keeps you in the rally.

What is a half volley in padel?

A half volley is hit just after the bounce, before the ball has had time to rise. Padel.FYI describes it as a defensive shot to defend balls bouncing right in front of a player's feet. A volley is hit before the bounce; a half volley is hit immediately after it. In our padel terms glossary it appears as bote pronto.

It is more common in padel than in tennis, Padel.FYI notes, because playing to the opponents' feet is so common. Padel World Press calls it a difficult shot to be used only when necessary, and says you can play it flat or with a little topspin depending on the ball and your position.

When the padel half volley happens

  • On the way to the net. You have played a lob or a chiquita and are moving forward. The opponents dip the ball at your feet before you arrive. This is the classic case, and Padel.FYI calls it a key risk of advancing to the net.
  • In defence. A volley or overhead is aimed at your feet and you have no time to let it reach the glass.
  • When you choose it by mistake. Many players, especially those coming from tennis, half-volley balls behind the service line that could have gone to the back glass. Padel World Press warns that more options open up if you let the ball bounce off the wall.

Fewer half volleys: stop, split, then move

The best half volley is the one you don't have to play. Two habits cut the number dramatically.

Don't stop in the middle. The Padel School warns against getting stuck in no man's land, the stretch between the back of the court and the net position where every low ball lands at your feet. Come forward behind a slow ball, such as a high lob or a chiquita, so you have time to cross it.

Split step as they hit. The Padel School's advice is to stop advancing just as the opponent is about to contact the ball and make a split step, then continue. If you are still running at contact, you can't react to a ball at your feet. Our padel split step guide explains the timing.

Half volley technique

The Padel School's breakdown of the backhand half volley applies on both sides, and Padel World Press says much the same.

  • Split step as the ball approaches. Land balanced, ready to go either way.
  • Turn the shoulders early and keep the backswing tiny. Padel World Press says the racket should be almost at the contact point already.
  • Get down to the ball. Widen your stance and bend at the knees and hips. Keep your chest low and steady through the shot rather than standing up as you hit.
  • Contact in front. The Padel School puts contact in the middle of the racket, in line with your front hip. If the ball gets past you, the shot usually fails or sits up.
  • Compact forward swing. Short and smooth, with the racket face slightly open so the ball clears the net. The follow-through depends on the target: low and deep, or up for a lob.
  • Use the continental grip. The same grip you use for the volley, because there's no time to change.
The feeling to look for

Think of it as a block with legs. The racket barely moves; your knees and the ball's own pace do the work.

Where to send a half volley

What not to do: smash a flat half volley at the net player. From below net height it has to go upwards, so a hard one either goes long or arrives at a perfect volleying height.

Forehand or backhand half volley?

Both use the same short block. Many club players find the backhand side easier because the arm stays in front of the body, just as with the backhand volley. On the forehand side, the usual fault is letting the racket head drop and swinging up from the wrist, which pops the ball up. Keep the wrist firm and bend the knees instead.

Half volley drills

1. Feet feed

A starts behind the service line and walks forward. As F, standing at the net on the other side, feeds a ball that bounces at A's feet, A split-steps and plays a half volley deep. Ten balls per side. Pass mark: 6 of 10 landing in the last three metres.

2. Bounce-only mini rally

Two players at the service lines play cross-court. Every ball must bounce once before it is hit, and nobody may move back. You'll play dozens of half volleys in ten minutes. Padel World Press suggests a similar one-on-one game where the ball must bounce.

3. Attack and defend

Two against two. The net pair try to volley at the feet of the pair coming in; the incoming pair score double for any point they win after reaching the net. Swap every five points.

Five half-volley habits to drop

  • Standing up as you hit. The ball goes into the net or flies. Stay down.
  • A tennis backswing. No time for it; the racket arrives late.
  • Half-volleying balls that could reach the glass. Let them come off the back wall.
  • Running through the shot. No split step means no balance.
  • Going for a winner. A half volley is a holding shot. Depth, not pace.

Frequently asked questions

What is a bote pronto in padel?

Bote pronto is the Spanish name for the half volley: a shot played immediately after the ball bounces, usually at your feet.

Is a half volley the same as a volley?

No. A volley is played before the ball bounces. A half volley is played just after the bounce, which is why it's usually lower and harder to control.

Should I half volley or let the ball hit the back glass?

If the ball will reach the glass and you have time to get back, let it. The glass gives you more time and more options. Half-volley only when the ball lands at your feet in mid-court and there is no alternative.

Why do my half volleys go into the net?

Usually because you stand up as you hit or close the racket face. Bend your knees, keep your chest low and the face slightly open, and keep the swing short.

How do I stop getting caught in no man's land?

Come forward behind slow balls such as high lobs and chiquitas, split step as the opponent hits, and keep moving to the net rather than stopping in the middle of the court.
