Padel shoe soles: herringbone vs omni vs clay

6 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 8 Nov 2026

Padel shoe soles come in three main patterns: herringbone (often called a clay sole), omni (small dense studs) and hybrid, a mix of the two. Which one grips best depends on the court you play on, and especially on how much sand is in the turf. This comparison sets out what each sole does, where the advice agrees and where it doesn't, and how to tell when your soles are finished.

Padel shoe soles at a glance

SolePatternStrengthsWeak spots
Herringbone (clay)Zigzag grooves across the soleGrip with some controlled sliding; deep grooves clear sand; durable on sandy turfCan feel too grippy on hard or low-sand courts; grip drops sharply once the pattern wears
OmniSmall, dense rubber dots or pimplesDirect, predictable grip from the first step; suits carpets with light sand and many indoor courtsLess grip on very sandy courts in some retailers' testing; wears faster on hard or cement surfaces
HybridHerringbone zones (often outside) with omni zones (often inside)Versatile if you play on different courtsDoesn't excel at maximum grip or maximum slide

PadelShop and PadelPROShop both describe the soles this way, and PadelShop notes that herringbone is also called a clay sole, because the same zigzag pattern is used on clay-court tennis shoes.

Where the advice disagrees: sand-filled turf

Most padel courts are artificial grass with sand brushed into the fibres. How much sand there is changes everything, and retailers don't fully agree on which sole handles a sandy court best.

  • Herringbone for sandy courts: PadelShop and PadelPROShop recommend herringbone for sand-covered turf, because the deep grooves clear sand. The Padel School says a deep zigzag herringbone gives useful traction on sandier surfaces.
  • Omni for sand-filled carpet: Tennisnuts describes the omni pattern as designed for sand-filled carpet courts, with dense studs that keep sand from getting trapped, and says both patterns work on all padel courts.

Part of the confusion is the name: Tennisnuts calls the pattern "Omni Court" and ties it to sand-filled carpet, while the padel-specialist shops above steer omni towards courts with less sand. Both views come from retailers, not independent testing. Our padel shoes guide makes the same point: try a pair on the courts you actually play on.

Which sole for artificial grass with sand?

If your club's courts have plenty of loose sand, start with a herringbone or hybrid sole. That's the majority recommendation, and the deeper grooves give you something to bite into when you push off for a lob. If you find yourself skating across the top of the sand, the sole may be worn or too shallow. If your feet feel stuck when you turn, try a hybrid.

Which sole for indoor, hard or cement courts?

Many indoor courts use carpet or turf with little sand. PadelShop's indoor guide suggests flatter or finer patterns, such as omni, for synthetic courts with little sand, and says the choice should depend on the surface rather than the indoor or outdoor label. Some indoor courts still use turf with light sand, so check.

On hard or cement courts, both sole types have drawbacks. PadelShop says herringbone can be too grippy on hard courts and limit movement. PadelPROShop says omni soles wear faster and slide less than herringbone on hard or cement surfaces. If you play on a hard court regularly, expect faster wear whichever sole you choose, and consider a hybrid. For more on how indoor and outdoor courts play, see indoor vs outdoor padel.

Choose herringbone if

you mostly play outdoors on turf with a lot of sand, you like a little controlled slide into wide balls, and you want a sole that lasts on sandy courts.

Choose omni if

your courts are indoor carpet or turf with light sand, you want firm footing from the first step, and you rarely play on very sandy courts.

If you play at several clubs, a hybrid sole is the safe middle.

Can you use tennis shoes for padel?

Yes, with the right sole. The Padel School says clay-court tennis shoes with herringbone soles can work on sandy padel courts, and that some all-court models may suit newer, lower-sand surfaces, but it advises checking traction and stability rather than assuming any tennis shoe will do. Tennisnuts notes that tennis shoes are designed for hard courts, grass or clay, while padel shoes are built for padel surfaces and often have extra toe reinforcement for the dragging and quick stops of padel.

A hard-court tennis shoe with a flat, smooth sole is the one to avoid on sandy turf. Running shoes and casual trainers are a worse choice still: Tennisnuts calls them the worst option for padel, and The Padel School points out that they're built mainly for forward movement.

Slipping on a padel court: causes and fixes

If you slip when…Likely causeWhat to try
You push off sideways on a sandy courtWorn or shallow pattern, or an omni sole on a very sandy courtCheck the forefoot tread; try herringbone or hybrid
You turn on an indoor carpetYour foot is moving inside the shoe, not the sole on the courtCheck fit and lacing; the heel shouldn't lift
You stop near the net or in the cornersSand piled up or worn thin in that areaLook at the court; ask the club if the sand needs brushing
The court is damp, early or late in the dayMoisture on the turf and glass (NOX notes courts get more humid in the cold, and the glass can get wet from late afternoon to early morning)Take shorter adjustment steps and avoid big slides

Many slips come from arriving late and having to brake hard. Short adjustment steps and a split step before your opponent hits keep your weight over your feet. The Padel Drill Book includes footwork drills for exactly this, and The Padel Playbook: Beginner & Intermediate covers court positioning so you're moving less in the first place. When are padel shoe soles worn out?

Look at the forefoot, especially under the ball of the foot and on the inside edge, where padel shoes take most of the dragging. PadelPROShop says a herringbone sole's grip falls off noticeably once the pattern wears down. Our padel shoes guide uses the same test: once the pattern at the front of the foot is worn smooth, the grip is gone.

  • The zigzag or dots are smooth in patches under the ball of your foot.
  • You've started slipping on courts that used to feel fine.
  • The toe is scuffed through, or the upper no longer holds your foot when you push off.
  • The midsole feels flat and your feet ache more than they used to.

How long that takes depends on how often you play and on the surface. Hard and cement courts wear soles faster.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best sole for padel shoes?

For most outdoor courts with sand-filled turf, herringbone or hybrid is the most common recommendation. For indoor carpet or low-sand courts, many retailers suggest omni. Advice varies between sources, so test a pair on the courts you play on most.

Is a clay sole the same as a herringbone sole?

In practice, yes. Clay-court tennis shoes use a zigzag herringbone pattern, and padel retailers often use the two names for the same type of sole.

Are omni soles good for padel?

Yes, especially on indoor carpets and courts with light sand, where they give direct, predictable grip. Some retailers find them less grippy on very sandy courts, and they wear faster on hard or cement surfaces.

Can I play padel in tennis shoes?

Clay-court tennis shoes with a herringbone sole work well on most sandy padel courts. Avoid smooth hard-court soles on sandy turf, and avoid running shoes altogether.

Why do I keep slipping on the padel court?

The usual causes are a worn sole, a sole that doesn't suit the amount of sand on your court, a foot moving inside a loose shoe, or damp turf. Check your tread first, then your fit.
