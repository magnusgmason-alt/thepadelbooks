Padel fitness: the complete guide to a body that keeps up

5 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 15 Nov 2026

Padel fitness isn't about running marathons or lifting huge weights. Padel is hundreds of short bursts: split steps, lunges to the side glass, turns to chase a lob, overheads, and back to the net. A fit padel body handles those bursts all evening and still feels fine the next morning. This guide explains what the game asks of you, the six qualities worth training, and a simple weekly routine of a 10-minute warm-up before you play plus two 25-minute strength sessions, with links to our detailed guides on every body part.

General information, not medical advice

This page explains common patterns and general training principles. It can't diagnose you or replace an assessment. If you have a heart condition, a recent injury or any medical concern, check with a doctor or physiotherapist before starting a new programme.

What padel actually asks of your body

A systematic review of padel match play by Mellado-Arbelo and Baiget (2022) describes padel as intermittent: short rallies of high effort separated by pauses, with mostly moderate heart-rate responses and moderate to fairly high perceived effort. The aerobic system carries you through the match; your legs, trunk and shoulders deliver the short bursts inside each rally. The same review found that the distance players cover and their speed vary with standard, so a club player and a pro do very different amounts of running.

The injury research tells you where that load lands. A 2023 systematic review (Dahmen and colleagues) ranked the elbow and the knee among the most frequently injured areas, with the shoulder, lower back, calf and ankle also common. A narrative review in the Aspetar Sports Medicine Journal adds that poor conditioning and an inadequate warm-up are risk factors, and that some injuries, such as calf strains, are typical of less-conditioned players over 40.

So a padel fitness plan has two jobs: make you better at the bursts, and make the tissues that take the most load (calf, knee, shoulder, elbow, back) able to cope with them.

Before you play: a 10-minute warm-up

Warm-up programmes built around raising the pulse, mobility, strength and landing control have a strong record in other sports. A 2017 systematic review of the FIFA 11+ programme in football found it reduced injuries in most of the studies it pooled. Padel has no equivalent trial yet, but the principle carries over: prepare the tissues for the movements you are about to do. A simple structure:

  1. Raise (2 minutes). Easy jogging, side shuffles and skipping around the court.
  2. Mobilise (2 minutes). Leg swings, walking lunges with a twist, arm circles.
  3. Activate (3 minutes). Calf raises, a few bodyweight squats, band shoulder rotations, a short side plank.
  4. Potentiate (3 minutes). Split steps, quick lateral steps, a few shadow overheads at growing speed, then the normal warm-up rally.

Two strength sessions a week

The WHO's 2020 physical activity guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days a week for adults, alongside regular aerobic activity. For padel players that is also the best-supported injury prevention tool: a 2014 meta-analysis by Lauersen and colleagues found strength training reduced sports injuries to less than a third, a far larger effect than stretching. Two 25-minute sessions are enough to start:

Speed, footwork and conditioning

Padel is won by arriving early and balanced. Short, sharp work trains that best: 5–10 m sprints, side shuffles to a cone and back, and split steps timed to a partner's swing. Keep each effort under ten seconds with full recovery. For conditioning, copy the rally pattern: 10–20 seconds of fast work followed by 20–40 seconds of rest, for 10–15 minutes. Protect the areas that get hurt most

Most padel injuries aren't bad luck. They happen when a calf, knee, shoulder or elbow is asked to do more than it is ready for, usually after a jump in playing time. Our guides cover each area:

  • Knee pain and hip pain
  • Heel pain and plantar fasciitis
  • Calf strain and the Achilles, hamstring strains and groin strains
  • Padel elbow, wrist pain and shoulder pain
  • Lower back pain and neck pain

Padel fitness after 40

Strength and power decline with age, and tendons recover more slowly, which is why the Aspetar review notes calf strains in poorly conditioned men over 40. That's a reason to train, not to stop: two strength sessions, a proper warm-up and more recovery between matches go a long way. See padel over 40 and padel for seniors.

Frequently asked questions

How do I get fit for padel?

Warm up properly before every game, do two short strength sessions a week (legs, trunk, shoulders and forearms), add a little footwork and interval work, and build up your playing time gradually.

Is padel good for fitness?

Yes. Match play is intermittent, with mostly moderate heart-rate responses and short bursts of high effort, so it trains both your aerobic system and your ability to repeat quick movements.

What muscles does padel use?

Mainly the legs and hips for lunges and braking, the calves for push-offs, the trunk for rotation, and the shoulder and forearm for overheads and volleys.

How often should I strength train for padel?

Two sessions a week is a good target, in line with the WHO recommendation of muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days a week.

Can fitness training prevent padel injuries?

It reduces the risk. A 2014 meta-analysis found strength training reduced sports injuries to less than a third, and well-designed warm-ups lower injury rates in other sports.
