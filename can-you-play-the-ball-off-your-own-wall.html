Can you hit the ball against your own wall in padel?

9 min readBy The Padel AcademyUpdated 23 Oct 2026

Yes: in padel you can hit the ball against your own glass wall, and it is a legal return as long as the ball then crosses the net and bounces in your opponents' court (FIP Rule 14b). What you cannot do is send it into your own metal mesh or your own ground, and the ball must not hit your opponents' walls before it bounces on their side. Those three conditions decide almost every own-wall argument at club level.

The short answer

Hitting your own glass is allowed. The ball then has to clear the net and bounce first in the opponents' court. If it touches your own mesh or your own ground after you hit it, or flies straight into the opponents' glass or fence without bouncing, you lose the point.

What the rules say

Several rules work together here, so it helps to take them one at a time.

FIP Rule 14b defines a correct return. The ball is good if it bounces directly in the opponents' court, or if it first strikes a wall of your own court and then bounces in the opponents' court. That second half is the whole reason own-wall play exists. You can play the ball into your back glass or your side glass, let it rebound over the net, and the shot is just as valid as a normal drive.

FIP Rule 13l sets the limit. You lose the point if, after you hit it, the ball touches the metal fence, the ground on your own side, or any object unrelated to the court lying on the ground on your side. So the permission is for glass and walls only. The mesh is treated completely differently, and many courts have mesh panels on the side walls next to the glass, so you need to know which part of your wall you are aiming at.

FIP Rule 13g covers the other end of the shot. If you return the ball, either directly or off your own walls, and without bouncing it strikes the opponents' walls, their metal fence or anything not related to their court, you lose the point. An own-wall shot therefore has two jobs: get over the net, and land on the opponents' ground before it touches anything else on their side.

FIP Rule 12 explains why you are often hitting off your own glass in the first place. Once the opponents' shot has bounced in your court, it may touch any part of the court (inside of the walls, the mesh including its frame, the ground, the net and the posts) and is still in play. You just have to return it before it bounces a second time. So letting the ball come off your back glass and then playing it is standard padel; hitting it back into your own glass on purpose is the extra step that Rule 14b allows.

Allowed

You hit the ball into your own back or side glass, it rebounds over the net and bounces in the opponents' court (Rule 14b). The ball off your own glass clips the net and still lands in their court (Rule 14e).

Not allowed

After your hit, the ball touches your own mesh or bounces on your own ground (Rule 13l). The ball comes off your glass, crosses the net and hits the opponents' glass or fence before bouncing (Rule 13g).

Real match situations

The emergency shot off the back glass

A lob goes over your head, bounces near the back of your court and you are too late to play it normally. You turn and hit the ball hard into your own back glass so it flies back over the net. Players often call this a contrapared. Verdict: legal, provided it lands in the opponents' court first (Rule 14b). If it drops short and bounces on your side, the point is lost (Rule 13l).

Your own side glass, before the ball crosses

You are near the side wall and hit a ball into your own side glass, and it angles back across the net. This is the question many people ask: can a ball go off your own side wall before it crosses? Yes. Rule 14b talks about a wall of your own court without limiting it to the back wall, so side glass counts. Just check it is glass and not mesh: the same shot that touches a mesh panel on your side is a lost point under Rule 13l.

Off your glass and straight into theirs

You play a hard shot into your back glass, it rockets over the net and smacks into the opponents' back glass on the full. Verdict: point lost. Rule 13g explicitly includes returns played off your own walls. The ball has to bounce in the opponents' court before it reaches their walls or fence.

The ball touches your mesh on the way

Your partner plays the ball into your side wall, but it catches the edge of the mesh section above the glass before coming back over. Verdict: point lost under Rule 13l. It does not matter that the ball eventually reached the opponents' court; once your shot touches your own fence, the point is over.

Your partner plays it off the glass

Nothing in the rules limits own-wall shots to one player. Either partner can use the glass. What matters is that only one of you hits the ball: if both partners touch it, Rule 13o applies and the point is lost.

The ball hits you after it comes off your own wall

You hit the ball into your back glass and it rebounds into your own body or your partner's leg. Verdict: point lost. Rule 13j says that after a player hits the ball, it must not touch the player, the partner or anything they wear.

Common misconceptions

  • "Own-wall shots are only for beginners." They are a legal shot for everyone. Advanced players use them mostly as a last resort, but the rule is the same at every level.
  • "You can only use the back wall, not the side wall." Rule 14b refers to a wall of your own court. Side glass is a wall.
  • "The fence is part of the wall, so it is fine too." Not for your own shots. Rule 13l singles out the metal fence of your own side. Glass yes, mesh no.
  • "If it goes over the net, it is good." It still needs to bounce in the opponents' court before touching their glass or mesh (Rule 13g).
  • "Playing the ball after it comes off your back glass is an own-wall shot." Not quite. That is simply playing an opponent's ball after it has bounced and touched part of your court, which Rule 12 allows. An own-wall shot is when your hit goes into your wall.

Is the serve different?

Yes. Rule 14b is about returns during the rally. For the serve, Rule 6.5 requires the ball to go diagonally into the receiver's box and land within its lines, and the rulebook does not describe serving off your own wall. Serve directly into the box and save the glass for the rally. For everything that can go wrong with a serve, see padel service faults.

Social games vs official matches

With an umpire, the umpire decides whether the ball touched glass or mesh. In social games you call it yourselves, and the honest convention is that the player closest to the ball, or the pair who played it, calls their own shot. The tricky calls are almost always about whether an own-wall shot brushed the mesh panel or the top of the glass, so make the call quickly and in good faith. If nobody saw it clearly, many clubs replay the point; that is a convention, not something the FIP rulebook sets out.

Some recreational players have their own house rules, for example banning own-wall shots for juniors. Agree anything like that before the match, because under the FIP rules the shot is legal.

When to use your own wall

Treat the own-wall shot as an escape. It buys you a return when you are beaten by a lob or caught too close to the glass, but it is hard to control and usually gives the opponents an easy ball at the net. A few practical points:

  • Hit up and hard enough. The ball needs height and pace to clear the net from deep in your court. The most common failure is a ball that drops on your own side.
  • Aim at the glass, not the corner seam. A ball that catches the joint between back and side glass can fly anywhere.
  • Know your court. Before the match, look at where the glass ends and the mesh begins on each side wall.
  • Prefer the normal play when you can. If you have time to let the ball come off the back glass and hit a proper return, that is almost always better. Our guide to playing off the back glass covers this, and the lob is often the best reply once you are back in position.

You will also hear the Spanish word bajada on court. It describes attacking an overhead after the ball has come off your back glass. It is a tactic name, not a separate rule; the relevant rules are the same ones above.

Frequently asked questions

Can you hit the ball into your own side wall in padel?

Yes, if the part you hit is glass (a wall) and the ball then bounces in the opponents' court (FIP Rule 14b). If it touches your own mesh, the point is lost (Rule 13l).

Can the ball hit your own wall twice before crossing the net?

Rule 14b speaks of the ball striking a wall of your own court and then bouncing in the opponents' court; the rulebook does not specifically address one wall versus two. The firm limits are that it must not touch your own mesh or ground (Rule 13l) and must bounce on their side before hitting their walls (Rule 13g).

What happens if my own-wall shot hits the net and goes over?

If it then lands in the opponents' court, it is a correct return. Rule 14e says a ball that touches the net or posts and lands in the opponents' court is good.

Is hitting your own wall the same as a double hit?

No. A double hit (Rule 13i) is touching the ball twice with the racket. Hitting the ball once into your own glass is a single hit and is allowed under Rule 14b.

Can I play the ball after it hits my own fence from the opponents' shot?

Yes. If the opponents' ball bounces in your court and then hits your mesh or glass, it is still in play (Rule 12). The mesh restriction in Rule 13l only applies to the shot you hit.
